Unit 6 — Session Overview

Use this tab to orient yourself before each section. It contains the purpose, the tone and the key facilitation questions for the full unit.

◆ Unit Intent

Unit 6 bridges strategy and execution accountability. Many organisations have measurement systems but lack management systems. Participants should leave understanding the distinction — and knowing how to build the management side. The emphasis is on design thinking: how to construct a system that drives behaviour as well as tracking it. This is one of the more technical units in S2R® — keep the energy applied.

◆ Key Facilitation Questions
  • “In your organisation, are you measuring what happened — or managing what will happen next?”
  • “If your team stopped producing reports tomorrow, what decisions would you lose the ability to make?”
  • “Is your current PM system designed to generate compliance — or to build commitment?”
  • “Where in your organisation does measurement transparency break down? Who does not see their numbers?”
  • “When you review performance, are you looking at activity or at cumulative progress toward target?”
◆ Tone & Watch Points

Avoid: turning the FACES or EXECUTION frameworks into a compliance checklist. They are diagnostic and design tools.

Watch for: leaders who equate “we have a scorecard” with “we have a PM system.” Probe: “What happens after you look at the scorecard? What changes? Who decides what?”

The Solid Gold reframe (Rating 3 = Solid Gold) is intentional and often powerful. Bring it to life with an example of a consistent contributor who reliably delivers and is still overlooked.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

Foundations of Performance Management

Establishing the conceptual ground: what performance management actually is, how it differs from measurement, and the functional architecture that makes it work.

Section learning outcomes
  • Distinguish the management of performance from its measurement.
  • Exercise consistent judgement on performance status and the leadership response it requires.
1.1 — Measurement vs Management
The core distinction

Performance management (PM) and performance measurement are distinct disciplines. This distinction is the single most important conceptual anchor in Unit 6 — and the one most organisations get wrong.

Measurement answers: What happened? It is an input — data, results, scores, dashboards.
Management answers: What do we do about it? It is the leadership response that turns data into decisions, actions, and accountability.

Most organisations have built sophisticated measurement systems and mistakenly believe they have performance management systems. A measurement system without a management architecture produces reports. A management system converts those reports into movement.

The Correct Sequence

Step 1
Define Success
▼

Before any system is designed, leadership must be explicit about what success looks like. What are we trying to achieve? By when? At what level of quality or scale? Without this, measurement has no target and management has no compass.

Step 2
Design the Management System
▼

Design the system for managing toward success. What conversations will we have? How often? Who is accountable? What decisions will be made, and by whom? The management architecture must exist before measurement is collected — otherwise data accumulates with no system to act on it.

Step 3
Design the Measurement Infrastructure
▼

Design the measurement tools that will feed the management system. What data do we need? How frequently? In what format? Measurement should serve the management conversations already defined.

Step 4
Manage Using Data
▼

Execute the management system using measurement data as the evidence base for conversations, interventions, recognition, and course correction. This is where most organisations start — collecting data before having a system to use it.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask: “In your organisation, which comes first — the data collection system or the definition of success? How many reports exist that nobody acts on?” Most will recognise that measurement infrastructure was built first and management systems are retrofitted — or entirely absent.

1.2 — The FACES Framework
Five functions of PM

A complete performance management system serves five interconnected functions. These are simultaneous functions that every well-designed PM system must perform.

F
Foster a Results Culture
▼

The PM system must reinforce a culture in which results matter, are visible, and are celebrated or addressed consistently. Culture is shaped by what gets measured, what gets discussed, and what gets rewarded. A PM system that avoids difficult conversations actively undermines results culture — no matter how sophisticated the dashboard.

A
Align Individual Performance
▼

Every individual must see a clear line of sight between their daily work and the organisation’s strategic outcomes. Without this alignment, effort is misdirected. The PM system must decompose strategic outcomes into team and individual targets that are genuinely connected.

C
Create Measurement Transparency
▼

People perform at a higher level when they can see their own performance data and understand how it is calculated. Transparency ensures that the logic of measurement is understood and trusted. Hidden formulas and opaque scoring destroy confidence in the PM system.

E
Embed Shared Accountability
▼

The PM system must embed accountability as a shared expectation. Shared accountability means teams co-own the results — they participate in setting targets, understand the evidence, and are part of the intervention process. Systems that locate accountability solely in the leader create dependency and fragility.

S
Support Continuous Development
▼

The PM system must identify what performance reveals about capability gaps, development needs, and growth trajectories. Performance data is a development signal as well as an accountability score. Leaders who use PM data only for ratings miss its most powerful application: designing targeted development that improves future performance.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask: “What does your PM system reward? Does it reward activity or results? Compliance or commitment?”

Ask participants to rate their current PM system against each FACES dimension (1–5). Typical gap: most organisations score highest on “Create Measurement Transparency” (they have dashboards) and lowest on “Embed Shared Accountability” (accountability lives with the leader alone).

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: their rating of their current PM system from 1 to 5 against each of the five FACES functions, the biggest gap and what closing it would change.

1.3 — The Three Sights of Progress
What gets measured

A well-designed PM system tracks performance across three distinct categories. Each sight provides a different angle on whether the organisation is progressing toward its strategic intent. At the individual level, weightings are assigned according to role and function.

Sight 1
Strategy Category
What it measures
Strategic OKRs directly connected to the organisation’s multi-year strategy. This sight asks: Are we making progress on the things that matter most for where we are going?
Individual weighting
Typically 30–50% at senior levels; lower for frontline roles. Reviewed quarterly with executive visibility.
Sight 2
Operational Category
What it measures
Departmental KPIs tracking the operational engine — efficiency, output, quality, and service delivery. This sight asks: Are our operations performing at the standard required to sustain strategy?
Individual weighting
Typically 30–50% across most role levels; highest for frontline and specialist roles. Reviewed monthly or fortnightly.
Sight 3
Behavioural & Values Alignment
What it measures
How individuals demonstrate the organisation’s values and leadership behaviours — assessed through 360° feedback and peer observation. How results are achieved matters as much as what results are achieved.
Individual weighting
Typically 20–40% across role levels; higher for leadership and customer-facing roles. Assessed bi-annually or annually via structured 360°.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

A common gap: organisations measure Sight 1 and Sight 2 rigorously but treat Sight 3 as a subjective add-on. Surface this: “In your current system, what weight does behavioural alignment carry in the final performance rating? Is it measured formally or informally?”

1.4 — Leader’s Response
Ratings & leadership responses

A rating scale only has value if it is consistently understood and each rating triggers a defined leadership response.

A good performance management system reduces subjectivity in how people’s performance is evaluated. It gives managers a common language for discussing contribution, results, behaviour and development.

That is why many organisations use a numbered rating system. The number is meant to create consistency. It helps managers avoid vague judgements such as “doing well”, “not trying hard enough”, or “needs improvement” without evidence.

But the rating scale only works when everyone understands what each number means. For example, a rating of 3 should not mean “average” to one manager, “safe” to another manager, and “not good enough” to another. If the meaning is unclear, the system becomes subjective again, even though it looks objective.

The real value of a rating scale lies in the leadership action that follows the number.

To maintain objectivity within the Performance Management system, organisations use a standardised scoring scale, typically a 1 to 5 scale. This scale defines, in precise and measurable terms, what each rating represents, ensuring a common language of performance that transcends departmental boundaries and minimises individual manager bias.

Each point on the scale is clearly defined with explicit performance descriptors and criteria. This ensures that ratings are based on demonstrated evidence of performance against agreed expectations, with subjective judgement removed. The scale is applied at an individual level across all performance components, with ratings assigned based on verifiable outcomes, behavioural demonstration, and delivery against Key Results or KPIs.

By anchoring performance in clearly defined standards, the system achieves three critical outcomes:

  • Personal bias is significantly reduced: ratings can be challenged and defended against explicit criteria, which take the place of managerial opinion.
  • A shared language of performance is established across teams and functions: a “3” means the same thing in Finance as it does in Operations.
  • Evaluation becomes consistent, comparable, and defensible, enabling fair decisions on rewards, development, and advancement.

A rating should trigger a clear leadership response.

  • If performance is strong, the response may be recognition, stretch opportunities, succession consideration, or increased responsibility.
  • If performance is acceptable but not growing, the response may be coaching, clearer goals, or a development plan.
  • If performance is weak, the response may be closer supervision, targeted support, performance improvement actions, or consequences where needed.

The first question is: What rating did this person receive?
The more important leadership question is: What must we now do because of this rating?

That is why a rating scale only has value if it is consistently understood and if each rating leads to a defined leadership response that addresses current performance, whether the performance is good, average, declining or poor.

Rating 1 — Poor
Leadership response: Immediate performance improvement plan within 30 days
▼

Definition: Performance is significantly below the minimum standard. Output is insufficient, quality is unacceptable, or behaviour is actively damaging.

Leadership response: Immediate intervention. A formal performance improvement plan within 30 days. Weekly check-ins. Assess whether the gap is capability, motivation, or role-fit. A Rating 1 sustained beyond 60 days without visible movement is a role-fit decision.

Rating 2 — Needs Improvement
Leadership response: Structured development plan
▼

Definition: Performance is below the expected standard in one or more significant areas. The individual shows some capability but has consistent gaps in output, quality, or behaviour.

Leadership response: Structured development plan with specific targets and milestones. Fortnightly check-ins. Clear articulation of the standard required to achieve a Rating 3 by the next review cycle.

Rating 3 — Meets Expectations  SOLID GOLD
Leadership response: Recognition & retention conversation
▼

Definition: Performance fully meets the standard expected for this role, at this level, in this context. Output is consistent, quality is reliable, behaviour is aligned. This is exactly what the role requires.

The Solid Gold Reframe: Most PM systems implicitly treat a Rating 3 as mediocre — a design flaw that erodes morale and incentivises performative overreach. A Rating 3 is “Solid Gold”: a person who reliably delivers and represents the backbone of execution in any high-performing organisation.

Leadership response: Recognition of consistent contribution. Exploration of growth aspirations — without implying that remaining at a 3 is a failure. Many exceptional contributors are sustainably and appropriately at a 3.

Rating 4 — Exceeds Expectations
Leadership response: Stretch opportunities; retention investment
▼

Definition: Performance consistently goes beyond what the role requires. The individual proactively adds value, solves problems beyond their mandate, and elevates the team. A Rating 4 is a pattern.

Leadership response: Explicit recognition and visibility with senior leadership. Conversation about stretch opportunities, expanded scope, or accelerated development. Failure to invest in a sustained Rating 4 is a retention risk.

Rating 5 — Outstanding
Leadership response: Mandatory structural follow-through
▼

Definition: Performance is transformational within the context of the role. The individual exceeds their own targets and demonstrably elevates others and advances the organisation. A Rating 5 is rare and must be ratified.

Leadership response: A Rating 5 carries a leadership obligation — increased scope, a promotion discussion, a compensation review, or a high-impact project assignment. A Rating 5 acknowledged verbally with no structural follow-through is a trust-destroying event. The individual will note that outstanding performance produced no change — and will adjust their effort accordingly.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

The Solid Gold reframe generates significant discussion. Invite reflection: “What message has your body language, tone, or incentive system sent to people rated 3?” Also surface the Rating 5 accountability point: “In the last three years, how many people received a Rating 5? What happened to them afterwards?”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the rating that best reflects the performance standard they measure toward, and what the leadership response to that rating looks like in practice.

1.5 — The Impact of a Calibrated System
Three shifts

When a performance management system is functioning correctly, the performance review becomes a no-surprise event.

Because the criteria are transparent and the weighting is predetermined, team members understand exactly where they stand at any given moment. They know what is being measured, how much it counts, and what each rating means, including what leadership will do in response.

This transparency produces three powerful shifts in the organisational dynamic:

Shift 1
Strategic Transparency
▼

Every employee sees exactly how their specific contribution fuels the enterprise’s broader strategic goals. The line of sight between individual effort and organisational outcome becomes visible and motivating.

Shift 2
Shift in Dynamics
▼

The relationship between a leader and their team evolves from one of top-down oversight to one of shared investment in a common outcome. The scorecard becomes a shared tool and stops being a judgement handed down from above.

Shift 3
Pathways to Excellence
▼

The rating scale provides a clear roadmap for growth, showing exactly what is required to move from one level to the next. Ambition becomes actionable.

By anchoring ratings in clear descriptors and decision triggers, the organisation replaces subjective, instinct-based assessment with a rigorous, high-alignment framework that drives execution.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask: “When was a performance review last a surprise to someone in your team? Which was missing: the criteria, the weighting, or the meaning of the rating?” Take each of the three shifts in turn and ask for one sign that it is present in the participant’s own organisation.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

PM as an Execution Bridge

Understanding the structural logic of how performance management connects strategy to individual action, and why most PM systems fail to make that connection.

Section learning outcomes
  • Define how performance management connects enterprise outcomes to individual accountability.
  • Evaluate whether current performance arrangements build compliance or commitment.
2.1 — PM as the Execution Bridge
Four bridge functions

Performance management is the intelligence layer that determines whether the high-level organisational objectives actually translate into observable progress, or whether they remain aspirational statements in a presentation deck that is reviewed at the beginning of the year and quietly forgotten by the middle of it.

Moving from strategy design to execution requires a bridge. Without this bridge, strategic goals may be intellectually coherent but operationally invisible. They exist in language that leadership understands, but that language has not been translated into the daily decisions, priorities, and habits of the people responsible for delivery.

The primary purpose of performance management is to build and sustain that bridge, ensuring that every ounce of individual effort contributes to the organisation’s strategic direction. Without it, organisations generate what might be called random acts of excellence: genuine effort, real capability, strong intentions, none of which reliably move the needle.

Performance management frameworks create a line of sight: a direct, visible connection between enterprise strategy and individual contribution. When this line of sight exists, performance management becomes a motivational system and stops being a compliance exercise. An employee can sit down on a Monday morning and understand precisely how their work connects to the organisation’s most critical outcomes.

A performance management system performs four critical bridging functions between strategy and execution. When any one is missing, the bridge develops a structural gap — and strategy leaks through it.

Bridge 1
Decomposing the Big Picture
▼

Strategy lives at the enterprise level. PM translates it into progressively smaller, actionable units: enterprise outcomes → business unit contributions → team targets → individual responsibilities. Without decomposition, strategy remains a declaration and never becomes a task.

In practice: Breaking each Key Result into functional measurable delivery signals: the specific, daily contributions required from every team. Strategy descends from the enterprise level to the desk level.

Bridge 2
Defining the Logic of Success
▼

The PM system must make explicit what “success” looks like at each level — as a measurable, observable outcome with defined evidence. When the logic of success is ambiguous, individuals define it for themselves — and those self-definitions are rarely aligned.

In practice: Establishing exactly how performance is measured, where the data comes from, and which levers carry the most weight. This removes ambiguity and ensures that the rules of the game are agreed before the game is played.

Bridge 3
Calibrating the Rhythm
▼

Strategy operates on an annual or multi-year timeline. Individual performance operates daily. The PM system must establish a structured rhythm connecting these timelines. Without rhythm, PM becomes an isolated event.

In practice: Setting incremental targets so that leaders are never guessing about whether they are on track. The calculation logic is straightforward, and it tells the leadership team exactly what “on track” looks like every single month.

Progress per Period = (End Target − Baseline) ÷ Number of Periods
This establishes the expected incremental progress in each review period, creating a visible trajectory through the year.

Bridge 4
Structuring the Dialogue
▼

Data without conversation is noise. The PM system must structure the dialogue that turns performance data into decisions. Define: who speaks, how frequently, about what, and with what authority to decide. The quality of the PM conversation is the quality of the PM system.

In practice: Establishing a cadence for reviews so that performance updates become active leadership conversations: structured moments where the team asks what this data means and what to do next. A performance update is more than a data entry.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask participants to identify which bridge function is most underdeveloped. “Where does your strategy most commonly leak? At decomposition, at defining success, at the rhythm, or at the quality of the conversation?”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the bridge function most underdeveloped in their organisation, and where their strategy most often leaks.

2.2 — Compliance vs Commitment Architecture
The design choice

Every PM system is built on one of two architectural orientations: compliance or commitment.

A PM system fails when it is built solely for compliance, using pressure and monitoring to force results. The Strategy2Results® approach is built on a fundamentally different architecture: shared commitment, where the scorecard is a product of alignment and shared ownership, with no directive issued from above.

Compliance

A compliance-driven PM depicts an environment where:

  • Leader holds accountability
  • Assessment and judgement
  • Consequence and control
Commitment

A commitment-driven PM depicts an environment where:

  • Team co-owns accountability
  • Inquiry and problem-solving
  • Root cause; co-designed recovery

The choice is rarely made explicitly — but it is always made. The six dimensions below reveal which architecture is currently operating.

Dimension △ Compliance △ Commitment
PurposeEnsure minimum standards are met and deviations correctedGenerate shared ownership of results and continuous improvement
OwnershipLeader holds accountability; team is subject to itTeam co-owns accountability; leader facilitates
ToneAssessment and judgement; hierarchicalInquiry and problem-solving; collaborative
UnderperformanceConsequence and controlUnderstanding root cause; co-designing recovery
MotivationAvoiding negative consequenceIntrinsic meaning; shared pride in progress
PM experienceAnxiety-producing; done to the individualEnergy-generating; done with the individual
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Most organisations have compliance systems that use commitment language. The test: “When performance data shows underperformance, what is the first instinct of the leader — to fix the person, or to understand the system?” Invite leaders to identify the one structural change that would shift their system most decisively toward commitment.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: where their current PM system sits between compliance and commitment, and the one structural change that would shift it most.

2.3 — Leader vs Team: A Critical Distinction
Who is measured on what

One of the most frequently misunderstood aspects of performance management is the distinction between how a team is measured and how its leader is measured. Conflating the two creates misaligned incentives and obscures accountability.

The team is measured on the quality and completion of specific activities. The leader is measured on the cumulative outcome that those activities produce.

Dimension The Team Is Measured On The Leader Is Measured On
FocusActivities, outputs, and behaviours within the review periodCumulative outcome trajectory across the full performance cycle
Time horizonCurrent period: what was done, how it was doneEnd-of-cycle: are we on track to reach the target by year-end?
EvidenceDeliverable completion, quality scores, behavioural indicatorsCumulative progress calculation; gap to target; trend direction
Review frequencyMonthly / quarterlyQuarterly / bi-annually

The practical implication: A team can complete every planned activity on time (strong team performance) while the leader’s cumulative outcome trajectory is still off track (weak leadership performance). The leader is accountable for ensuring tasks are producing the outcomes the strategy requires.

Example: The strategic goal is to reduce complaint resolution time from 48 hours to 12 hours. The team is measured on their daily processing speed, quality of resolution, and adherence to handling protocols. The leader is measured on the steady, cumulative reduction in cycle time that demonstrates the strategy is gaining traction. If the team is active but the number is not moving, the leader must intervene.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

This is a powerful reframe for leaders who conflate team busyness with strategic progress. “Is your current PM review looking at your team’s activities — or at your cumulative outcome trajectory? When last did you review whether your team’s activities are actually producing the outcomes the strategy requires?”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: whether their current PM system evaluates the team member on execution progress and the leader on progress towards the strategic outcome or Key Result, and their own contribution to closing the gap.

Section 3 · Extrapolating — Where

PM in the Executive Context

Extending PM architecture to the enterprise level: how executive teams align their measurement signals, where execution gaps emerge, and how industry context is shifting.

Section learning outcomes

  • Evaluate the execution gaps that prevent performance arrangements from supporting strategic priorities.
3.1 — Unity in Diversity
10 executive PM contributions

At the enterprise level, the performance management system sits in the executive team. The C-suite is, collectively, the organisation’s measurement alignment system. When the executive team is not aligned on what they are measuring, why, and what they are doing about it, the enterprise PM system is structurally broken regardless of the tools in place.

Think of the executive team as The Alignment Brigade, more than heads of departments. Every leader carries deep professional instincts about what “progress” looks like. A CFO sees progress in the margins. A CTO sees it in system uptime and deployment velocity. A CHRO sees it in engagement scores and leadership capability. These instincts are valuable, but if they are not synchronised, they create measurement imbalances that fragment the organisation’s understanding of its own performance.

The Alignment Brigade concept recognises that each executive function carries a unique measurement responsibility — a “hot zone” of the enterprise that they are best positioned to track, interpret, and act upon. When these hot zones are integrated through a shared cadence and common language, the executive team becomes a precision measurement system. When they operate independently, they produce competing narratives of organisational performance.

The critical question for any executive team: “When we look at the same performance data, do we interpret it from the same perspective — or from the perspective of our function? And when the data tells us to act, do we act as a system or as a collection of functions?”

3.2 — The Three Execution Gaps
Where PM breaks down

Three structural gaps account for the majority of PM system failures at the enterprise level. Each gap is a predictable consequence of a specific design flaw.

Gap 1
Competing Definitions of Progress
▼

Different functions or leaders are using different definitions of what constitutes progress — finance says margin, operations says throughput, sales says pipeline. Without a unified definition of progress at the enterprise level, each function optimises its own metric at the expense of collective momentum.

Intervention: Establish an enterprise progress definition in the executive PM session. Agree on the three to five metrics that, if achieved, confirm the strategy is working. Everything else is a supporting indicator.

Gap 2
Measurement Blind Spots
▼

The organisation is systematically not measuring something that matters. Common blind spots: quality of cross-functional collaboration, health of the leadership pipeline, customer experience at key touchpoints. What is not measured is not managed — and eventually becomes a crisis.

Intervention: Map the current measurement system against the Three Sights of Progress. Ask: “What would we need to see to know our strategy is working before the financial results confirm it?”

Gap 3
Incentive Misalignment
▼

The incentive structure rewards behaviour inconsistent with the strategic intent. Leaders are rewarded for short-term financial performance while the strategy requires long-term capability investment. People follow the incentives. When incentives and strategy diverge, strategy loses.

Intervention: Audit the current incentive structure against strategic priorities. Redesign to reward the behaviours the strategy actually requires. Where are incentives producing the wrong behaviour? Which strategic priorities have no incentive attached?

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask participants to post in the chat the Execution Gap most present in their organisation. In most executive contexts, Gap 1 and Gap 3 will dominate. The discussion about why these gaps persist despite being well understood is where the real facilitation work happens. The answer is usually political: fixing the gap requires someone to give up control of a metric or accept shared accountability they have historically resisted.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the Execution Gap most present in their organisation, and why it persists.

3.3 — The Continuous PM Shift
Industry context

41% of organisations have moved to real-time or near-real-time feedback systems, replacing annual or bi-annual performance review cycles with continuous check-in models. This shift reflects a fundamental change in how high-performing organisations understand the relationship between feedback and performance.

Trend 1
From Annual to Continuous
▼

The annual performance review is being supplemented or replaced by structured quarterly conversations, monthly check-ins, and continuous pulse feedback. The shift recognises that annual feedback is too infrequent to change behaviour and too delayed to be developmental.

Trend 2
From Rating-Centric to Development-Centric
▼

Leading organisations are shifting from the rating (a backward-looking judgement) to development planning (a forward-looking investment). The rating becomes an input to development. Associated with higher engagement, stronger retention, and more rapid capability improvement.

Trend 3
From Individual to Team Performance
▼

As organisations become more cross-functional, individual performance ratings are being complemented by team performance assessments. Most outcomes require coordinated contribution. Organisations that exclusively measure individual performance create hidden disincentives for collaboration.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Invite participants to locate their organisation on the spectrum from traditional (annual, rating-centric, individual) to leading-edge (continuous, development-centric, team-integrated). “Where are you now — and where do you need to be in three years?”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the current PM architecture of their organisation, and the PM system currently in use.

Designing for Enterprise Performance

4.1 — The EXECUTION Framework
Nine design principles

The EXECUTION framework synthesises the nine design principles of a high-quality enterprise PM system. Each letter represents a principle that, when absent, creates a specific and predictable PM failure mode.

FACES and EXECUTION

FACES · the function test

FACES is the function test. It asks whether the PM system is serving its five essential purposes: building a results culture, aligning individual performance, making measurement transparent, embedding shared accountability, and supporting development. In simple terms, FACES answers: “What must this PM system achieve for people and performance?”

EXECUTION · the design quality test

EXECUTION is the design quality test. It goes deeper into the architecture of the system. It asks whether the PM system has the right design principles: strategy anchoring, line of sight, evidence, collective ownership, outcomes, transparent logic, incentive alignment, review rhythm and collective insight. In simple terms, EXECUTION answers: “How must this PM system be designed so that it actually produces those functions?”

FACES tells us what a PM system must deliver.
EXECUTION tells us what must be designed into the system for it to deliver.

The Nine Design Principles

E
ENTERPRISE
Strategy First

The PM system must be anchored to enterprise strategy. Every metric should be traceable to a strategic outcome. The enterprise intent is the filter through which all PM design decisions are made.

Self-check: Can every metric in your PM system be traced back to a strategic priority?
X
eXECUTION
Line-of-Sight

Every individual must see a clear line of sight from their daily work to enterprise strategy. Line-of-sight is the translation mechanism that makes strategy personal. Without it, individuals execute their role description and lose sight of the strategy.

Self-check: Can every person on your team draw a line between their daily work and the strategy?
E
EVIDENCE
Evidence-Based Measurement

Performance assessments must be grounded in defined, agreed evidence. Evidence-based measurement protects the integrity of the PM system and builds trust. Without it, ratings are subjective and disputes are inevitable.

Self-check: Does your team know exactly what evidence is required to achieve each rating?
C
COLLECTIVE
Collective Ownership

Accountability for results must be shared across the team and organisation. Collective ownership means everyone with a contribution to an outcome has a stake in its measurement and a responsibility for its achievement.

Self-check: When a result is missed, does accountability sit with one person or is it genuinely shared?
U
UNDERSTAND
Outcomes First

The PM system must measure outcomes — the actual change produced. A team can be 100% active while producing 0% of the required outcome. Leaders must measure impact.

Self-check: If your team completed all planned activities this period, would outcomes automatically follow?
T
TRANSPARENT
Measurement Logic

The logic by which performance is calculated, rated, and rewarded must be transparent to everyone subject to it. Hidden formulas and opaque scoring destroy confidence in the system and generate cynicism that undermines the entire PM architecture.

Self-check: Does your team fully understand how their performance score is calculated?
I
INCENTIVE
Incentive Alignment

The incentive structure must reward the behaviours and outcomes the strategy requires. Misaligned incentives are one of the most reliable predictors of strategy failure. When what gets rewarded diverges from what the strategy needs, people follow the incentive — this is a design failure.

Self-check: What behaviours does your incentive system actually reward? Do they match your strategy?
O
ONGOING
Review Rhythm

PM must be an ongoing rhythm. An annual review cycle produces information too infrequently to inform in-year course correction. The review rhythm must be consistent and embedded in the operational calendar so that PM is the way work is managed. Monthly reviews monitor operational signals. Quarterly reviews evaluate strategic progress. Annual reviews assess long-term performance and development.

Self-check: Is PM embedded in how your team works week to week, or does it only surface at review time?
N
NAVIGATE
Collective Insight

The ultimate function of a PM system is navigation: using performance data, collectively interpreted, to make better decisions about where to go and how to get there. A PM system that collects data but does not generate collective insight is a reporting system dressed as management.

Self-check: In your last performance review, did you make a decision together that you would not have made individually?
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Teach the distinction first: FACES is what the PM system must deliver, and EXECUTION is what must be designed into the system for it to deliver. Then teach each principle with its self-check question. “Which principle is weakest in your current PM system? What is the cost of that gap? What is the one change that would address it?” This becomes the bridge into the Application section, where each group designs the FACES and the EXECUTION of its own PM architecture.

Unit Summary
Unit 6 synthesis
Awareness — What

PM is the leadership response to measurement. The correct sequence: define success, design the management system, design the measurement infrastructure, then manage using data.

Intelligence — Why

PM is an execution bridge with four functions: decomposing the big picture, defining the logic of success, calibrating the rhythm, and structuring the dialogue. The architecture choice — compliance vs commitment — shapes every conversation.

Extrapolating — Where

The executive team is the organisation’s measurement alignment system. Each CXO hot zone contributes a distinct signal. The Three Execution Gaps explain most PM failures at enterprise level.

Integration — Collective

FACES tells us what a PM system must deliver. EXECUTION tells us what must be designed into the system for it to deliver: nine design principles, from strategy anchoring to collective insight.

Application — In Practice

Each group designs the PM architecture for the strategy in its Capstone Blueprint: the scoring logic, the FACES and the EXECUTION of the architecture, and a PM scorecard for the CEO and for the CFO on two of the group’s Key Results. The confirmed record feeds the team’s Capstone Blueprint.

“A performance management system that only measures has not yet become a management system. The moment measurement becomes the basis for a decision, a conversation, and an accountable intervention — that is the moment execution begins.”
