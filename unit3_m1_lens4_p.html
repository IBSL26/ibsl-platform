Aligning Heart & Mind

Influence

Before strategy becomes operational activity, it must be interpreted, experienced, and accepted by the people responsible for carrying it forward. This unit establishes the human operating system that enables strategy to move from intention to execution. It equips you to lead people through the changes the strategy requires.

Key learning outcomes Critically examine how functional expertise, personality, cognitive bias and emotional conditions influence the interpretation of strategy. Demonstrate shared strategic understanding and commitment across the leadership system. Recognise the organisational conditions that strengthen or weaken strategic alignment. Lead people through the changes the strategy requires, with deliberate attention to readiness, resistance and adoption.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

What is Aligning Heart & Mind?

Before any strategy can be executed, it must travel through people. People arrive at every strategy meeting carrying a personality profile, emotional defaults, and personal motives that determine how much energy they will invest in the organisation’s goals.

Section learning outcomes
  • Examine how functional expertise, cognitive bias and emotion shape the interpretation of strategy.
  • Recognise what people need in order to adopt a strategic change, and the behaviours that sustain it.
1.1 — The Human Terrain of Strategy Execution
Foundation

Strategy enters an organisation through the people responsible for interpreting and acting on it. Before strategy becomes operational activity, it passes through two human filters:

Cognition — Mind

How individuals interpret what strategy means. Shaped by professional expertise, mental shortcuts, past experience, and risk perception.

Emotion — Heart

How individuals experience the strategy. Shapes engagement energy, morale, friction load, psychological safety, and values alignment.

The Result

Whether strategy produces clarity or confusion, commitment or compliance, momentum or hesitation. Execution begins inside the human system.

The deeper challenge behind execution friction: Strategy is fundamentally a human interpretation problem. The Functional Bias Problem reveals that organisations do not interpret strategy as a single, shared reality. When perspectives diverge, even highly capable executives can unintentionally pull the organisation in different directions.

1.2 — The Five Principles of Aligning Heart & Mind
Expand Each Principle
Principle 1
Strategy Moves Through People
▼

The human entry point of execution. Alignment of the mind (cognition) and heart (emotion) is the first execution system.

1
Interpretation

People first make sense of the strategy. They interpret what it means, what matters, what has changed, what is expected, and what risks or opportunities they see. This is where assumptions, mental models, expertise, past experience and bias shape the meaning people attach to the strategy.

2
Experience

People then experience the strategy emotionally. They may feel energised, uncertain, threatened, excluded, hopeful, tired or resistant. This emotional state affects trust, morale, psychological safety, engagement energy and the willingness to participate honestly in execution.

3
Choices

People then make choices and trade-offs. They decide what to prioritise, what to resource, what to protect, what to stop, what to challenge and what to support. This is where strategy becomes real through judgement, commitment, accountability and allocation of attention.

4
Actions

People finally act in ways that either carry the strategy forward or weaken it. Behaviour shows up in follow-through, handovers, routines, conversations, ownership, discipline and how people respond when execution becomes difficult.

5
Results

Results emerge when thinking is clear, feeling is aligned, decisions are coherent, and behaviour is disciplined. When any part of the chain is weak, execution begins to leak.

“Strategy does not jump from intention to outcome. It moves through people. It is interpreted through thinking, experienced through feeling, converted through deciding, and delivered through behaving.”
➤ Insight: Can you name the dominant emotion your team is carrying into the current strategy? If you cannot name it, you cannot manage it.
Principle 2
Misalignment Fragments Effort
▼

The cost of ignoring the human system. When interpretation and emotional experience are not aligned, effort begins to fragment. Everyone remains active, yet the organisation moves with split effort.

Chinese Whispers

Intent mutates as it travels downward. Frontline execution bears little resemblance to boardroom intent.

Clarification Debt

60–80% of leadership time spent re-explaining, correcting, realigning. Leaders are patching.

Silent Disengagement

People comply outwardly but withdraw inwardly. Looks like cooperation. Feels like exhaustion.

Data: Low-alignment organisations experience 41% lower productivity and 48% higher turnover. This drift is dangerous because it is gradual, rationalised, and expensive (Gallup; PMI).

Principle 3
Alignment Must Be Designed
▼

Alignment rarely emerges naturally. It must be intentionally designed through structured interventions that make interpretation and emotional climate visible. Two diagnostic assessments support this design:

OCEAVL Assessment

Surfaces psychological tendencies influencing executive decision-making. Dimensions: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Emotional Stability, Values Alignment, Learning Orientation.

Emotional Climate Assessment

Identifies the prevailing state of being across six core emotional categories: Joy, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Disgust, Surprise. Allows you to stabilise engagement before demanding execution.

You complete the OCEAVL Assessment in part 3.1. The Emotional Climate Assessment is available on demand.

➤ Insight: These assessments are leadership mirrors. Their power lies in the conversation they generate.
Principle 4
Human Reactions Are Predictable
▼

Reactions to strategic change follow predictable psychological patterns. The SCARF Model (David Rock) explains five social conditions that trigger emotional responses during change. The human brain processes social change using the same threat circuitry as physical danger — any new goal, system, or way of working is evaluated emotionally before it is evaluated rationally.

The five SCARF triggers are explored in detail in part 1.3.2.

➤ Insight: When you can name which SCARF trigger is driving resistance, you can address the root cause.
Principle 5
Execution Must Be Visible in Behaviour
▼

Strategic alignment ultimately becomes visible through behaviour. Without observable behaviour, alignment remains theoretical. With behavioural standards, alignment becomes visible, measurable, and sustainable.

The ACE-IT Framework defines five universally desired behaviours: Accountability, Commitment, Engagement, Integrity, Transparency. These behaviours are independent of an organisation’s stated values — they represent the minimum behavioural standard for any leadership team serious about execution. Explored in detail in part 1.3.4.

➤ Insight: If someone external observed your team for one week, which ACE-IT behaviour would they see most clearly? Which would be absent?

1.3 — Change Management: The 4 Checks of Mind, Heart, Hands and Habit
Expand Each Check

1.1 showed that strategy reaches people through two filters, Mind and Heart. 1.2 showed that alignment has to be designed. This part applies both to the moment a strategy asks people to work differently.

Change management is the leadership discipline of moving people from current practice to the practice the strategy requires.

The event and the transition

A change is an event. It is announced on a date, and on that date the structure, the system or the target is different. The transition is what each affected person goes through afterwards: letting go of a familiar practice, working out what the new one asks of them, and becoming able to do it. The event belongs to the organisation. The transition belongs to the person.

Your leadership team debates a change for months before announcing it. You finish your own transition on the day everyone else begins theirs. The distance between the two is where impatience on one side meets resistance on the other. Change management is the work of leading people across that distance.

The 4 Checks of Mind, Heart, Hands and Habit

A change is adopted when it passes four checks for the people it affects. Mind and Heart are the two filters from 1.1. Hands and Habit extend the same path to the point where the new practice is real. Each check asks one question and has one tool. Expand each check.

Check 1 · Mind
They understand the change, why it matters and what they do differently.
▼

Understanding is tested by what people can say back in their own words. An announcement shows that you have spoken. The check shows what the people have understood, after each of them has passed the message through their own expertise, shortcuts, experience and sense of risk.

Tool: the 3S Check (1.3.1).

✓ Passed when: people in the same team describe the change in the same way.
⚠ Failed when: people repeat the slogan and cannot say what they personally do differently.
Check 2 · Heart
They want it to succeed.
▼

Wanting is a decision each person makes. It is the ignition point described in Section 2: “This matters to me.” Most changes threaten something for the people they touch: standing, certainty, control, belonging or fairness. Until that threat is addressed, a person who understands the change holds back their energy.

Tool: the SCARF Model (1.3.2).

✓ Passed when: people bring problems and ideas to the change.
⚠ Failed when: the room is quiet, effort is minimal and people say they are “waiting for alignment”.
Check 3 · Hands
They are able to do it.
▼

Ability is practical. It rests on four things: skill, time, authority and tools. A person can understand a change and want it, and still be unable to do it on an ordinary working day. From a distance, that person looks the same as one who is unwilling.

Tool: the STAT Check (1.3.3).

✓ Passed when: people do the new work unaided.
⚠ Failed when: workarounds appear, decisions are passed upward and the new practice is done last.
Check 4 · Habit
It has become the way they work.
▼

A practice becomes habit when it is repeated, noticed and expected. Until then it depends on effort and attention, and both fall away under pressure. The five ACE-IT behaviours are what hold a new practice in place after the launch has passed.

Tool: ACE-IT (1.3.4).

✓ Passed when: the practice holds when you are absent.
⚠ Failed when: the old way returns under the first pressure.

Why the order matters

Each check depends on the one before it. A person cannot want a change they have misread. They will not perform a change they do not want, and skill offered to an unwilling person is left unused. A practice cannot become habit while most people are unable to perform it.

The practical rule follows. A change stalls at the first check it fails, and that check is where your effort belongs. Effort spent further along the path is lost.

  • A team that understands a change and resents it has stalled at Heart. More explanation will not move it.
  • A team that wants a change and has no authority to act has stalled at Hands. More encouragement will not move it.
  • A team that did it well for a month and drifted back has stalled at Habit. A relaunch will not hold it.

Your four jobs as leader

Leading the transition means doing one job at each check. Each job is taught with its tool in the four parts that follow.

1.3.1 — The 3S Check: Shift, Stake, Step
Three Questions

3S is the Mind check. It asks whether the people affected have understood the change as you intended it. Understanding is interpretation. Each person rebuilds the message through their own expertise, mental shortcuts, past experience and risk perception. The Functional Bias Problem from 1.1 applies in full: finance hears a cost decision, operations hears a process change, and the front line hears a judgement on how they have worked until now.

You are poorly placed to judge this for yourself. After months inside a change, its logic feels obvious to you, and an obvious message feels like a clear one. The 3S Check replaces that feeling with a test. Mind passes when the people affected can say three things about the change in their own words.

Shift
“What is changing?”
What It Is
The Shift names the practice that ends and the practice that begins, for a named group, from a named date. It is concrete enough to be observed: someone watching the work could tell whether it is happening. A direction such as “more customer-focused” leaves every person to decide what it means, and each will decide differently.
Weak
“We are becoming more customer-focused.”
Clear
“From March, agents resolve complaints on the first call.”
Shows Up As
People describe the old practice under a new name • Different teams describe different changes
⚠ Execution: Chinese Whispers. The change mutates as it travels, and each function executes its own version.
➤ Your move: State the Shift as from and to: what stops, what starts, for whom, from when.
Stake
“What do we gain if we change, and what do we lose if we stay as we are?”
What It Is
The Stake is why the change matters, and it has two sides. The gain says what the change is for. The loss says what standing still will cost. Both are needed, because people weigh a loss more heavily than an equal gain. A change described only by its benefits leaves the people affected holding the one visible loss: the practice they are asked to give up. Naming what is lost by staying put places a second loss on the scales. The Stake is stated in terms the affected group cares about. An enterprise benefit that never reaches their work reads as someone else’s reason.
Weak
“It supports our strategy.”
Clear
“We keep the customers who now leave after a two-day wait. If we stay as we are, we keep losing them.”
Shows Up As
“Because head office said so” • People can describe the change and cannot say what it is for
⚠ Execution: Compliance without conviction. The change is carried out while it is watched and set aside under pressure.
➤ Your move: State the gain and the loss together, in the group’s own terms.
Step
“What do I do differently?”
What It Is
The Step is the person’s own part. It is said in the first person and is small enough to do next week. The Shift belongs to the organisation and the Step belongs to the person, so each role affected needs its own. A change with a clear Shift and no Step is understood as something that will happen to other people.
Weak
“Embrace the new process.”
Clear
“I decide on the call, within my limit.”
Shows Up As
“I am waiting to be told” • People agree with the change and carry on as before
⚠ Execution: Agreement without movement. The change is endorsed in meetings and absent from daily work.
➤ Your move: Give each affected role its own Step, and ask people to say it back.

How to run the 3S Check

  1. Write your own Shift, Stake and Step for the change.
  2. Ask three people from the affected group to say theirs, without prompting.
  3. Compare their versions with yours and with each other.

Reading the result

What you hearWhat it tells you
The versions match.Mind passes. Move to the Heart check.
Same Shift, different Stake.People know what is changing and disagree on why. Restate the Stake in their terms.
Shift and Stake are clear, and there is no Step.People see the change as someone else’s. Give each role its own Step.
Different Shifts.Chinese Whispers. Return to one message, said in the same words by every leader.

Where a version differs from yours, the person’s own filter has reshaped it. The difference is information. It shows which part of the message to restate, and to whom.

Your job at this check · Mind — Explain

The job: give people a version of the change they can repeat. State the Shift, the Stake and the Step in the same words each time, in every forum, until people can say them back. Repetition feels excessive to you long before the message has landed.

Complete when: three people from the affected group give the same Shift, Stake and Step.

Sounds like: “From March, complaints are resolved on the first call. We are losing customers who wait two days for an answer. Your part is to decide on the call, within your limit.”

⚠ When skipped: people fill the gap with their own version, and Clarification Debt builds: leadership time is spent re-explaining a change that was never understood the first time.

1.3.2 — The SCARF Model: Five Triggers of Human Resistance
Expand Each Trigger

SCARF is the Heart check. When a trigger fires, a person who understands the change still withholds their energy from it.

Expand each trigger to explore what activates it, how it shows up in behaviour, its execution impact, and the counter-strategy that addresses it.

Status
“Will this reduce my importance?”
Activated By
New leaders • New KPIs exposing performance • New systems shifting expertise • Changed reporting lines
Shows Up As
Defensive behaviour • Undermining decisions • Withholding information or cooperation
⚠ Execution: Political friction • Slowed decisions • Misalignment masked as professionalism
➤ Counter: Explicitly acknowledge existing credibility. Involve senior leaders in co-designing new structures — status is protected when people have agency in the change.
Certainty
“Do I know what success looks like?”
Activated By
New goals • Ambiguous ownership • Changing metrics or timelines • Shifting review processes
Shows Up As
Repeated clarification requests • Delay disguised as “waiting for alignment” • Reversion to old habits
⚠ Execution: Loss of momentum • Paralysis under pressure • Risk avoidance
➤ Counter: Create visible milestones early. The brain seeks pattern — give it enough structure to move without removing all ambiguity.
Autonomy
“Am I losing control of my work?”
Activated By
New systems • Standardised processes • Centralised decision rights • Increased oversight or reporting
Shows Up As
Passive compliance • Minimal effort delivery • Resistance through bureaucracy
⚠ Execution: Low engagement energy • Mechanical execution • Fragile performance
➤ Counter: Standardise the what and offer bounded choice on the how. Bounded choice preserves autonomy while honouring the mandate.
Relatedness
“Do I still belong here?”
Activated By
Team restructuring • New leaders or external hires • Cross-functional initiatives • Remote or hybrid shifts
Shows Up As
Silo behaviour • Reduced collaboration • Guarded communication
⚠ Execution: Trust erosion • Coordination breakdown • Rework and duplication
➤ Counter: Create visible connection rituals during transitions. People need to feel part of the tribe before they will share resources or risk vulnerability.
Fairness
“Is this being applied equitably?”
Activated By
Uneven workload • Selective enforcement of rules • Inconsistent rewards • Unclear decision rationale
Shows Up As
Cynicism • Gossip replacing dialogue • Emotional withdrawal
⚠ Execution: Values misalignment • Morale drain • Cultural decay
➤ Counter: Proactively explain rationale behind decisions — especially asymmetric ones. Transparency about trade-offs is perceived as fairness even when outcomes differ.
Your job at this check · Heart — Involve

The job: give people a hand in shaping how the change lands. You decide what changes and open up how it is done. Name plainly what people give up. Identify the live SCARF trigger and apply its counter. Involve their managers first, because managers carry the change into every team conversation.

Why it works: a part in the decision answers each trigger. Being consulted restores Status. Seeing the plan early restores Certainty. Choosing how restores Autonomy. Working on it together restores Relatedness. Hearing the reasons restores Fairness.

Complete when: the people affected can point to a part of the change they shaped.

Sounds like: “You know these calls better than I do. Where should the decision limit sit?”

⚠ When skipped: Silent Disengagement. People comply, do the minimum and wait to be chased.

✎ Reflection
1.3.3 — The STAT Check: Skill, Time, Authority, Tools
Four Elements

STAT is the Hands check. It asks whether people who understand a change and want it are able to carry it out on an ordinary working day. Mind and Heart sit inside the person. Hands sits largely in the conditions around the person, and those conditions are set by you and your fellow leaders.

This is the check most easily misread. A person who is unable to perform a change looks, from a distance, like a person who is unwilling: the new practice is late, partial or missing. If you read that as resistance, you answer with more persuasion, when the gap is a missing decision right or a system that still fits the old way. STAT separates the two. Hands passes when all four elements are in place.

Skill
“Do they know how to do it?”
What It Is
Skill is the practised ability to do the new work to standard. It is built by doing the work with feedback. A briefing transfers information, and information becomes skill only after a person has performed the practice several times and been corrected.
Shows Up As
Errors and rework • Reliance on one or two experts • People reverting to the old method “to get it done”
⚠ Execution: Quality falls at the moment the change needs to prove itself, and the old way looks safer.
➤ Your move: Teach, practise on real work and coach on the job. Pair the experts with the rest.
Time
“Do they have the hours to do it?”
What It Is
Time is the working capacity to perform the new practice properly. Most changes are added to a full day. When nothing is removed, the new work competes with the old, and the old work has the deadlines, the habits and the measures on its side.
Shows Up As
The new practice is done last • Done in a rush • Dropped on a busy day
⚠ Execution: The change is alive in quiet weeks and absent in busy ones.
➤ Your move: Take old work away before adding new work, and say what stops.
Authority
“Are they allowed to decide and act?”
What It Is
Authority is the permission to decide and act as the change requires. Many changes move decisions closer to the customer or the front line. Until the limit of the new decision is stated, the safest course for the individual is to ask permission, and the decision travels back up to where it used to sit.
Shows Up As
Escalations • People ask permission for what the change asks them to do
⚠ Execution: The change runs slower than the practice it replaced, and you become the bottleneck.
➤ Your move: Set the decision limit, state it openly, and back the first decisions made under it. The first decision that is overruled teaches everyone to keep asking.
Tools
“Do they have the systems and resources?”
What It Is
Tools are the systems, information and resources the new practice depends on. A practice has to be possible in the system people use every day. Where the system, the form or the budget still fits the old way, people build workarounds, and a workaround is the old practice surviving inside the new one.
Shows Up As
Spreadsheets beside the system • Manual steps • Requests waiting on another team
⚠ Execution: Two versions of the truth, extra effort and errors that stay hidden.
➤ Your move: Provide access and budget before the start date, and retire the old form or route.

One missing element is enough to stop people who understand and want the change. Training closes a Skill gap. It leaves a Time, Authority or Tools gap exactly where it was.

Your job at this check · Hands — Equip

The job: make the new practice possible on an ordinary working day. Run the STAT Check with the people affected, since they know what is missing. Supply it, and remove the old work, rules and approvals that stand in the way.

Complete when: a person can perform the new practice from start to finish without asking permission or using a workaround.

Sounds like: “What do you need from me to do this on Monday?”

⚠ When skipped: willing people fail, and their failure is read as resistance. Heart, which had passed, begins to fail as well.

➤ Insight: Which of the four is your organisation quickest to supply, and which is it slowest to grant?

1.3.4 — ACE-IT: The Observable Behaviour Standard
Expand Each Behaviour

ACE-IT is the Habit check. These five behaviours are what hold a new practice in place.

A
Accountability
▼
Definition
Taking full ownership for results. Acknowledging missteps and driving corrective action.
Desired Behaviour
Defines clear ownership • Acknowledges missteps • Holds self and others to measurable standards
✓ Observable: Commitments tracked • Reviews end with named owners • “We missed it, here’s the fix” language common
C
Commitment
▼
Definition
Staying anchored to agreed strategic intent even when pressure rises. Following through despite discomfort.
Desired Behaviour
Aligns daily choices to long-term direction • Protects priorities from noise • Closes loops they open
✓ Observable: Strategic priorities stay consistent • Leaders decline non-strategic work • Promises kept visibly
E
Engagement
▼
Definition
Actively involving self and others in sense-making, dialogue, and execution. Listening to understand.
Desired Behaviour
Invites participation and dissent • Connects roles to purpose • Creates two-way dialogue
✓ Observable: Team meetings are two-way • Feedback loops visible • People quote the shared “why”
I
Integrity
▼
Definition
Acting consistently with values, facts, and truth — even when inconvenient. Declaring conflicts of interest early.
Desired Behaviour
Says what is true, even when it is hard • Uses data to support choices • References values in trade-offs
✓ Observable: Evidence cited before decisions • Values referenced in hard choices • Teams trust word matches deed
T
Transparency
▼
Definition
Making reasoning, progress, and constraints visible to all stakeholders. Sharing challenges openly.
Desired Behaviour
Communicates decisions with rationale • Documents choices and lessons • Shares progress including setbacks
✓ Observable: Dashboards updated regularly • Decisions logged and accessible • “No surprises” culture evident

How ACE-IT holds a new practice

Habit is the last check and the one asked earliest. The question is put before the change starts: how will the five behaviours hold the new practice in place? A change with no answer has no plan for the day after launch.

BehaviourWhat it does for a new practice
AccountabilityOne named person owns the practice and its result.
CommitmentThe practice is kept when pressure rises and the old way is quicker.
EngagementPeople talk about how it is going and improve it together.
IntegritySlips are reported as they are, and early.
TransparencyIts results are visible to everyone the change affects.
Your job at this check · Habit — Reinforce

The job: make the new practice the expected practice. Show it yourself first. Notice it when it happens, recognise it by name and correct a slip early. Put it into the regular review so that it is asked about every time. What you ask about, people repeat. A practice that is never asked about is understood to be optional.

Complete when: the practice holds through a full review period with you absent.

Sounds like: “I saw you close that case on the call. That is the standard.”

⚠ When skipped: the old way returns under the first pressure, and the organisation concludes that the change was a phase.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why Does Heart & Mind Alignment Determine Execution Outcomes?

Within the Strategy2Results® process, Aligning Heart & Mind functions as the ignition point of execution — the moment where strategy transitions from a document describing intent into a system capable of producing movement.

Section learning outcomes
  • Explain why shared understanding and commitment convert strategy into execution.
  • Distinguish commitment from compliance and explain why change delivers value only when it is adopted.
2.1 — The Ignition Point of Strategy Execution
Foundation Logic

A strategy document contains strategic potential. It articulates direction, ambition, and possibility. However, potential by definition represents energy at rest. For strategic potential to convert into kinetic energy — movement, execution, and results — human action must occur. No system executes itself.

Execution begins the moment an individual decides: “This matters to me. I am willing to invest energy in making this succeed.” Until that moment occurs across the organisation, strategy remains theoretical. Aligning Heart & Mind activates the human energy required to convert intention into action. This is the ignition point.

Without this ignition point, strategy remains defined but not delivered. With it, the organisation shifts from supervised effort to self-driven momentum — the strategic payoff that makes alignment the highest-leverage leadership intervention available.

✎ Reflection
2.2 — Three Foundational Logics
Why It Matters Operationally
I. Potential vs. Kinetic Energy Logic

A strategy document is strategic potential — energy at rest. For it to become kinetic energy (movement, execution, results), a human decision must activate it. The alignment of heart and mind is precisely the conversion mechanism. Every day that decision is not made is a day strategic potential sits unused. The organisation does not move until people decide to move it. Leadership cannot shortcut this decision — it can only create the conditions for it.

II. Compliance vs. Commitment Logic
Compliance — Mind Only

People act because authority requires it, rules enforce it, or systems monitor it. Produces visible output short-term. Requires continuous supervision. Creates hidden costs: leadership time consumed by follow-ups, teams dependent on instruction, decision-making slows as control increases. Mechanical and brittle.

Commitment — Mind & Heart

People act because they understand strategic intent, believe the direction is meaningful, and feel personal ownership. Generates behaviours compliance cannot sustain: discretionary effort, adaptive problem-solving, resilience when conditions change. Execution continues even when leaders are not present.

III. The Strategic Payoff of Alignment

When both cognition and emotion are aligned: people act with ownership, coordination improves across functions, and leaders spend less time correcting and more time advancing strategy. This shift reduces rework, lowers escalation, and preserves leadership capacity for decisions that truly require executive attention. Aligning Heart & Mind is the mechanical conversion point where strategic potential becomes strategic movement.

✎ Reflection
2.3 — Why Change Must Be Led: Installed and Adopted
The Value Logic

2.2 described a strategy as potential energy that a human decision converts into movement. A change follows the same logic. On the day it is installed, its value is still potential. The value is earned later, when people work the new way without being watched.

Installed

The new system, structure or KPI is in place, and people have been told.

Installation is a project outcome. It can be planned, funded and delivered on a date, and it is easy to report as success.

What you see: the launch is complete, the training register is signed and the dashboard is live.

What it yields: cost. The organisation has paid for the change and is still working the old way.

Adopted

People work the new way without supervision. The change has passed all four checks.

Adoption is a behavioural outcome. It arrives group by group, at the pace of each group’s slowest check, and no launch date can declare it.

What you see: the new practice holds on a busy day, people correct each other, and the old workaround has gone.

What it yields: value. The benefit the strategy counted on is being earned.

The adoption gap

The time between installed and adopted is the adoption gap. While it lasts, the organisation runs two practices at once: the new one it has built and the old one people still use. Supervision rises, because compliance has to be checked. The benefits counted when the strategy was approved remain unearned.

The gap also has a cost that outlasts the change itself. Every change that is installed and left unadopted teaches the organisation that a change can be waited out. The next change starts with less belief, and Heart is harder to pass.

The gap closes one check at a time. Compliance can install a change. Commitment is what carries it through to Habit, and adoption holds when you notice and reinforce the new practice.

Three signals a change is installed and awaiting adoption

  1. Use of the new practice falls when you are absent.
  2. Reports are complete and the result has not moved.
  3. People call it “the new system” and describe their own work as unchanged.

Data: 81% of those who planned for reinforcement met or exceeded their project objectives (Prosci, Best Practices in Change Management, 12th edition).

Where Does the Leadership Team Stand?

The OCEAVL Assessment shows the behavioural profile you bring to the strategy, and the profile your leadership team brings together. It shows where alignment work starts.

Section learning outcomes
  • Recognise where interpretation, emotional climate and behaviour strengthen or weaken alignment, and the signals that misalignment is escalating into crisis.
  • Assess the readiness of the people most affected by the changes the strategy requires.
3.1 — The OCEAVL Assessment: Personal and Team Behavioural Profile
Seven Dimensions

Principle 3 established that alignment must be designed. The OCEAVL Assessment is where the design starts. It surfaces the psychological tendencies that influence executive decision-making, across seven dimensions.

The assessment maps the behavioural dispositions that shape how a leader interprets strategy, manages disagreement and responds under pressure. It is a personal assessment: you complete it for yourself, on your own page. The portal then reads the scores of your whole team together.

Portfolio work · How to complete and read your OCEAVL Assessment
  1. Score yourself from 1 to 5 on each of the seven dimensions: 1 is strongly low, 3 is balanced and 5 is strongly high. Work alone. Your scores are your own.
  2. A score of 4 or 5 is High, 3 is Balanced, and 1 or 2 is Low. This gives your own level on each dimension.
  3. Read your own profile. Each level carries a risk, a response and three routines that hold the response in place.
  4. Select Submit to My Team’s Capstone. Your scores join your team’s profile.
  5. The level held by most members of your team is the team’s level for that dimension. Your team’s profile appears in your team’s Capstone Blueprint once every member has submitted.

Every level carries a risk. A High level is a disposition to manage, as Low and Balanced are.

Your team sees the team’s levels only. Your own scores stay on your page and reach your facilitator when you select Submit to Facilitator at the end of the unit.

4.1 — Leading Change with One Voice
Four Commitments

The 4 Checks describe what one group of people needs from you. Most changes reach a group through several leaders at once. People hear from you, watch your fellow leaders, and compare. Where you differ, the group takes the difference as the real message.

What one voice creates

The Converging Zone is the point where the separate views of each function come together into one shared reading of the strategy. A leadership team that reaches it has Collective Intelligence, and the organisation hears one voice. The two columns show what people receive with it and without it.

With Collective Intelligence
  • Direction remains clear across all layers
  • Resources reinforce priorities
  • Systems enable execution
  • People move with coordinated effort

✓ One coherent signal across the organisation. Changes pass the 4 Checks of Mind, Heart, Hands and Habit.

Without Collective Intelligence
  • Different leaders transmit different interpretations
  • Changes stall at the first check they fail
  • Functions receive competing signals
  • Strategic instability spreads invisibly

⚠ Multiple conflicting signals. Each function becomes a source of execution friction.

The four commitments

In the Converging Zone, your leadership team makes four commitments that no single function can make alone. Each one protects a check. Expand each commitment.

One message
Protects Mind
What Your Team Does
You and your fellow leaders give the same Shift, Stake and Step for each change. The Functional Bias Problem works on you first: each of you translates the change through your own function before passing it on. The test is the 3S Check applied to the leadership team itself. Each of you writes the three for the same change, and you compare the versions before anyone speaks to their people.
⚠ When absent: Each function hears a different change, and the 3S Check fails across the organisation.
One example
Protects Heart
What Your Team Does
You show the ACE-IT behaviours the change needs before you ask for them. People read your diary, questions and decisions as the real message. If you ask for the new practice and keep to the old one, you have told the organisation which of the two is safe.
⚠ When absent: People copy what you do and discount what you say.
One sequence
Protects Hands
What Your Team Does
You and your fellow leaders pool your changes and count how many land on the same group in the same period. The team then agrees the order and the pace. Each function sees its own change, and each change looks reasonable alone. The total load on a group is visible only when the team looks together, and Time is the first STAT element to give way under it.
⚠ When absent: One group is asked to absorb several changes together, and its willingness falls.
Data: Employees’ willingness to support enterprise change fell from 74% in 2016 to 43% in 2022, while planned changes per employee rose from two to ten (Gartner, reported in Harvard Business Review, 2023).
One owner
Protects Habit
What Your Team Does
Each transition has one named leader who answers for adoption, through to Habit. The owner runs the 4 Checks, reports which check the change has reached, and stays with it after the launch, when attention moves to the next priority.
⚠ When absent: The change is installed and nobody answers for whether it is adopted.
Data: Projects with extremely effective sponsors were 79% likely to meet their objectives, against 27% with extremely ineffective sponsors (Prosci, Best Practices in Change Management, 12th edition).

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CEO, you integrate the functional perspectives of your leadership team into one coherent strategic narrative. You ensure direction, priorities, and trade-offs are communicated with one leadership voice. Your primary alignment responsibility is preventing the “Chinese Whispers” effect by keeping the strategic story intact as it moves through the organisation.

When your alignment is absent or inconsistent, all other roles struggle to converge, and the leadership team loses its anchor point.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Shift · Mind (3S)

People need to know exactly what is changing.

Why your role is inclined to itYou set the direction for the whole organisation. A direction is broad, and it is easy to announce one without saying which practice changes, for which group, from which date. People then cannot picture the change, and each team fills the gap with its own version.

What sets it offThe change is announced as a direction, such as “we are becoming more customer-focused”. Nobody says which practice stops, which practice starts, for which group and from which date.

What you will see and hearPeople keep working the old way and call it by the new name. Three teams asked what is changing give three different answers.

What it costsPeople cannot follow a change they cannot picture. Each team acts on its own version, and effort goes in different directions.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CFO, you ensure capital allocation and financial discipline reinforce strategic intent and concentrate resources on the agreed priorities. Your alignment responsibility is making the budget reflect the stated strategy — and having the courage to make trade-offs visible when they arise.

When your alignment is absent, resources follow inertia, and the credibility of strategic priorities collapses.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Transparency · Habit (ACE-IT)

People need to see the reasoning, the progress and the constraints.

Why your role is inclined to itYou decide where the money goes, and every budget decision is a trade-off. A trade-off is uncomfortable to show, so the decision often reaches people without its reasons. People then read the cut as the real message about the change.

What sets it offA budget is cut, frozen or moved, and people are told the decision without the reason. Good results are shared quickly and setbacks are shared late.

What you will see and hearPeople say “Finance said no” and cannot explain why. Teams stop keeping their own figures up to date, because they never see what the figures are used for.

What it costsPeople treat the change as a cost exercise that will pass. They keep the new practice while it is checked and drop it afterwards.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As COO, you convert strategy into operational flow, ensuring execution routines, coordination, and hand-offs move the organisation forward. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring the operating system is configured for the current strategy.

When your alignment is absent, execution machinery runs efficiently but in the wrong direction.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Time · Hands (STAT)

People need the hours to do the new work.

Why your role is inclined to itYou turn the strategy into daily work. Each new practice looks small when it is added, and it is easy to add it to a day that is already full without taking any old work away. People then want the change and have no hours to do it.

What sets it offA new practice is added to the working day and nothing is taken away. The old targets and deadlines stay as they were.

What you will see and hearThe new practice is done at the end of the day, or dropped on a busy day. People say “we will get to it when things calm down.”

What it costsPeople who agree with the change still cannot do it. Their goodwill wears out, because they are asked for more than the day can hold.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CHRO, you align leadership behaviour, capability development, and organisational trust with the strategic direction. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring the human system has the capacity, engagement, and psychological safety to carry the strategy forward.

When your alignment is absent, strategy consistently exceeds the human system’s capacity to carry it.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Skill · Hands (STAT)

People need to know how to do the new work.

Why your role is inclined to itYou lead the building of capability. A briefing or a training session is easy to count as capability built. People have heard about the new work and have not yet practised it, so they make errors and go back to the old method.

What sets it offPeople attend a briefing or a training session and are then expected to perform. Nobody watches them do the new work and corrects them.

What you will see and hearErrors and rework rise. Everyone goes to the same one or two experts. People say “let me do it the old way to get it done.”

What it costsPeople lose confidence in themselves and in the change, and the old way starts to look safer.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CTO or CIO, you align technology architecture and data capability with the organisation’s strategic priorities and future operating model. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring technology investments actually change how the organisation works.

When your alignment is absent, technology investments produce demonstrations with no capability shift.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Tools · Hands (STAT)

People need the systems and resources the new work depends on.

Why your role is inclined to itYou supply the systems people work in every day. A new practice can be agreed long before the system, the form or the budget has changed to fit it. People then try the new way, find that the system still fits the old way, and build workarounds.

What sets it offThe new practice starts before the system, the form or the access rights have been changed to support it.

What you will see and hearPeople keep their own spreadsheets beside the system. Work waits on another team for access or data.

What it costsPeople do the work twice, once in the new way and once in the old. The extra effort turns willing people against the change.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CMO, you align market positioning and brand promise with the organisation’s operational ability to deliver. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring that demand created externally can be fulfilled internally — and that the brand promise is one the organisation can keep consistently.

When your alignment is absent, market signals are disconnected from strategic direction, and brand promises outpace operational reality.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Engagement · Habit (ACE-IT)

People need a say in how the change is going.

Why your role is inclined to itYou shape the message about the change. A strong message travels one way, from leaders to people, and it is easy to treat the message as the conversation. People then have nowhere to say how the change is going, and they stop owning it.

What sets it offThe change is launched as a campaign: a town hall, emails and posters. People are told about it and are never asked how it is going.

What you will see and hearMeetings about the change are presentations, and people ask nothing. Asked why the change matters, they cannot say it in their own words.

What it costsPeople stay spectators of the change, so it fades when the campaign ends.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CCO, you align revenue generation and customer growth strategies with enterprise positioning and operational capacity. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring all deals strengthen the organisation’s distinctive positioning — that all revenue is strategic revenue.

When your alignment is absent, growth strategies create internal incoherence that compounds over time.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Fairness · Heart (SCARF)

People need to see the change applied evenly.

Why your role is inclined to itYou set the targets and the rewards. People read every target and every reward as a signal of what is fair. When the change asks more of one team than of another, or the rewards still favour the old behaviour, people stop wanting it to succeed.

What sets it offThe change asks more of one team than of another, or the targets and rewards still pay for the old behaviour.

What you will see and hearPeople ask “why us and never them?” They talk about the change in the corridor and say nothing in the meeting.

What it costsPeople stop wanting the change to succeed. They do what is measured and hold back the rest.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CPO, you ensure procurement decisions strengthen operational capability and reinforce strategic priorities. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring cost efficiency decisions serve the customer’s end-game and the strategy’s priorities.

When your alignment is absent, procurement optimises for cost at the expense of strategic capability.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Authority · Hands (STAT)

People need permission to decide and act.

Why your role is inclined to itYou set the approval rules for spend. A change often moves a decision closer to the front line, and the approval rule stays where it was. People then ask permission for what the change has asked them to do, and the decision travels back up.

What sets it offThe change asks front-line people to decide for themselves, and the approval limits stay where they were. The first person who decides alone is overruled.

What you will see and hearRequests for approval rise. People ask “am I allowed to do this?” about the very thing the change asks of them.

What it costsDecisions travel back up, and the new way runs slower than the old one. People conclude that the change is talk.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CRO, you align enterprise risk interpretation so that strategic initiatives operate within a shared risk appetite framework. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring risk assessment enables strategic ambition — and that risk tolerance is consistently interpreted across all functions.

When your alignment is absent, risk appetite becomes function-by-function interpretation, creating asymmetric exposure.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Autonomy · Heart (SCARF)

People need some control over how they do their own work.

Why your role is inclined to itYou set the controls and the reporting. Each control is added for a sound reason, and each one takes some discretion from the people doing the work. As the controls add up, people feel they have lost control of their own work, and they comply without energy.

What sets it offNew controls, sign-offs or reports arrive with the change, and people are given no choice in how they meet them.

What you will see and hearPeople do exactly what the rule says and nothing more. Forms are completed and questions stop.

What it costsPeople comply and stop caring. The paperwork arrives, and the judgement and energy the change depends on are lost.

Converging Zone Contribution

What your role brings to the shared reading of the strategy

As CSO, you maintain the integrity of the strategic narrative as it moves from formulation into execution across the organisation. Your alignment responsibility is ensuring the strategy survives the transition from idea to implementation — that strategic intent does not mutate as it moves through the organisation.

When your alignment is absent, the strategic narrative fragments and different layers execute different versions of the strategy.

Alignment Hot Zone

Where your role is most likely to lose people

Stake · Mind (3S)

People need to know why the change matters to them.

Why your role is inclined to itYou hold the reasoning behind the strategy. That reasoning is usually stated as a benefit to the enterprise: growth, margin or position. A benefit that never reaches a team’s own work sounds like someone else’s reason, so people can describe the change and cannot say what it is for.

What sets it offThe reason for the change is given as a benefit to the organisation, such as growth or margin. Nobody says what the team gains, or what it loses by staying as it is.

What you will see and hearPeople say “because head office said so.” They can describe the change and cannot say what it is for.

What it costsPeople carry out the change while they are watched and set it aside under pressure. They have the instruction and are missing the reason.

Your group’s Shift, Stake and Step
Shift“What is changing?”

What it isThe Shift names the practice that ends and the practice that begins, for a named group, from a named date. It is concrete enough to be observed: someone watching the work could tell whether it is happening. A direction such as “more customer-focused” leaves every person to decide what it means, and each will decide differently.

Weak“We are becoming more customer-focused.”
Clear“From March, agents resolve complaints on the first call.”

Your moveState the Shift as from and to: what stops, what starts, for whom, from when.

Stake“What do we gain if we change, and what do we lose if we stay as we are?”

What it isThe Stake is why the change matters, and it has two sides. The gain says what the change is for. The loss says what standing still will cost. Both are needed, because people weigh a loss more heavily than an equal gain. A change described only by its benefits leaves the people affected holding the one visible loss: the practice they are asked to give up. Naming what is lost by staying put places a second loss on the scales. The Stake is stated in terms the affected group cares about. An enterprise benefit that never reaches their work reads as someone else’s reason.

Weak“It supports our strategy.”
Clear“We keep the customers who now leave after a two-day wait. If we stay as we are, we keep losing them.”

Your moveState the gain and the loss together, in the group’s own terms.

Step“What do I do differently?”

What it isThe Step is the person’s own part. It is said in the first person and is small enough to do next week. The Shift belongs to the organisation and the Step belongs to the person, so each role affected needs its own. A change with a clear Shift and no Step is understood as something that will happen to other people.

Weak“Embrace the new process.”
Clear“I decide on the call, within my limit.”

Your moveGive each affected role its own Step, and ask people to say it back.

The behaviours your group expects from the leadership team
Accountability

DefinitionTaking full ownership for results. Acknowledging missteps and driving corrective action.

What it does for a new practiceOne named person owns the practice and its result.

ObservableCommitments tracked • Reviews end with named owners • “We missed it, here’s the fix” language common

Commitment

DefinitionStaying anchored to agreed strategic intent even when pressure rises. Following through despite discomfort.

What it does for a new practiceThe practice is kept when pressure rises and the old way is quicker.

ObservableStrategic priorities stay consistent • Leaders decline non-strategic work • Promises kept visibly

Engagement

DefinitionActively involving self and others in sense-making, dialogue, and execution. Listening to understand.

What it does for a new practicePeople talk about how it is going and improve it together.

ObservableTeam meetings are two-way • Feedback loops visible • People quote the shared “why”

Integrity

DefinitionActing consistently with values, facts, and truth — even when inconvenient. Declaring conflicts of interest early.

What it does for a new practiceSlips are reported as they are, and early.

ObservableEvidence cited before decisions • Values referenced in hard choices • Teams trust word matches deed

Transparency

DefinitionMaking reasoning, progress, and constraints visible to all stakeholders. Sharing challenges openly.

What it does for a new practiceIts results are visible to everyone the change affects.

ObservableDashboards updated regularly • Decisions logged and accessible • “No surprises” culture evident
