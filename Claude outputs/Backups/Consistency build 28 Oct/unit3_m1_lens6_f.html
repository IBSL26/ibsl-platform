Unit 7 — Session Overview

Use this tab to orient yourself before each section. It contains purpose, tone, key facilitation questions, and watch points for the full unit.

◆ Unit Intent

Unit 7 addresses the single most common reason strategy fails at the human level: expectations are communicated but never truly established. The distinction is subtle in language and profound in execution impact. The unit moves leaders from announcing targets to securing genuine ownership. This unit equips leaders to close the gap between a well-designed performance management system and genuine human commitment to deliver it.

It builds on the S2R® Contribution Map™ and FACES of Unit 6. The expectation conversation covers both performance views: the human commitments people make, and the organisational conditions the organisation provides.

◆ Key Facilitation Questions
  • “If you distributed your team’s scorecard right now — without conversation, without context — how many of them would genuinely own those targets?”
  • “Think of the last time a delivery goal was missed. Was the expectation set — or was it established?”
  • “Who in your expectations chain could derail delivery — and have you had the alignment conversation with them?”
  • “When someone on your team gives you a 6 out of 10 on alignment — what does your instinct tell you to do? And what should you do?”
◆ Tone & Watch Points

Watch for: leaders who confuse clarity with alignment. A person can understand an expectation perfectly and still not own it. Probe: “Clarity tells you they heard it. Alignment tells you they’ll deliver it. How do you know the difference?”

Watch for: leaders who treat the Alignment Score as a report card. The score is a diagnostic. If someone gives a 6, the leader’s job is to understand it and resolve it.

Application: Each group applies the four steps to two contributions from its S2R® Contribution Map™, for the people and for the organisation, then prepares the alignment dialogue with its board chair. The closer the group stays to its real strategy, the more transferable the skill.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

What is Establishing Performance Expectations?

Naming the distinction between setting and establishing expectations — and the two mechanisms that close the gap between a delivery goal and genuine ownership.

Section learning outcomes
  • Facilitate expectation-setting conversations that produce genuine ownership.
  • Negotiate explicit human commitments and organisational enabling conditions.
1.1 — Setting vs Establishing Expectations
The defining distinction

The performance management and accountability frameworks and structures have been established. The delivery goals have been defined. The targets have been set.

Unit 6 has already translated enterprise outcomes into functional contributions, team and individual commitments, and the organisational conditions: the Environment, Systems, Resources and Governance (ESRG). Unit 7 establishes agreement around that design. An assigned KPI or OKR is a proposed expectation until the contributor and relevant enabling owners have confirmed what delivery requires.

People performance
Human expectations
Describe the result, contribution, standard, timing and behaviours for which people will take responsibility.
Organisational performance
Organisational expectations
Specify the Environment, Systems, Resources and Governance that the organisation will provide or resolve.

Both must be explicitly agreed.

The difference between setting expectations and establishing expectations is subtle in language and profound in execution impact. It determines whether a performance target becomes an instruction that people comply with or a commitment that people own.

Execution begins when the people responsible for those goals internalise the expectation and accept ownership of the result.

Dimension Setting Expectations Establishing Expectations

FocusClarity of instructionMutual readiness for execution

ResultKey performance indicator (KPI) as metric to be monitoredKPI as personal leadership promise
Enabling conditionsAssumed to be in placeAgreed with the owners of the Environment, Systems, Resources and Governance

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Open with the script question: “If you distributed your team’s scorecard right now — without conversation, without context — how many of them would genuinely own those targets?” Let the silence sit. Most leaders will realise their answer is “fewer than I thought.” That discomfort is the entry point for the rest of the unit.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the last time they communicated a delivery expectation, whether it was set or established, and how they know.

1.2 — The Three Conditions of Established Expectations
Clarity · Capability · Commitment

Establishing performance expectations is a leadership process through which strategic contributions become understood, achievable and consciously owned.

In Unit 6, the S2R® Contribution Map™ established how enterprise results depend on the contributions of business functions, teams and individuals. It also identified the organisational conditions required to support those contributions.

The next responsibility is to ensure that these expectations are genuinely established with everyone involved in delivering the results.

Three conditions must be addressed: Clarity, Capability and Commitment. Together, they establish accountability for people performance and organisational performance:

People performance
What individuals and teams must deliver, how they must perform and the behaviours expected of them.
Organisational performance
The environment, systems, resources and governance arrangements that must support delivery.

Each condition answers a fundamental leadership question.

Condition 1
Clarity — Does everyone understand what is expected, why it matters and what successful delivery requires?
▼

Clarity establishes a shared understanding of the expected contribution.

A performance expectation must connect directly to the enterprise result it supports. Individuals and teams need to understand the contribution they are responsible for, the standard of performance required and how success will be demonstrated.

Clarity must also extend to the organisation’s responsibilities. Leaders need to establish what support, decisions and enabling conditions must be provided for the contribution to be delivered.

Clarity in people performance

The leader and individual establish a shared understanding of:

  1. Strategic contribution: Which enterprise objective or result does this work support?
  2. Performance expectations: What KPI, OKR or other agreed deliverable defines success?
  3. Performance standard: What level of quality, quantity, timeliness or consistency is required?
  4. Behavioural expectations: What observable behaviours and ways of working are necessary?
  5. Evidence: How will successful performance be demonstrated and assessed?

Clarity in organisational performance

The leader establishes what the organisation must provide:

  • Environment: What facilities, infrastructure and technology must support delivery?
  • Systems: Which processes must be standardised, integrated across functions and efficient enough to deliver?
  • Resources: What assets and funding must be available, and how must they be deployed?
  • Governance: What policies and procedures, decision authority and risk management must be established?

Clarity is established when the parties share the same understanding of both the expected contribution and the conditions required to deliver it.

Test: Ask someone on your team to describe what success looks like for their key result. If the description differs from yours, clarity has not yet been established.
Condition 2
Capability — Can the people and the organisation realistically deliver what is expected?
▼

Capability establishes whether the expectation is achievable under the conditions in which performance must occur.

An individual may understand an expectation perfectly and be willing to deliver it, yet lack the skills, capacity, resources or organisational support required. This makes capability a shared leadership responsibility.

Capability in people performance

The leader assesses whether the individual or team has:

  1. Competence: The knowledge and skills required to perform the work.
  2. Capacity: Sufficient time and workload capacity to meet the expectation.
  3. Readiness: The confidence, experience and practical preparation needed to begin.
  4. Coordination: Access to the colleagues and teams whose contributions affect the result.

Where gaps exist, the leader and individual agree on appropriate development, support or adjustments.

Capability in organisational performance

The leader examines whether the organisation has established the conditions necessary for delivery.

Organisational conditionCapability question
EnvironmentAre the facilities, infrastructure and technology fit for the required performance?
SystemsAre the processes standardised, integrated and efficient enough to support consistent delivery?
ResourcesAre the required assets and funding available and productively deployed?
GovernanceAre the policies and procedures, decision authority and risk management clear and responsive?

An identified gap must lead to an agreed response. For every significant constraint, leaders establish:

  • What must be resolved.
  • Who is accountable for resolving it.
  • When the resolution is required.
  • What interim arrangements will apply if the constraint remains unresolved.

Capability is established when the required human abilities and organisational conditions have been confirmed, or when identified gaps have accountable resolution arrangements.

Condition 3
Commitment — Have the people responsible consciously accepted their respective responsibilities for delivering and enabling the result?
▼

Commitment is the point at which a performance expectation becomes an agreed undertaking.

Understanding an expectation does not by itself create ownership. Similarly, confirming that delivery is possible does not guarantee that the people involved have accepted responsibility for making it happen. Commitment must therefore be established through dialogue.

Commitment in people performance

The individual consciously accepts responsibility for:

  1. Delivering the agreed contribution and performance standard.
  2. Demonstrating the expected behaviours.
  3. Monitoring progress and providing reliable performance evidence.
  4. Raising risks and constraints early.
  5. Taking corrective action within their authority.

The leader must create space for the individual to question assumptions, discuss concerns and confirm what they can realistically undertake.

Commitment in organisational performance

The leaders responsible for enabling delivery accept responsibility for:

  • Providing agreed resources and support.
  • Resolving systems and coordination constraints.
  • Making decisions within agreed timeframes.
  • Removing organisational barriers that threaten delivery.
  • Responding when emerging conditions require intervention.

This creates mutual accountability: individuals own their performance commitments, while organisational leaders own the conditions they have undertaken to provide.

Commitment is established when the relevant parties have consciously accepted their respective responsibilities, including any agreed actions required to enable delivery.

The Alignment Dialogue asks: “On a scale of 1–10, how aligned are you with the expectation for you to deliver this outcome?” A score below 9 means Commitment is not yet established.

Key principle: Performance expectations are established when people understand their contribution, have a credible basis for delivering it and consciously accept responsibility, supported by leaders who are equally accountable for the organisational conditions required for success.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Most leaders overestimate how much Clarity they have provided and underestimate how little Commitment they have secured. The conversation usually sounds complete to the leader and feels incomplete to the team member. Ask: “After your last expectations conversation, which of the three conditions did you explicitly verify? Clarity — probably. Capability — sometimes. Commitment — almost never.”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: which of the three conditions they most consistently confirm and which they most often assume.

1.3 — The Expectations Chain
Mapping who else influences the outcome

Very few delivery goals are achieved by a single person acting alone. Most results depend on a network of decisions, resources, and coordination across the organisation. The Expectations Chain identifies everyone whose work directly affects the delivery of a Key Result or KPI.

Mapping the Expectations Chain is a critical step before establishing expectations with any individual. It ensures that the KPI is not treated as an isolated personal obligation — and surfaces the dependencies that will determine whether delivery is possible.

Central question: With whom must performance expectations be established for each contribution to succeed?

The Expectations Chain uses the completed Contribution Map to identify the people with whom expectations must be explicitly established. It examines three directions:

The Critical Distinction

Contribution Map
Customer Service requires IT to maintain a reliable case-management system to achieve its response-time target.

This establishes a dependency.

Expectations Chain
The Customer Service Executive and IT Executive must establish expectations concerning system availability, incident response times, escalation arrangements and accountability for resolving failures.

This establishes what must be agreed between the people responsible.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask participants to pick one current Key Result and post in the chat one contributor from each direction: Upline, Downline and Crossline. Then ask: “Have you had an alignment dialogue with everyone in that chain? Or only with the person holding the KPI?” Most will realise they have had the conversation with one person and assumed the rest of the chain would follow. That assumption is where most execution gaps begin.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: their Expectations Chain for one current Key Result, and the contributors with whom they have not yet had an alignment dialogue.

1.4 — The Alignment Dialogue
Testing whether the expectation is owned

With the Expectations Chain mapped, the leader conducts an Alignment Dialogue with the individuals responsible for delivering the Key Result or KPI. This is a structured conversation designed to test whether the expectation has moved from instruction to commitment.

The Alignment Dialogue tests two questions together: “What contribution will you deliver?” and “What must the organisation enable for you to deliver it?” The leader asks the individual to explain the target in their own words, describe the behaviours and evidence, identify constraints in the organisational conditions and dependencies, and confirm what they can commit to.

Where alignment is below the agreed threshold, the leader explores the reason, separating a clarity gap, a skill or commitment gap, and a constraint in the organisational conditions or across functions. The leader agrees a specific resolution owner and review date before recording the expectation as established.

The Alignment Dialogue uses a simple scale of 1–10. A score of 9 or 10 indicates genuine ownership of delivery. Anything lower demands further deliberation through the Score → Evaluate → Resolve sequence.

The power of the Alignment Dialogue lies in what the number reveals. A score of 6 is data: there is something unresolved between the expectation and the person’s readiness to own it. The leader’s job is to find it and resolve it before execution begins.

Score → Evaluate → Resolve

When the alignment score is below 9, the leader treats the number as data — it signals that something is unresolved. The Score → Evaluate → Resolve sequence transforms that signal into a resolved commitment.

1. Score It
2. Evaluate It
3. Resolve It

The individual provides a numerical assessment of their level of alignment. The score acts as a signal of readiness for ownership.

Score What it signals Leader action
9–10Clear ownership and readiness to deliverProceed; confirm the commitment and document
7–8Partial alignment — some uncertainty remainsMove to Evaluate → surface what is unresolved
5–6Significant concerns affecting confidence in deliveryMove to Evaluate → expect multiple concerns to surface
Below 5Expectation is not yet viable in its current formDo not proceed — the expectation must be redesigned

If the score is below 9, the leader explores the reasons behind the rating. The objective is to understand what is preventing full alignment.

The leader guides the conversation using diagnostic questions:

  • “What influenced the score you gave?”
  • “What feels unclear about the expectation?”
  • “What concerns do you have about delivering this outcome?”
  • “Where do you anticipate obstacles or constraints?”

This step surfaces issues that might otherwise only appear during execution — when they are far more costly to resolve. Common concerns include:

  • Unclear definition of the outcome or success criteria
  • Cross-functional dependencies not yet confirmed
  • Capacity or resource constraints affecting feasibility
  • Confidence in the delivery pathway or timeline

The purpose of Evaluate is to make hidden concerns visible before execution begins.

Once the sources of misalignment are understood, the leader works with the individual to resolve them.

The guiding coaching question becomes:

“What would help move your alignment from where it is now to a 9 or 10?”

This question shifts the conversation towards solutions. Resolution may involve:

  • Clarifying the outcome definition or success criteria
  • Adjusting priorities or realistic timelines
  • Identifying additional support or resources required
  • Confirming collaboration across the Expectations Chain

Once concerns are resolved, the leader asks the alignment question again to confirm the expectation has moved into clear ownership. If it has not, the conversation continues — or the expectation itself is redesigned.

Leader’s Reflection Capture (after the dialogue):
1. Alignment Rating — Where does the commitment currently stand?
2. Primary Concern Identified — What surfaced during the discussion?
3. Action for Elevation — What will move alignment upward?

The GROW Coaching Model

GROW is the coaching model the leader uses when holding the Alignment Dialogue. It is a widely used coaching model, popularised by Sir John Whitmore in Coaching for Performance. Its four stages are Goal, Reality, Options and Will. At each stage the leader asks questions and the individual does the thinking, so the answers the individual reaches are their own.

Score → Evaluate → Resolve is the sequence of the dialogue. GROW is how the leader coaches inside it.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Introduce the Alignment Dialogue as a leadership safety system. “You are asking for the score so you can help. A 9 or 10 means go. Anything below 9 means there is still work for you to do as the leader.”

The Resolve question, “What would help move your alignment from where it is now to a 9 or 10?”, is the most important question in the whole architecture. It keeps the leader in the right posture: solving for the person’s ability to own it. Teach GROW as the way the leader holds that posture through the whole dialogue. Participants practise it in the role play of part 4.1.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the Resolve question in their own words, applied to a real expectation they are currently facing.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why Establishing Expectations Determines Execution Outcomes

Understanding the behavioural mechanisms through which expectation alignment drives ownership, reduces ambiguity, surfaces constraints, and transforms accountability from formal obligation to personal commitment.

Section learning outcomes
  • Maintain commitment by revisiting and realigning expectations during execution.
  • Diagnose unclear expectations and correct the conditions that undermine delivery.
2.1 — Compliance vs Commitment: The Execution Dynamic
Ignition point

Strategy is ultimately delivered through human commitment. The moment when expectations are clarified between leader and contributor is the moment a performance target transitions from organisational intention to personal responsibility. If that transition does not occur, execution becomes fragile regardless of how well the system has been designed.

A compliance response may emerge when people are held accountable for results while essential organisational conditions remain outside their control. Commitment strengthens when leaders visibly fulfil their side of the performance agreement: decisions, systems, resources, governance and working environment.

Dimension Compliance-Driven Commitment-Driven
How it looksExpectations communicated as instructions; acknowledged but not ownedExpectations established through dialogue; owned
Behaviour patternWait for direction; escalate problems upward; focus on reportingAnticipate risks earlier; coordinate across functions actively; invest effort in solving obstacles
When obstacles ariseRemain cautious; protect reporting; defer resolution to leadershipTake initiative; surface issues early; co-design solutions with the leader
AccountabilityActivity ownership: responsible for completing tasksOutcome ownership: personally accountable for the final result
EnergyReserved; discretionary effort withheldFully invested; discretionary effort freely given
How it sounds“I completed everything that was asked of me.”“I’ll make sure this gets delivered.”
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Invite leaders to locate their current team: “If you are honest — when your team hits an obstacle, what happens first? Do they solve it, or do they escalate it? The answer tells you whether you have compliance or commitment.” This is a signal about whether expectations were established.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: what happens first when their team hits an unexpected obstacle, and what that says about the execution dynamic they have created.

2.2 — Five Mechanisms: Why Expectation Alignment Determines Outcomes
The behavioural logic
Mechanism 1
From Instruction to Ownership
▼

When expectations are merely communicated, people interpret them as instructions. The individual understands what must be achieved but does not yet feel personally accountable for ensuring the outcome is delivered. Establishing expectations through structured dialogue moves the individual from acknowledging the requirement to accepting ownership of the result. This is the fundamental shift that makes execution commitment-driven.

Mechanism 2
Reducing Interpretation Ambiguity
▼

Even when strategic objectives are clearly articulated, individuals often interpret what success requires differently depending on their role, expertise, and operational context. Without structured expectation alignment, people may pursue the same goal while working from different assumptions. Establishing expectations creates a shared interpretation of: what must be delivered, why the result matters, and how success will be recognised. This reduces confusion and allows teams to move forward with greater coordination.

Mechanism 3
Surfacing Constraints Before Execution Begins
▼

Most organisational results require coordination across functions, access to resources, timely decision-making, and cooperation from multiple contributors. If these dependencies are not identified during the expectation conversation, they only become visible later — often when deadlines are already approaching. Establishing expectations allows leaders and contributors to identify these factors early, ensuring required resources are understood, cross-functional dependencies are visible, and potential risks are recognised before they become execution crises.

Mechanism 4
Strengthening Accountability
▼

Accountability is strongest when individuals perceive that the expectations placed on them are clear, achievable, and meaningful. When these conditions are present, people are far more likely to take ownership of outcomes and sustain effort when challenges arise. Establishing expectations strengthens accountability by ensuring both the leader and contributor share a common understanding of: the responsibility involved, what support will be available, what constraints must be addressed, and what standards will be used to evaluate success. This mutual clarity transforms accountability from a formal obligation into a shared commitment.

Mechanism 5
Building the Behavioural Foundation
▼

Strategy execution is fundamentally a behavioural challenge. Plans, metrics, and dashboards provide direction and visibility, but outcomes are ultimately produced through the daily decisions and actions of people. Establishing performance expectations ensures that individuals are psychologically prepared to take responsibility for doing it. When this foundation is in place: individuals understand the purpose behind the work, confidence in feasibility increases, collaboration improves, and accountability becomes proactive.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask participants to identify which mechanism is most absent in their current execution environment. “Is your team unclear on what success looks like (Mechanism 2)? Are constraints surfacing too late (Mechanism 3)? Or is accountability diffuse — everyone feels responsible and no one is (Mechanism 4)?” The diagnosis shapes the intervention.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the mechanism most absent in their current execution environment, and the cost of that absence.

2.3 — Engagement & Accountability: The Two Casualties
What breaks down when expectations are only set

When expectations are set without being established, two organisational conditions degrade in a predictable sequence.

Two failures often appear together: contributors disengage because promises of support are not honoured, and accountability becomes defensive because responsibility for the result is disconnected from authority over its enabling conditions. The expectation record must therefore distinguish the individual who delivers the result from the leader who resolves a constraint in the organisational conditions.

First Casualty
Engagement Weakens — Observable but Subtle
▼

When leaders communicate expectations without alignment dialogue, individuals may understand what is required but do not feel personally involved in shaping how the outcome will be delivered. The expectation is acknowledged cognitively but not internalised behaviourally. Work continues and progress may still be visible, but the energy becomes uneven.

  • Discretionary effort is limited — people do only what is assigned
  • Individuals wait for direction and leave emerging problems unaddressed
  • Cross-functional coordination slows because people focus primarily on their own responsibilities
  • Engagement becomes transactional — people perform their roles but the deeper sense of collective ownership does not materialise
The signal: work is happening, but energy is not invested. People are present but not propelled.
Critical Long-Term Impact
Accountability Dilutes — The Deeper Organisational Cost
▼

The most significant long-term consequence is in the quality of accountability. When expectations are set without alignment, individuals may feel responsible for carrying out activities but not for the final organisational result. Activity ownership and outcome ownership become blurred.

  • Performance discussions revolve around explanations
  • Conversations focus on constraints: resources, dependencies, external conditions
  • Cross-functional dependencies create delays because responsibility for resolving them is unclear
  • Accountability becomes dispersed: multiple teams feel partially responsible, none fully accountable
The paradox: teams appear to be working hard, meetings are frequent, updates are detailed — yet the organisation consistently struggles to achieve what it set out to deliver. The issue is that ownership of the outcome was assigned without being secured.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

The paradox in the Accountability card is often a moment of recognition for leaders. Name it explicitly: “Have you ever been in a review where everyone has a reason, and no one has an answer? That is diluted accountability in action. It happened because ownership was never established.”

4.1 — Practising the Alignment Dialogue
GROW coaching model · Portfolio Work

Part 1.4 taught the Alignment Dialogue and the GROW coaching model. In this part participants practise them: in class, through a role play that you run, and then on the portal, as Portfolio work.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Role Play in Class · The Alignment Dialogue

Participants practise the Alignment Dialogue with the GROW model, both taught in part 1.4. You run this role play in the live session, between any two roles that you choose. Nothing is entered on the portal during the role play.

Decide three things when you prepare:

  1. The expectation. One Key Result. Use the example “Reduce complaint resolution time from 48 hours to 12 hours”, or a Key Result from a group’s Unit 3 Enterprise OKRs.
  2. The two roles for each round. One is the leader who establishes the expectation. The other is the owner who must deliver it. Any two roles work: CEO and COO, COO and Head of Customer Service, CFO and Head of Procurement, or an executive and the board chair.
  3. The owner brief for each round. It gives the owner’s score, the concerns behind it and what moves the owner to a 9 or 10. Two briefs for this example are below. Write your own in the same pattern for the roles you choose.

Run it in this order:

  1. Set it up. Say: “We will now practise the Alignment Dialogue on one expectation. Listen for the four GROW stages.” State the expectation and name the two roles.
  2. Give the observers their task. Everyone who is not in a role posts in the chat each GROW stage as they hear it (G, R, O or W) and the question that opened it.
  3. Round 1 · You demonstrate. You play the leader. A volunteer plays the owner. Send the volunteer the owner brief in a private chat message. Connect the expectation to the Future in one sentence, state the outcome, then ask the alignment question. Work through Score → Evaluate → Resolve with the GROW questions, and end by asking the alignment question again.
  4. Pause. Ask the observers: “Which question moved the score?” Read two answers from the chat.
  5. Round 2 · Participants lead. One volunteer plays the leader and a second volunteer plays the owner, in the roles you chose. Send the owner brief to the owner in a private chat message. Run further rounds with other roles as you choose.
  6. Debrief. Ask: “What did the leader do when the score was low?” “Which concern would have stayed hidden without ‘What else?’” “What felt unfamiliar about asking for a score?”
  7. Point to the Portfolio work. Say: “On the portal, in part 4.1, you prepare an Alignment Dialogue with your board chair on one of your own Key Results.”
Owner brief · example AYou give a 7. The outcome is clear to you. Your concern is that the billing team must answer customer queries on the same day, and nobody has agreed this with them. You move to a 9 when the leader agrees to establish that expectation with the Head of Billing.
Owner brief · example BYou give a 5. You have two concerns. First, you do not know how “resolved” will be measured. Second, your team has three vacancies. Give only the first concern until the leader asks “What else?” You move to a 9 when success is defined and the two of you have agreed how the vacancies will be covered.

Watch for:

  • The leader defends the target or argues with the score. Stop the round and ask the leader to return to “What influenced the score you gave?”
  • The leader offers solutions before asking for the owner’s options. Ask: “Whose idea was that?”
  • The dialogue ends without the alignment question being asked again. Ask: “How do you know where the owner stands now?”

The scenario participants work on. The board chair has disagreed with the current metric and timeframe of the Key Result the participant has chosen. The participant asks the alignment question: “On a scale of 1–10, how aligned are you with this Key Result as it stands?” The chair gives a 5. The participant’s aim is to move the chair to a 10.

What each participant completes on the portal
  • One drop-down list: the Key Result for which the participant may need an alignment dialogue with the board chair.
  • Four boxes, one for each GROW stage: Goal · Reality · Options · Will. The lesson from part 1.4 sits above each box.
◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Each participant completes part 4.1 of their own file alone, as Portfolio work:

  1. They choose from a drop-down list the Key Result for which they may need an alignment dialogue with their board chair. The list holds the Key Results their group confirmed in Unit 3.
  2. They read the scenario: the board chair has disagreed with the current metric and timeframe of that Key Result and gives a 5.
  3. They write their side of the dialogue under each GROW stage (Goal, Reality, Options, Will), with the questions they ask in their own words.
  4. Under Will they state what they and the chair agree to do and how they confirm that the chair has reached a 10.

Unit Summary
Unit 7 synthesis
Awareness — What

Setting expectations communicates the required result. Establishing expectations ensures the individual understands it, can deliver it, and consciously accepts ownership of it. The Expectations Chain and Alignment Dialogue are the mechanisms that close this gap.

Intelligence — Why

Expectation alignment determines execution outcomes through five mechanisms: shifting instruction to ownership, reducing ambiguity, surfacing constraints early, strengthening accountability, and building the behavioural foundation of execution.

Extrapolating — Where

Every CXO function has a characteristic tendency to set expectations without establishing them. Without alignment dialogue, the organisation may appear aligned at the surface while engagement weakens and accountability dilutes beneath it.

Integration — Collective

The Expectation Architecture (Start with the Future → Clarify → Identify Chain → Confirm Ownership) and the Alignment Dialogue (Score → Evaluate → Resolve) create a shared leadership discipline that transforms expectation-setting into a commitment practice. Participants practise the dialogue with the GROW model (Goal, Reality, Options, Will).

Application — In Practice

Each group establishes delivery expectations for two contributions from its Contribution Map, taking each through the four steps for the people and for the organisation, and prepares the alignment dialogue with its board chair. The agreed record feeds the team’s Capstone S2R® Blueprint.

“Strong performance cultures are built by ensuring that expectations are mutually understood, realistically supported, and consciously accepted by the people responsible for delivering them.”

◆ Facilitator Closing Note

Close by asking each leader to name one expectation they have set but not yet established — with a specific person in their expectations chain. Ask them to capture the score, the primary concern, and the action for elevation when they hold that alignment dialogue.
