Module 3 · Influence · Unit 8

Performance Measurement

Influence · Pillar 2 of DIG

Influence mobilises the people and performance systems through which strategy is carried into execution. It considers how leaders build shared understanding and commitment, establish and measure performance, and clear the blockages that slow delivery.

The Influence learning journey progresses from Unit 5 — Aligning Heart & Mind (people alignment), to Unit 6 — Performance Management Setup (performance architecture), Unit 7 — Establishing Performance Expectations (owned commitments), Unit 8 — Performance Measurement (review discipline), and then to Unit 9 — Strategic Unclogging (flow of execution).

Module learning outcomes Build shared strategic understanding and commitment that carry strategy from intention into coordinated action. Establish performance arrangements that connect enterprise results to owned individual contributions and enabling organisational conditions. Sustain execution momentum by interpreting performance evidence and acting on it with balanced executive judgement. Restore the flow of strategy execution by resolving the people and organisational constraints that impede delivery.
Key learning outcomes Interpret performance evidence in relation to strategic objectives and organisational context. Discriminate meaningful strategic progress from activity, compliance and temporary variation. Exercise balanced executive judgement when performance diverges from expectations. Translate performance evidence into decisions, learning and corrective leadership action.

Unit 8 — Session Overview

Use this tab to orient yourself before each section. It contains the unit intent, key facilitation questions, tone guidance, and watch points for the full session.

◆ Unit Intent

Unit 8 closes the loop opened by Units 6 and 7. Key performance indicators (KPIs) have been established, targets have been set, and expectations have been secured. Now the most important discipline of execution begins: the performance review conversation. This unit makes that conversation structured, honest, and useful: a genuine dialogue about execution reality.

The centrepiece is the Review–Realign–Restore Cycle. Leaders must leave this unit understanding that measurement is an execution recovery system. The Accountability Loop role play in part 4.2 is where the conceptual work becomes a leadership skill, and Section 5 applies the four consequences of weak review discipline to the group’s own strategy.

The Integration section raises the focus to enterprise level through the Enterprise Performance Intelligence Circle (EPIC), shifting leaders from functional reporting to shared execution intelligence.

◆ Tone & Watch Points

Watch for: leaders who conflate reporting with reviewing. Reporting describes what happened. Reviewing examines why, and determines what must change. Probe: “If your dashboard went dark for 60 days and you only had conversations to guide you — how confident would you be about execution progress?”

Watch for: leaders who treat the Accountability Loop as a confrontation tool. Its purpose is diagnosis and restored momentum. If the root cause conversation feels uncomfortable, that discomfort is a signal that the culture is still treating performance gaps as verdicts.

Teach EPIC horizontally: each signal is read for what it means for the other functions. The Enterprise Performance Intelligence Circle exists to surface cross-functional dependencies.

1.1 — The Execution Visibility Infrastructure
Are we actually moving closer to our Success in Practice?

The performance management system has been set and mechanisms for establishing performance are in place. Commitments have been secured. Now the most important discipline of execution begins.

Strategy execution fails at the level of visibility. Performance measurement is the discipline that makes execution visible. It is the execution visibility infrastructure of the strategy: it shows whether the organisation is actually moving closer to its Success in Practice.

The pattern in many organisations

In many organisations, enormous effort goes into designing strategy. Leaders invest months clarifying direction, defining Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), setting targets, and aligning teams around ambitious outcomes. Scorecards are built. Key performance indicators (KPIs) are cascaded. Performance frameworks are communicated.

Yet several months later, when leadership teams pause and reflect, the same uncertainty often appears. Work is happening. Teams are busy. Initiatives are underway. Leaders still cannot confidently answer a more fundamental question: “Is the work being done actually advancing the strategy?” This is the gap that performance measurement exists to close.

The visibility infrastructure

Performance measurement creates the visibility infrastructure that allows leaders to observe whether the organisation is genuinely progressing towards its strategic outcomes. Without this infrastructure, strategy remains largely interpretive. Each function develops its own sense of progress. Activity becomes mistaken for advancement. Energy is expended, but the connection to the enterprise result becomes difficult to see.

Performance measurement restores that line of sight. It allows the organisation to move from activity visibility to progress visibility.

People performance

Leaders see whether individuals and teams are moving their KPIs, OKRs and behaviours towards the enterprise result.

Organisational performance

Leaders see whether the organisational conditions support the execution of the individuals’ tasks. These conditions are the Environment, Systems, Resources and Governance (ESRG) agreed in Unit 6.

From activity to progress

Most organisations are extremely good at measuring activity: how many projects were launched, how many meetings were held, how many deliverables were completed. These indicators provide evidence that work is occurring and create a sense of operational movement. But activity alone does not indicate whether the organisation is actually moving closer to the strategic outcome it set out to achieve.

Performance measurement therefore asks a different set of questions. The question moves from "What work was done?" to "What changed because of the work?" The organisation examines evidence of progress. This shift transforms performance management from an administrative reporting exercise into an execution intelligence system.

People performance

The question moves from “How busy are our people?” to “What has their work changed in the Key Result?”

Organisational performance

The question moves from “Has the support been put in place?” to “Does the support enable the individuals’ tasks to move the Key Result?”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: what they measure today that reflects activity, what would reflect genuine progress towards their strategic outcome, and what evidence they hold on whether the organisational conditions support the tasks behind that progress.

1.2 — The Four Strategic Purposes of Performance Measurement
Click each to explore

Performance measurement creates execution rhythm. Most strategic outcomes are the result of cumulative progress built over time. Regular review cycles allow leaders to notice the subtle signals that indicate whether execution is strengthening or weakening. Momentum may begin to accelerate in one area while slowing in another. Resource constraints may appear. Decision bottlenecks may surface. Capability gaps may become visible. When these signals are visible early, leaders can respond while the situation is still manageable.

This rhythm prevents the most common leadership surprise in organisations: discovering performance failure only at the end of the reporting period. Performance measurement ensures that strategy is observed while it is unfolding, as well as evaluated after the fact.

At its core, performance measurement serves four strategic purposes within the organisation. Select each to explore.

Purpose 01
Creates Visibility
▼

Leaders can observe where the organisation is gaining momentum and where it is stalling. Strategic priorities that once existed only as intentions begin to appear as measurable progress signals. Without measurement, leaders are flying blind: confident in effort but uncertain about direction.

People performance

Where individuals’ and teams’ KPIs are gaining momentum and where they are stalling.

Organisational performance

Where the organisational conditions are supporting the execution of the tasks, and where they are holding it back.

Facilitator prompt: “Where in your organisation does execution happen in the dark? Where are you most likely to be surprised at the end of a reporting period?”
Purpose 02
Establishes Accountability
▼

Strategic completion requires tracking every component of the declared goal, including the parts that bring no visible victory. What has been accomplished is recorded as evidence that commitments are being kept. Leaders and teams understand which part of the strategy they are responsible for advancing.

Accountability without measurement is aspiration without a feedback loop: it depends entirely on personal commitment, with no organisational structure behind it.

People performance

Individuals and teams know which part of the strategy they are responsible for advancing, and their delivery is recorded as evidence.

Organisational performance

Leaders account for whether the organisational conditions they undertook to provide in Unit 7 support the execution of the individuals’ tasks.

Facilitator prompt: “If your KPIs disappeared from your scorecard but the targets remained unchanged, how many would still be actively owned?”
Purpose 03
Execution Intelligence
▼

At this point, performance measurement begins to evolve beyond data collection. It becomes execution intelligence. Data on its own rarely improves performance. Numbers simply describe what has happened. Insight emerges only when those numbers are interpreted through disciplined conversation. Performance measurement therefore transforms data into decision insight.

Each review cycle helps leaders answer three essential questions:

  1. What progress has been achieved since the last review?
  2. What obstacles are preventing further progress?
  3. What adjustment will restore momentum towards the target?

Through these conversations, performance management becomes a learning system. Teams develop a deeper understanding of how strategy unfolds in practice. Leaders strengthen their ability to guide execution through uncertainty and complexity.

People performance

The review learns what accelerates and what slows the progress of individuals and teams.

Organisational performance

The review learns which organisational conditions enable execution and which constrain it.

Facilitator prompt: “What did your organisation actually learn from its last strategy cycle? What did it learn about how execution works inside your system?”
Purpose 04
Enables Timely Correction
▼

Early signals allow teams to intervene while progress is still recoverable. Problems are addressed when they are small enough to resolve. Without early signals, problems are discovered only when targets have already been missed.

This purpose is why measurement frequency matters: a signal detected in week four carries a different cost from the same signal detected in week twelve.

People performance

An early signal in an individual’s progress lets the leader intervene while the KPI is still recoverable.

Organisational performance

An early signal in an organisational condition, such as a system outage, a capacity shortfall or a delayed decision, lets the leader responsible act before the individuals’ delivery is affected.

Facilitator prompt: “What is the average gap in your organisation between when a performance problem begins and when leadership first becomes aware of it? What does that gap cost?”
1.3 — From Performance Measurement to Execution Recovery
The Review–Realign–Restore Cycle (R3)

Part 1.2 established that performance measurement serves four strategic purposes: creating visibility, establishing accountability, generating execution intelligence and enabling timely correction. These four purposes work together to help leaders understand how strategy is progressing and where intervention is required.

But identifying a performance gap does not by itself resolve it. Leaders need a disciplined process for interpreting what the evidence reveals, identifying what needs attention and ensuring that corrective decisions translate into improved execution.

Within the S2R® system, this process is called the Review–Realign–Restore Cycle (R3). The cycle provides a recurring leadership rhythm through which performance evidence is examined, execution gaps are addressed and progress is restored.

The cycle applies to people performance and organisational performance.

Stage 1
Review
Stage 2
Realign
Stage 3
Restore
Stage 1 — Review: Establish Execution Reality

Review brings together the first three purposes of performance measurement: visibility, accountability and execution intelligence.

The KPI owner assesses progress against agreed targets and submits evidence of what has been achieved. The leader independently reviews the same KPI, bringing a broader organisational perspective. Together, they establish a shared understanding of execution reality.

Three questions guide the conversation:

  1. What progress has been achieved against the agreed target?
  2. What does the evidence tell us about performance?
  3. Where is actual progress falling short of expectations?
People performance

The individual and the leader establish the progress made against the agreed target.

Organisational performance

The leader brings the agreed measures or evidence of readiness for the organisational conditions, and the review establishes whether those conditions support the execution of the individuals’ tasks.

Outcome: A shared, evidence-based understanding of progress and any emerging performance gaps.
Stage 2 — Realign: Diagnose and Address Execution Gaps

Once Review establishes where progress is falling short, Realign determines what is preventing the expected performance.

The leader activates the S2R® Accountability Loop (part 1.4) to investigate whether the gap arises from:

  • Ownership: the individual understands the responsibility but has lost connection to its purpose or accountability.
  • Capability: the individual is committed but lacks the skills or support required to deliver.
  • Role or structural constraints: the responsibilities, resources or organisational conditions are preventing effective execution.

The leader and KPI owner identify an appropriate intervention, which may include renewed ownership, coaching, additional support or structural adjustments.

People performance

The Accountability Loop (part 1.4) diagnoses what is preventing the individual’s expected performance.

Organisational performance

The leader determines what is preventing an organisational condition from supporting the execution of the individuals’ tasks, and agrees the response.

Outcome: A clear understanding of the underlying performance constraint and an agreed corrective response.
Stage 3 — Restore: Rebuild Execution Momentum

Restore activates the fourth purpose of performance measurement: timely correction.

The agreed intervention is translated into concrete execution commitments. The leader and KPI owner establish what must happen next, who is responsible and when progress will be reviewed. At this stage, they:

  • Agree on corrective actions and expected progress.
  • Reaffirm responsibilities and accountability.
  • Establish follow-up timelines and support requirements.
  • Update the KPI alignment state to reflect whether clarity, capability and ownership have been restored.

Restoring momentum requires renewed commitment to the intended outcome, supported by the conditions necessary to achieve it.

People performance

The individual commits to the corrective actions and the follow-up dates.

Organisational performance

The leader responsible commits to the actions on the organisational conditions, each with a review date.

Outcome: A clear recovery pathway, renewed execution ownership and agreed follow-through.
How the cycle remains continuous

The next Review examines whether the corrective actions have produced the expected improvement. If gaps remain, the cycle continues until execution returns to the required trajectory.

The next Review examines both: the individuals’ progress, and whether the organisational conditions now support their tasks.

1.4 — The Accountability Loop
Diagnosing the source of performance gaps

Many organisations respond to performance gaps with one default response: they push harder. More urgency is applied. More pressure is introduced. Deadlines are reinforced. Pressure alone rarely solves execution challenges because pressure does not identify the underlying cause of the problem.

When a gap is identified, the leader activates the Accountability Loop: a structured diagnostic process designed to restore traction and ownership without collapsing into unproductive performance pressure. Most performance gaps emerge from one of three underlying conditions, and each calls for its own recovery.

The leader achieves the recovery through the GROW model participants used for the Alignment Dialogue in Unit 7: Goal, Reality, Options and Will. The goal of the conversation is ownership of the KPI: an alignment score of 9 or 10. Select each root cause to explore its recovery.

Root Cause A
Lack of Accountability
▼

The individual understands the task but has lost the motivational connection to it. The work may feel detached from a meaningful outcome. Competing priorities may have diluted attention. Personal energy may have shifted elsewhere.

Recovery: Reconnect purpose and ownership. The leader revisits the broader organisational context of the KPI. The conversation reconnects the task to the enterprise outcome it supports, and the individual is reminded how their contribution fits into the wider organisational map. By restoring that line of sight, motivation and commitment can often be reactivated.

Achieving the recovery through GROW:

  • GoalReconnect the KPI to the enterprise outcome it supports, and agree what an alignment of 9 or 10 would look like.
  • RealityExplore what has drawn attention away from the KPI and how the individual now sees the target.
  • OptionsThe individual names how they will restore focus on the KPI among competing priorities.
  • WillThe individual restates ownership, the actions and the follow-up date, and the leader asks the alignment question again.
Root Cause B
Lack of Skill
▼

The individual is committed but lacks the capability required to achieve the expected level. The challenge may involve technical expertise, analytical capability, leadership skills, or process knowledge that was initially underestimated.

Recovery: Develop capability, with no added pressure. Targeted coaching may be introduced. Peer support may be arranged. Formal training may be provided. In some cases the timeline attached to the KPI may also require adjustment if the capability development process requires additional time.

Achieving the recovery through GROW:

  • GoalAgree the result the KPI requires, the capability it calls for, and what an alignment of 9 or 10 would look like.
  • RealityEstablish which part of the task the individual cannot yet do, and what they can already do well.
  • OptionsChoose together among targeted coaching, peer support, formal training and an adjusted timeline.
  • WillAgree the development plan, any adjusted milestones and the follow-up, and ask the alignment question again.
Root Cause C
Misfit of Role or Structural Constraint
▼

The issue lies beyond motivation and skill development. The individual’s strengths do not align with the specific responsibilities, or the role requires capabilities that the individual does not naturally bring to the task.

Recovery: Structural alignment. The task may be redistributed across the team. Responsibilities may be adjusted. In some situations the individual may be repositioned into a role that better aligns with their strengths.

Achieving the recovery through GROW:

  • GoalAgree the outcome the KPI must deliver, whoever delivers it.
  • RealityEstablish where the demands of the role and the strengths of the individual, or the structure around them, do not match.
  • OptionsConsider redistributing the task, adjusting responsibilities or, in a separate role conversation, repositioning the individual.
  • WillAgree the structural change, who decides it and by when, and ask the alignment question again with whoever now owns the KPI.
Keep the two conversations separate

The performance conversation must remain separate from the role conversation. Performance discussions focus on progress towards outcomes. Role alignment discussions focus on placing individuals where their capabilities can generate the greatest organisational value. By maintaining this distinction, the organisation protects both fairness and clarity.

The correct root cause determines the correct recovery action: pressure alone will not solve a skill gap, and coaching will not solve a structural misfit.

Performance measurement makes execution visible, and the Review–Realign–Restore cycle turns what it shows into recovery.

Why Performance Measurement Determines Execution Velocity

Understanding why the discipline of review conversations is the factor that determines whether strategy execution sustains momentum or quietly stalls.

2.1 — Four Consequences of Weak Review Discipline
Click each to explore
Execution velocity

Execution velocity is the rate at which an organisation converts strategic intent into measurable results. That rate is shaped by how quickly leaders detect progress or deviation, understand its causes, make decisions and translate those decisions into action.

Performance measurement provides the evidence. Review discipline converts that evidence into timely decisions.

This is why organisations can have well-designed strategies, clearly assigned KPIs and detailed execution plans, yet still experience slow progress. The measures may exist, but the rhythm of examining them, resolving constraints and adjusting execution may be weak.

Four consequences of weak review discipline

When performance reviews occur inconsistently or superficially, several predictable patterns begin to emerge. These are the foreseeable consequences of allowing the review rhythm to weaken.

Consequence 01
Early Warning Signals Are Missed
▼

Execution rarely collapses suddenly. Performance deterioration typically begins with small, subtle indicators: a milestone that slips by a few days, a process bottleneck beginning to slow delivery, a capability gap emerging in one team, a dependency between functions that introduces unexpected delays. These signals often appear months before the final KPI target is at risk. But if leaders are not regularly examining performance evidence, these signals remain invisible. By the time the problem becomes visible at the end of the reporting period, the opportunity for early intervention has already been lost.

People performance

Early warnings in people performance show in the review conversation before they show in the KPI. Three patterns recur:

  • The quality of evidence falls. Results give way to explanations, and percentages arrive without the evidence behind them.
  • Alignment drifts. The individual who rated alignment at 9 when the target was agreed now gives short, guarded answers about the target, and no one asks the alignment question again.
  • Effort migrates. The individual’s time moves to work that senior leaders ask about, and the KPI receives what is left.

Each pattern leads a missed KPI by weeks. A leader who reviews monthly sees it as a trend across two or three conversations; a leader who reviews at quarter end sees the result alone. The difference is the recovery available: a capability gap seen in week four can be met with coaching; the same gap seen in week twelve leaves a revised target.

Workforce capacity. A contact centre carries a customer-retention Key Result. Two agents resign and their posts stay open for a quarter. The remaining team covers the volume through overtime, and resolution times hold for six weeks, then fall sharply as absenteeism rises. The vacancy and the overtime were on record weeks before the KPI moved.

Psychological safety. In a function where raising a problem in front of senior leaders has carried a cost, team leads report amber milestones as green. The review sees on-track ratings until a milestone is missed. The warning was there all along: months without a single amber rating.

Organisational performance

An early warning in organisational performance is evidence that an organisational condition has started to weaken in its support for the individuals’ tasks. It seldom carries a number of its own, so the review sees it only when it asks for it.

  • Systems · Integration. Sales, operations and finance each report from their own platform, and every cross-functional figure is consolidated by hand. The executive review works on information a week old, and a slipping delivery reaches the review after customers have already escalated it.
  • Environment · Infrastructure. Power outages at the main depot rise from one a month to one a week. Dispatch holds on backup generators until fuel costs exceed the budget; the outage log showed the trend weeks before the delivery KPI moved.

Measures or evidence of readiness for each condition give these signals a place in the review. When the review does not ask for them, the condition shows itself through the KPI it was meant to support.

Facilitator prompt: “In your experience, what is the typical lag between when a performance problem begins and when leadership acts? What would closing that lag by half be worth to your organisation?”
Consequence 02
Gradual Diffusion of Accountability
▼

Accountability is sustained through visibility. When review conversations disappear or become irregular, the psychological signal shifts. Targets remain written on the scorecard, but they no longer appear in leadership dialogue. KPI owners begin to interpret the absence of review as an indication that the target is no longer an active organisational priority. The KPI quietly moves from being operational to being aspirational. Work continues, but the urgency associated with the strategic outcome begins to fade. People assume progress is occurring somewhere in the system, yet no one feels the immediate responsibility to ensure that it actually is.

People performance

Individuals read priority from the review agenda. When a KPI is absent from three reviews in a row, the conclusion is reasonable: leadership attention has moved. Accountability then weakens in three ways:

  • The KPI is managed for appearance. Updates are written to reassure, and risks are held until they can no longer be hidden.
  • Ownership of a shared KPI spreads thin. Each individual assumes another is carrying the result, and the dependencies between them belong to no one.
  • The commitment loses its force. The commitment given when the target was agreed has not been tested since.

When the gap finally surfaces, the review becomes a search for who was responsible. The Accountability Loop cannot diagnose a commitment no one has been asked about.

Capability. A new procurement system is introduced with the promise that every buyer will be trained. Three months later half the buyers are untrained and purchase orders take twice as long. The buyers’ cycle-time KPI is reviewed every month; the training commitment has never been raised.

Organisational performance

Leaders make commitments on organisational conditions when targets are agreed. These commitments seldom appear on any scorecard, so they diffuse first.

  • Governance · Authority. A regional manager is accountable for a margin Key Result, yet every discount above a set level still needs head-office sign-off. The change to the delegation was approved in principle and never issued. The manager carries the accountability without the authority to deliver it.
  • Resources · Investment. The upgrade budget for the delivery fleet is approved in the plan and never released. The fleet-availability commitment is reported as on track every month until the vehicles are needed.

Individuals remain held to their targets while the support they were promised quietly lapses. Mutual accountability becomes one-sided, and individuals protect themselves: they commit to less, document more and move from commitment to compliance.

Facilitator prompt: “Which KPIs in your current scorecard are operational and which have quietly become aspirational? What made the difference?”
Consequence 03
Loss of Organisational Learning
▼

Every review conversation contains valuable insight about which approaches accelerate progress, where operational friction exists, and which cross-functional dependencies are working and which are slowing things down. When review conversations are reduced to occasional reporting exercises, results are recorded but the organisation never pauses to understand why those results occurred. Without reflection, improvement becomes accidental. The organisation’s execution capability stagnates even as it accumulates more strategic ambition.

People performance

Every review produces evidence on how results are achieved, and that evidence is the raw material of the organisation’s performance capability.

  • Practices that accelerate progress. One regional team meets its targets because its manager runs a short daily huddle on the Key Result. Recorded and examined, the practice can be taught to other teams. Unexamined, it stays one manager’s habit and leaves when that manager leaves.
  • Capability gaps that recur. When the same skill gap appears in reviews across several functions, it signals a development need at enterprise level.
  • Behaviours that strengthen ownership. Reviews show which leaders’ teams surface problems early and which conceal them.

When reviews only record results, talent decisions, development plans and succession rest on ratings with no evidence of how the results were achieved. The organisation keeps rewarding outcomes it cannot reproduce.

Organisational performance

Reviews also show how the organisational conditions behave as the strategy places weight on them. The learning is cumulative: the same constraint appearing in several reviews is evidence that a condition is not yet equal to the strategy.

  • Systems · Integration. Three functions each report delays at the handover between sales order and dispatch. Reviewed function by function, each delay looks local. Reviewed together, they show one handover that no one is responsible for.
  • Resources · Investment. For two years running, the budget for strategic initiatives is released in the second quarter, and each year the initiatives deliver in the second half alone. The pattern becomes visible only when reviews are compared across cycles.
  • Governance · Authority. Capital requests above a set value wait a month on average for an executive decision, and every Key Result that depends on one slips by about the same margin.

When reviews do not capture this learning, the organisation sets its next strategy on the same conditions. Targets rise, the conditions stay where they were, and the same constraints slow execution again. This learning is the evidence the executive team needs to judge whether the organisation can carry its next level of ambition.

Facilitator prompt: “What does your organisation know now about how execution works inside your system that it did not know eighteen months ago? If the answer is ‘not much,’ that is a review discipline signal.”
Consequence 04
Erodes Leadership Credibility
▼

When leaders set ambitious targets and communicate strategic priorities, they create an expectation that those priorities will remain visible in the leadership conversation. If those targets are not revisited through structured performance discussions, a subtle message spreads: the strategy announcement was more symbolic than operational. Teams become less attentive to new strategic commitments because they have learned that priorities often quietly fade. Consistent performance reviews counteract this by demonstrating that strategic priorities are ongoing leadership commitments.

People performance

Credibility is built in the review. A leader who asks for evidence every month, recognises progress and addresses shortfalls consistently shows that the priority is real. Two patterns erode it:

  • Priorities that fade. A strategic KPI launched with energy disappears from the review agenda, and individuals conclude the announcement was symbolic. High performers, who invest most in strategic commitments, become the most sceptical, and the organisation learns to wait and see whether a priority survives its first quarter.
  • Standards applied unevenly. Some individuals’ KPIs are examined closely while others are excused, often by seniority. Individuals read this as unfairness, and commitment falls across the whole team.

Values and consequences. The organisation states accountability as a value. A junior manager is challenged in the review for a missed milestone, while a senior executive’s missed commitment to release staff for the same project is never raised.

Psychological safety. Leaders invite open reporting of risks, and the first person to raise a delivery risk in a review is questioned in front of peers. From then on, risks reach the review late.

Organisational performance

Leaders make commitments when they agree organisational conditions, and individuals track these closely because their own delivery depends on them. Credibility in organisational performance is a test of fairness.

  • Governance · Risk Management. The executive team commits to a monthly decision forum to clear constraints, then postpones it twice in one quarter while the decisions wait.
  • Governance · Policies & Procedures. The review procedure requires evidence for every individual KPI and none for the organisational conditions, so a failed condition never reaches the agenda.

When leaders review individuals’ targets and leave their own commitments unreviewed, individuals see accountability running one way. Reviewing both, to the same evidence standard, shows that leaders hold themselves to the standard they set for others.

Facilitator prompt: “What is the current level of strategic commitment in your organisation? How much of that is shaped by what leaders consistently review, and how much by what they announce?”
◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: where they have seen the velocity gap in their own setting: a strategic priority that moved more slowly than planned, where the gap opened (detecting the deviation, understanding its cause, making the decision or translating it into action), the consequence that followed, what the delay cost and whether an organisational condition played a part.

Section Closing Insight

Review discipline sustains execution momentum; when it weakens, warning signals, accountability, learning and credibility weaken with it.

3.1 — Four Fractures in Performance Measurement
Select each gap

Performance measurement is ultimately a leadership conversation discipline. Most organisations have agreed goals and KPIs; the breakdown usually occurs in how leaders review and interpret the measurement together. When that discipline weakens, several predictable fractures appear across the organisation. Select each gap below to examine where it occurs and what it costs.

Gap 01
The Horizontal Coordination Gap
▼

One of the most common breakdowns occurs when performance reviews happen inside functions, with no cross-functional view. Each department reviews its KPIs independently. Finance examines financial indicators. Operations reviews throughput. Marketing examines campaign performance. Individually, these conversations may be rigorous and technically sound.

But strategy execution rarely moves through a single function. Enterprise outcomes depend on interdependent execution across multiple functions. When performance reviews remain confined within functional boundaries, leaders see partial performance signals and miss the full execution pattern. Each function may report strong progress while the overall strategic outcome remains inconsistent.

The leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, closes this gap by introducing periodic cross-functional performance reviews where the executive team collectively examines how the KPIs across functions interact to produce enterprise outcomes.

Implication for people performance

When each function reviews its own KPIs, individuals are judged on the part of the result they control, and the hand-offs between functions are never examined. An operations planner meets every internal measure while customer orders wait at the boundary with sales. Individuals learn to optimise their own measure, and the commitments they made to other functions go unreviewed: no one is asked whether they delivered what another function needed from them. Individuals whose work depends on another function are held to results they cannot complete alone, and the review sees their shortfall without the dependency behind it.

Implication for organisational performance

The organisational conditions that span functions fall between the reviews.

  • Systems · Integration. The order-to-cash process runs through sales, credit control, the warehouse and finance. Each function reviews its own step and reports it on time. No review sees the end-to-end cycle, which is where the customer experiences the delay.
  • Governance · Authority. A non-standard customer contract needs approval from legal, finance and operations. Each reports its own turnaround as acceptable; end to end, the decision takes three weeks, and the sales Key Result absorbs the wait.

The executive team sees each function’s conditions as adequate while the conditions at the boundaries hold back the enterprise result.

Facilitator question: “Name a strategic outcome in your organisation that required three or more functions to execute successfully. Where did the handoffs break down, and would a cross-functional review have caught it earlier?”
Gap 02
The Evidence Quality Gap
▼

Performance measurement loses power when review conversations rely on weak or inconsistent evidence. In some cases KPI owners present narrative explanations with no verifiable indicators. In others, the evidence reflects activity alone, with no evidence of progress towards the outcome. Sometimes information arrives too late to support timely decisions.

When evidence quality declines, review conversations shift away from execution insight and towards interpretation debates. Leaders begin discussing whether the data can be trusted, and the review loses sight of what the data reveals about progress. Over time, the credibility of the performance system weakens.

Closing this gap requires the leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, to reinforce a simple but powerful principle: every performance review must be anchored in verifiable evidence that shows observable movement towards the outcome, is timely enough to inform decisions, and is clearly connected to the KPI, target, or Key Result being assessed.

Implication for people performance

Weak evidence harms individuals in two ways. Strong performers cannot show what their contribution changed, and weak delivery stays hidden behind narrative. When the evidence offered is activity, such as training attended or meetings held, the review rates effort. Ratings lose credibility with the individuals themselves, and the performance standard agreed at the start of the period loses its anchor. Over time, individuals learn that the persuasive account is rewarded.

Workforce capacity. HR reports headcount at budget. The review does not see that a third of the roles in the unit carrying the Key Result are held by new staff still in training. The evidence that matters is productive capacity: how many of those roles can carry the work today.

Implication for organisational performance

Most reviews hold no evidence at all on organisational conditions. Their state is asserted: the system is stable, the roles are filled, the budget is available.

  • Environment · Technology. IT reports system availability at 99 per cent. The finance team reports that the month-end report takes three days to run. The first figure describes the system; the second describes whether it supports the tasks.
  • Resources · Asset Availability. The asset register shows every delivery vehicle in service. A third are off the road awaiting parts. The evidence that matters is how many vehicles can carry the work today.

With no evidence standard for the conditions, the review cannot tell whether they support the individuals’ tasks, and the discussion settles on the individuals’ numbers, the only evidence in the room.

Facilitator question: “In your last performance review, what share of the conversation was spent debating the data, and what share acting on it? That ratio is a proxy for your evidence quality gap.”
Gap 03
The Review Rhythm Breakdown
▼

Even well-designed performance systems weaken if the organisation does not maintain a consistent rhythm of review conversations. Strategy execution unfolds over time. Progress appears gradually through incremental movement across weeks and months. If review conversations occur too infrequently, leaders lose visibility into the trajectory of that movement. Small deviations accumulate unnoticed until they become significant performance gaps.

Irregular review cycles also weaken behavioural discipline. KPI owners begin to treat measurement as a reporting event.

Healthy performance measurement requires a predictable organisational rhythm. Leaders know when reviews will occur. KPI owners know when evidence must be prepared. Teams expect progress to be examined regularly. This rhythm creates a cultural signal: execution progress is observed continuously.

Implication for people performance

The rhythm of review sets the individuals’ planning horizon. Reviewed quarterly, individuals work in quarterly bursts: a slow start, a surge before the period closes and figures prepared for the review. Reviewed irregularly, evidence is assembled in a hurry and the conversation is spent reconstructing what happened. The recoveries of the Accountability Loop need a rhythm short enough for coaching, development or a structural change to take effect within the period.

Employee sustainability. Workload is seen through the annual engagement survey, so overload in a critical team shows first in resignations.

Implication for organisational performance

Organisational conditions often have no rhythm of review at all; they reach the agenda when something fails.

  • Governance · Authority. Capital and risk decisions follow the board calendar, so a decision on a condition a Key Result depends on can wait up to three months.
  • Resources · Resource Utilisation. Budgets are reallocated only at the mid-year forecast. An initiative found short of funds in its second month waits until its sixth.

When the conditions are reviewed on a slower rhythm than the KPIs they support, the conditions lag the work they are meant to enable.

Facilitator question: “How predictable is your organisation’s review rhythm? If a KPI owner was asked to describe the review schedule without referring to a calendar, could they?”
Gap 04
The Learning Suppression Gap
▼

A subtle but corrosive breakdown appears when performance reviews focus exclusively on evaluation. If review conversations concentrate only on whether targets were achieved, teams often become cautious about discussing obstacles openly. People instinctively defend results and leave the deeper causes unexplored. This dynamic suppresses organisational learning.

Yet strategy execution is inherently experimental. Organisations are constantly discovering what works, where friction exists, and how coordination across functions actually unfolds. Healthy performance reviews therefore balance accountability with curiosity. Leaders examine evidence rigorously, yet they also explore what the organisation is learning from execution patterns.

Questions shift from “Why was the target missed?” to “What is the system teaching us about how execution actually works?” When this learning dimension becomes part of review conversations, performance measurement evolves from a scoring mechanism into a capability-building system.

Implication for people performance

When reviews judge, individuals defend. They bring the explanation and hold back the obstacle, and in the next cycle they propose targets they are sure to meet. Learning moves out of the review into corridors and private conversations. The organisation loses the individuals’ first-hand knowledge of what is slowing execution, the most current intelligence it has.

Psychological safety. A team lead knows the approval process is the real constraint on the Key Result and does not say so, because the approval sits with the executive who chairs the review.

Implication for organisational performance

Suppression falls hardest on the conditions, because naming a condition often means naming a senior leader’s area.

  • Governance · Policies & Procedures. Shortfalls in individual KPIs are examined closely, while a condition failure in a senior leader’s area passes without examination. People conclude that the conditions are off limits.
  • Governance · Risk Management. The risk register lists the approval process as a delivery risk, and no review has examined it since it was logged.

The review then hears one side of every gap, and the organisation never learns which of its conditions the strategy is outgrowing.

Facilitator question: “What is the dominant mood in your organisation’s performance review meetings: defence or curiosity? What would shift it?”

4.1 — The Enterprise Performance Intelligence Circle (EPIC)
Select each signal

The Enterprise Performance Intelligence Circle (EPIC) is the executive team’s structured review of enterprise execution. The question that guides EPIC is: “Is the chain of execution across our functions holding together?”

Many organisations conduct performance reviews. Few organisations convert those reviews into collective strategic intelligence. EPIC ensures that insights emerging from PM review sheets are brought together across functions in a structured conversation that allows the leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, to observe execution as a living system.

In EPIC, each member of the executive team brings three signals from their own PM review sheet: where their Key Results stand, what is starting to break, and what execution is teaching them. Each signal answers one question, and the three are shared in that order. Progress comes first because it gives the warnings their context and the learning a system to learn about.

Select each signal.

Signal 1
Collective Trajectory
Signal 2
Early Warnings
Signal 3
Execution Learning
Signal 1 · Collective Trajectory: “Where are we on execution?”

What each leader brings. The current percentage progress towards their Key Results and KPIs, taken from the PM ratings and evidence in their PM review sheet, and whether momentum is accelerating, stable or slowing. The percentage involves no estimate and no narrative opinion.

How each leader says it. “We are at [X]% progress, supported by [evidence], and momentum is [accelerating, stable or slowing].”

Examples:

  • The COO reports that operational readiness KPIs show 72% progress towards the strategic Key Result, supported by throughput improvements and cycle-time reductions documented in the review sheets.
  • The CFO reports that financial indicators show 65% progress towards the quarterly profitability milestone, supported by margin improvements and cost optimisation evidence.

What the executive team sees. Which functions show strong forward momentum and which show slower movement. The executive team sees the collective trajectory of strategy execution, and uneven progress becomes visible at once.

Signal 2 · Early Warnings: “What is starting to break?”

What each leader brings. One or two warning signals that emerged in their PM review conversations, with the evidence from the review sheets. An early warning appears before a KPI or Key Result is formally off target.

How each leader says it. “An emerging risk is [signal], supported by [evidence].”

Examples of early warnings:

  • An operational process beginning to slow delivery times
  • A capability gap that may affect upcoming milestones
  • A supply constraint that could delay implementation of strategic initiatives
  • A change in customer feedback signalling emerging dissatisfaction

What the executive team sees. Where a signal in one function is connected to activity in another. What looked like an isolated operational issue shows itself as a cross-functional execution dependency, and the leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, can act on it before it becomes a measurable performance gap.

Signal 3 · Execution Learning: “What is execution teaching us?”

What each leader brings. One key learning from their PM reviews, with the evidence behind it: a capability insight, a coordination improvement, a leadership practice that strengthened execution, or a structural obstacle that slowed progress.

How each leader says it. “A key execution learning is [insight], supported by [evidence].”

Examples:

  • The CHRO highlights that performance review discussions revealed a leadership capability gap affecting accountability conversations.
  • The CTO shares that system visibility improvements significantly accelerated operational decision-making.
  • The CMO highlights a pattern in customer experience data that revealed which service interactions most strongly influence trust.

What the executive team sees. How execution actually works inside the organisation. Over time, the organisation builds a body of practical execution intelligence that improves how strategy is implemented.

What EPIC produces
  1. A clear snapshot of enterprise progress towards the strategic Key Results.
  2. Emerging risks visible early enough for coordinated intervention.
  3. Execution learning captured to strengthen future performance.

Through this practice, performance measurement becomes the organisation’s radar system for strategy execution.

4.2 — The Accountability Loop Role Play
Practice in class · Portfolio Work

Part 4.2 has two parts. In class, participants role play an Accountability Loop conversation in groups of three. After class, in their own file, each participant reads a full conversation on a new case, works out which of the three root causes is evident and analyses the conversation with the GROW model.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — In class: running the role play online

This is class practice. Nothing is entered on the portal during the role play.

  1. Put participants into breakout rooms of three: the leader, the team member and the observer.
  2. Each group reads the scenario and the three roles.
  3. Before the conversation, the team member privately chooses one of the three root causes as the reason behind the gap: Lack of Accountability, Lack of Skill, or Misfit of Role or Structural Constraint. The team member plays the conversation from that position, drawing on their own experience, and tells no one which root cause they chose.
  4. The leader decides what the conversation must achieve and runs it through the GROW model. The observer says nothing and completes the observation sheet.
  5. The observer reports first: what the leader set out to achieve, how the conversation went at each GROW stage, and which root cause it revealed. The team member then says which root cause they chose. The leader closes with what they would do differently.
► In class · the scenario

The Head of Customer Service is responsible for the Key Result: “Reduce the average time to resolve customer complaints from 10 working days to 5 by the end of the quarter.” It supports the enterprise Objective of improving customer retention.

The Complaints Team Leader leads a team of eight agents and was fully committed to the Key Result when it was agreed at the start of the quarter.

Seven weeks into the quarter, the latest Review shows that resolution time fell from 10 days to 8 in the first month and has not moved since. The Complaints Team Leader missed the last two weekly check-ins, and their written updates have become short. The Head of Customer Service calls a conversation to establish what lies behind the gap.

👤 The leader · Head of Customer Service

Decides the goal of the conversation, then works through Reality, Options and Will to reach it.

👤 The team member · Complaints Team Leader

Responds as the person behind the stalled Key Result, from the root cause they chose.

👤 The observer

Says nothing during the conversation and completes the observation sheet.

The observation sheet
  1. Goal: what was the leader aiming to achieve? Was it clear to the team member?
  2. Reality: which questions opened up what is really happening? Which lines point to the root cause?
  3. The root cause: Lack of Accountability, Lack of Skill, or Misfit of Role or Structural Constraint? Which lines rule out the other two?
  4. Options: who generated the ways forward? Did they match the root cause?
  5. Will: what was agreed, by whom, and when will it be reviewed?
  6. Did the conversation reach its goal? What did the leader do that helped, and what closed the conversation down?

Accountability Loop Diagnosis

The leader must determine whether the root cause is:

Root Cause A
Lack of Accountability

The individual understands the task but has lost motivational connection. Recovery: reconnect to purpose and enterprise context.

Root Cause B
Lack of Skill

The individual is committed but lacks the capability required. Recovery: coaching, training, timeline adjustment.

Root Cause C
Misfit of Role or Structural Constraint

Strengths do not align with responsibilities. Recovery: structural alignment, redistribution, or repositioning.

The GROW Model in the Accountability Loop

The GROW model guides a conversation with an end in mind. Each stage asks a question, and the team member does the thinking, so the answers reached are their own.

G
Goal

What the leader aims to achieve in the conversation. It may be identifying the root cause of the gap, restoring alignment with the Key Result, or both.

“What do we want to have agreed by the end of this conversation?”

R
Reality

What is true today. The leader explores what stands behind the gap and listens without defending the target.

“What is happening on this Key Result, and what makes it hard?”

O
Options

What could be done. The team member generates the ways forward, matched to the root cause.

“What could change? What support would make the difference?”

W
Will

What will be done, by whom and by when, and when it will be reviewed.

“What will you do, and when shall we look at it again?”

This case is different from the role play, and the root cause is never named in the conversation.

► After class · the Portfolio scenario

The Chief Financial Officer is responsible for the Key Result: “Reduce debtor days from 65 to 45 by the end of the half-year.” It supports the enterprise Objective of strengthening cash flow.

The Credit Control Manager joined the finance team four years ago as a credit analyst and was appointed manager eighteen months ago. Three months into the half-year, debtor days fell from 65 to 58 in the first two months and have not moved since. Most of the overdue balance now sits with twelve large customers.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

Thank you for making time. I want us to leave this conversation with a clear view of what is holding debtor days at 58, and an agreed way to move them. Can we start with the numbers?

Credit Control Manager

Of course. We went from 65 to 58 in two months. Since then, nothing.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

What produced the first seven days?

Credit Control Manager

The clean-up. I went through every disputed invoice, matched them against the delivery notes and cleared most of the disputes. I also redesigned the monthly statements so customers can see exactly what is overdue. That took the easy part out.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

That was strong work. What is left?

Credit Control Manager

Twelve large customers. Their invoices are correct and undisputed. They simply pay late, and they will only move if someone calls their finance director and holds firm.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

Who makes those calls today?

Credit Control Manager

Mostly the senior clerk. I make some of them.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

Would another course help? You attended the negotiation programme last year.

Credit Control Manager

I know what to say. The course was useful, and I can tell you the techniques. The calls themselves wear me down. Every one of them becomes an argument, and I put them off for days. When I do make them, I give ground too early.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

So the technique is there, and the calls themselves are hard for you. Tell me more about how your week goes.

Credit Control Manager

Honestly, the parts I enjoy are the analysis, the reconciliations and fixing the process. I would happily spend all week on those. The phone work for the big accounts is most of what this Key Result now needs, and it is the part I am weakest at.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

How do you see the 45 target?

Credit Control Manager

It is the right target. Cash matters, and I want us to reach it. I just do not think I am the right person to be on the phone with those twelve customers.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

What could change?

Credit Control Manager

The senior clerk is very good on the phone. Customers respect her. If she took the twelve large accounts, I could take over all the dispute work and the reporting for the whole ledger, including her current accounts.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

What would she need from you?

Credit Control Manager

The analysis on each account before every call: what is overdue, the payment history and the agreed terms. I can have that ready every Monday.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

Let us agree that. From next week she leads the calls on the twelve accounts, with your analysis every Monday, and you take the disputes and reporting for the whole ledger. I will confirm the change with her tomorrow. We will look at debtor days together at the end of the month.

Credit Control Manager

Thank you. That works.

CFO · Chief Financial Officer

One more thing. Whether the shape of your role needs to change for the longer term is a separate conversation. I would like us to have it next week.

Credit Control Manager

I would welcome that.

What the conversation shows
  • The root cause: Misfit of Role or Structural Constraint. The misfit shows in “The calls themselves wear me down” and “the parts I enjoy are the analysis…”.
  • Lack of Accountability is ruled out by “It is the right target… I want us to reach it.”
  • Lack of Skill is ruled out by “I know what to say… I can tell you the techniques.”
  • The conversation. The CFO sets the goal at the start. The question about another course assumes Lack of Skill before Reality has been explored; the CFO recovers, lets the Credit Control Manager generate the option, agrees the change and the review date, and keeps the role conversation separate.
◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

After the role play, participants complete personal Portfolio work in their own file. They read the conversation between the Chief Financial Officer and the Credit Control Manager, work out the root cause and analyse the conversation with the GROW model:

  1. Review: what the evidence shows about the Key Result.
  2. Realign: which of the three root causes is evident, the lines that point to it and the lines that rule out the other two.
  3. Restore with GROW: the goal of the conversation, and how Reality, Options and Will led to it.
  4. Their judgement: how well the leader ran the conversation, and what they would do differently.

The lesson participants see for consequence 1

From part 2.1 · Early Warning Signals Are MissedExecution rarely collapses suddenly. Performance deterioration typically begins with small, subtle indicators: a milestone that slips by a few days, a process bottleneck beginning to slow delivery, a capability gap emerging in one team, a dependency between functions that introduces unexpected delays. These signals often appear months before the final KPI target is at risk. But if leaders are not regularly examining performance evidence, these signals remain invisible. By the time the problem becomes visible at the end of the reporting period, the opportunity for early intervention has already been lost.

An example decision pointIf a Key Result shows no movement across two monthly reviews, then a recovery plan is presented at the next executive review, taken by the executive accountable for the Key Result.

The lesson participants see for consequence 2

From part 2.1 · Gradual Diffusion of AccountabilityAccountability is sustained through visibility. When review conversations disappear or become irregular, the psychological signal shifts. Targets remain written on the scorecard, but they no longer appear in leadership dialogue. KPI owners begin to interpret the absence of review as an indication that the target is no longer an active organisational priority. The KPI quietly moves from being operational to being aspirational. Work continues, but the urgency associated with the strategic outcome begins to fade. People assume progress is occurring somewhere in the system, yet no one feels the immediate responsibility to ensure that it actually is.

The lesson participants see for consequence 3

From part 2.1 · Loss of Organisational LearningEvery review conversation contains valuable insight about which approaches accelerate progress, where operational friction exists, and which cross-functional dependencies are working and which are slowing things down. When review conversations are reduced to occasional reporting exercises, results are recorded but the organisation never pauses to understand why those results occurred. Without reflection, improvement becomes accidental. The organisation’s execution capability stagnates even as it accumulates more strategic ambition.

An example decision pointIf the same obstacle is reported in two functions in one quarter, then the executive team names the cause and records what it changes in the way the strategy is executed, taken by the CEO.

The lesson participants see for consequence 4

From part 2.1 · Erodes Leadership CredibilityWhen leaders set ambitious targets and communicate strategic priorities, they create an expectation that those priorities will remain visible in the leadership conversation. If those targets are not revisited through structured performance discussions, a subtle message spreads: the strategy announcement was more symbolic than operational. Teams become less attentive to new strategic commitments because they have learned that priorities often quietly fade. Consistent performance reviews counteract this by demonstrating that strategic priorities are ongoing leadership commitments.

An example decision pointIf a strategic priority is announced with no date for its first review, then the date and the evidence to be brought to it are fixed in the same meeting, taken by the CEO.

Unit Summary
Unit 8 synthesis
Awareness — What

Performance measurement is the execution visibility infrastructure of the strategy. It serves four strategic purposes: visibility, accountability, execution intelligence and timely correction. The Review–Realign–Restore Cycle (R3) turns them into a recurring leadership rhythm, and the Accountability Loop diagnoses the root cause of a gap. Each review examines the progress of individuals and teams and whether the organisational conditions support the execution of their tasks.

Intelligence — Why

The rhythm of performance reviews determines execution velocity. When it weakens, early warning signals are missed, accountability diffuses, organisational learning is lost and leadership credibility erodes, in the performance of individuals and in the organisational conditions.

Extrapolating — Where

Performance measurement weakens when reviews remain confined within functions, evidence quality is inconsistent, review rhythms become irregular, or conversations focus only on evaluation. Each fracture affects human and organisational performance, and each executive role tends to feed one or two of them.

Integration — Collective

EPIC brings together three signals from each leader’s PM review sheet: collective trajectory, early warnings and execution learning. In the Accountability Loop role play, groups of three practise a conversation with an end in mind through the GROW model; after class, each participant works out the root cause in a full conversation on a new case.

Application — In Practice

For each of the four consequences of weak review discipline, each group simulated a potential gap in the performance of individuals and in the organisational conditions of its own strategy, and set a decision point for recovery for each. Each group then tested the WARD check-up it set in Unit 6: the alert fired, and the group ran the recovery. The agreed record feeds the team’s Capstone S2R® Blueprint.

“The result is an organisation that continuously restores performance. And that capability is one of the defining characteristics of organisations that consistently convert strategy into results.”
