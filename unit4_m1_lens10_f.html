Section 1 · Awareness — What

Values, Culture & Organisational Health

How organisational values become lived culture, and how that culture shows in the condition of organisational health.

Section learning outcomes
  • Evaluate how organisational values translate into cultural norms and practices that shape organisational health.
  • Diagnose organisational health strengths and vulnerabilities across observable behaviour and its underlying conditions.
1.1 — Organisational Values: What We Stand For

Organisational values are the fundamental principles that guide how an organisation conducts itself in pursuit of its purpose and strategic objectives. They establish the standards of behaviour expected of leaders and employees.

Values serve two organisational purposes:

1. Establish Behavioural Expectations. Values define the standards of conduct expected across the organisation. They guide how people interact, exercise authority, fulfil responsibilities and collaborate in delivering strategic priorities.

2. Provide a Basis for Accountability. Values establish the principles against which organisational conduct can be assessed. They enable leaders to evaluate how results are achieved, alongside the results themselves.

Translating Values into Behavioural Standards

For values to influence conduct, they must be expressed as observable behaviours.

Organisational ValueExpected Behavioural Standard
IntegrityAct honestly, communicate transparently and uphold ethical commitments.
AccountabilityTake ownership of responsibilities, honour commitments and report progress accurately.
RespectTreat others fairly, listen constructively and recognise different perspectives.
ExcellenceMaintain high standards, address performance gaps and pursue continuous improvement.
CollaborationShare information, coordinate efforts and contribute to collective outcomes.

Values and behavioural standards shape conduct only when leaders and employees repeatedly demonstrate, reinforce and uphold them. Through that consistent application, values become culture.

1.2 — Organisational Cultural Code
Select each part to open it

Organisational culture is the system of shared behavioural norms that determines how people conduct themselves and work together. Behaviours that are repeated become accepted as “the way we do things around here”. The actual culture therefore reflects the behaviours that have become normal, whatever the stated values say.

The Cultural Operating Code

The Cultural Operating Code translates each organisational value into a norm: the expected behaviour that shows the value being lived. The behavioural standards in 1.1 describe what a value means in practice; the Code turns each value into one norm that every leader and employee can recognise, practise and be assessed against.

Value → Norm (the expected behaviour) → Demonstrable behaviours

Through its norms, the Code makes explicit how people work together, exercise accountability, communicate and contribute to organisational priorities.

The Code connects values to daily leadership practice. When executives model, recognise and correct behaviour against the same norms, the conditions of organisational health begin to change: shared accountability reduces politics, explicit expectations reduce confusion, and consistent consequences protect morale. 1.3 shows how the Code connects to each health trait.

Why a Code is needed

Most organisations have stated values. Few have agreed what those values require people to do on an ordinary working day. Where that agreement is missing, each leader interprets the values in their own way, people read the real standard from what is tolerated, and the culture forms by default.

A Cultural Operating Code closes that gap. It states, value by value, the behaviour the organisation expects, recognises and corrects, so every leader applies the same standard.

The anatomy of a norm

Each norm in the Code has four parts:

  • The value: the principle the organisation stands for.
  • The definition: one line stating what the value means for this organisation.
  • The norm: the expected behaviour, stated as three actions a person performs, whose first letters form a short, memorable acronym.
  • The norm statement: one sentence describing the norm in daily practice, specific enough to show which behaviour will be recognised and which will be corrected.

Because the norm is made of actions, it shows as demonstrable behaviours that can be observed, coached and assessed.

What makes the Code real
  • Demonstrable: every value is translated into observable, specific behaviours.
  • Assessed: the norms appear in 360-degree feedback, so how results are achieved is assessed alongside what is achieved.
  • Enforced: consequences apply consistently regardless of seniority.
  • Maintained: norms are explicit, modelled by executives, reinforced in reviews and revisited periodically.
Where the Code is embedded

The Code holds when it is built into the practices that shape everyday experience:

  • Recruitment and onboarding: candidates are assessed against the norms, and new employees learn them in their first weeks.
  • Meetings and decision forums: the norms set how issues are raised, debated and decided.
  • Recognition and promotion: the people who are rewarded and promoted are those who live the norms.
  • Career development: development plans, progression and succession decisions take account of how consistently a person lives the norms.
  • Leadership development: leaders are coached on the norms where their behaviour falls short.
Three tests for every norm
  • The New Employee Test: Would a new employee reading this norm on the first day know exactly which behaviour will be recognised and which will be corrected?
  • The Pressure Test: Does the norm hold when a senior leader breaches it, when a high performer contradicts it, and when telling the truth is costly?
  • The Mirror Test: Would every executive be comfortable being assessed against this norm in their own 360-degree feedback?

Section 4 works through a complete Cultural Operating Code, the IBSL Code, built from these parts and tested against the three tests.

1.3 — Organisational Health: The Resulting Condition
Select each part to open it

Organisational health is the condition that results from how effectively organisational values are lived within the culture. It shows in the quality of working relationships, clarity of expectations, employee commitment, accountability and the organisation’s ability to pursue its strategic objectives.

Patrick Lencioni describes a healthy organisation as one that is whole, consistent and complete, with its management, operations, strategy and culture working together coherently.

The Five Observable Health Traits

The Five Observable Health Traits are the visible indicators of the condition of organisational health.

Health TraitObservable Organisational Condition
1. Minimal PoliticsEmployees collaborate openly, with limited personal agendas, territorial behaviour and internal competition.
2. Minimal ConfusionPeople understand organisational direction, priorities, responsibilities and performance expectations.
3. High MoraleEmployees demonstrate commitment, motivation and confidence in their working environment.
4. High ProductivityCollective effort is coordinated effectively and translates into meaningful performance outcomes.
5. Low TurnoverThe organisation retains capable employees through a working environment that supports commitment and contribution.

The Visible Traits
1. Trust — The Foundation

Trust forms the foundation of organisational health. It develops through consistent leadership behaviour, including keeping commitments, communicating transparently, acknowledging mistakes and treating people fairly.

When trust is established, employees develop confidence in their leaders and colleagues. They become more willing to communicate openly, raise concerns and contribute to collective objectives.

Leadership implication: Organisational health begins with the trust established through consistent leadership conduct.

2. Experiences — Shaped by Trust

Employees experience organisational culture through everyday interactions, meetings, performance conversations, leadership decisions and recognition practices.

These experiences communicate what the organisation genuinely values and the behaviours it considers acceptable. For example, the way leaders respond to mistakes influences whether employees experience the organisation as supportive of learning or inclined towards blame.

Leadership implication: Everyday organisational experiences reinforce or weaken the values leadership seeks to establish.

3. Beliefs — Formed by Experiences

Through repeated experiences, employees develop beliefs about how the organisation operates. They form assumptions about whether leaders listen, whether commitments matter, whether effort is recognised and whether honest communication is encouraged.

Over time, these beliefs influence how employees interpret organisational expectations and their own responsibilities.

Leadership implication: Repeated organisational experiences establish the beliefs that influence collective behaviour.

4. Actions — Driven by Beliefs

Employees’ beliefs influence how they behave and contribute to organisational performance. When employees believe their contributions are valued, they are more likely to share ideas, collaborate and take initiative.

When they believe accountability is consistently reinforced, they are more likely to take ownership of responsibilities and honour commitments.

Leadership implication: The beliefs established through organisational culture influence the behaviours employees demonstrate.

5. Results — The Visible Traits

The actions employees consistently demonstrate produce collective organisational outcomes. These outcomes become visible through the Five Observable Health Traits: Minimal Politics, Minimal Confusion, High Morale, High Productivity and Low Turnover.

At the apex of the pyramid, organisational results reflect the cumulative influence of trust, experiences, beliefs and actions. A commitment to continuous improvement helps sustain these results as the organisation evolves.

Leadership implication: The visible condition of organisational health reflects the underlying cultural experiences and behaviours established throughout the organisation.

Reading the Traits Through the Pyramid

The traits show the condition of organisational health; the Pyramid shows where it is formed. When trust deteriorates, employees’ experiences create scepticism, their beliefs become defensive and their actions shift towards self-protection. Over time, the visible traits weaken.

A weak trait is therefore strengthened at the layer where the weakness starts: the trust, experiences and beliefs that shape everyday employee behaviour.

How the Code Strengthens Organisational Health

The Organisational Cultural Code in 1.2 is the leadership lever on organisational health. Its norms act on the experiences layer of the Pyramid: when leaders model, recognise and correct against the same norms, employees experience consistent standards, form the belief that the values are real and act on that belief. Over time, the visible traits strengthen.

Health TraitWhat the Code Provides
Minimal PoliticsShared norms for raising issues, sharing information and resolving conflict openly.
Minimal ConfusionAn explicit expected behaviour for every value, applied the same way by every leader.
High MoraleConsistent recognition of behaviour that matches the norms.
High ProductivityNorms for ownership, coordination and follow-through on commitments.
Low TurnoverFair consequences applied consistently, regardless of seniority, in an environment where people want to stay.

A weak health trait therefore points to a norm that is missing, unclear or inconsistently applied.

1.4 — Vulnerable Health Signals

The traits in 1.3 describe organisational health when it is strong. When a trait begins to weaken, it shows first in employee behaviour, relationships and contribution. These emerging signals are read from observable behaviour and supporting evidence.

From Individual Assessments to Functional Health Scores

The responses are consolidated by function. Each trait score is the average of the valid individual trait totals, and the five trait scores add up to the functional health score out of 100. The functional leader receives the consolidated scores and is accountable for interpreting them.

Functional Trait Score = Sum of Individual Trait Totals ÷ Number of Valid Responses

Functional Health Score (/100) = Sum of Five Functional Trait Scores (/20 each)

Assessment safeguard: Employee responses should be collected confidentially. Executives receive consolidated function-level results only; individual responses remain unidentifiable. Small teams require appropriate aggregation to protect confidentiality.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why Organisational Health Determines Execution Performance

How organisational health multiplies execution capability, what poor health costs the enterprise, and how health sustains performance over time.

Section learning outcomes
  • Determine how organisational health can be leveraged to strengthen strategy execution capability and sustain enterprise performance.
  • Quantify the enterprise cost of poor organisational health to direct leadership attention to its performance implications.
2.1 — Organisational Health as a Performance Multiplier

Organisational health influences how effectively an organisation converts its strategic intentions into results. It acts as a multiplier on strategy execution capability: it raises or lowers how much of each capability converts into results, and how fast. When it deteriorates, mistrust, confusion, internal competition and disengagement reduce the effectiveness of otherwise sound strategic systems.

The Multiplier Effect

The multiplier works on each of the organisation’s existing capabilities:

Strategic CapabilityInfluence of Organisational Health
Strategic DirectionShared commitment strengthens alignment around organisational priorities.
Execution AccountabilityTrust and ownership support consistent delivery of commitments.
Performance ManagementOpenness enables constructive performance conversations and corrective action.
Enterprise CollaborationShared responsibility strengthens coordination across organisational functions.
Organisational AdaptabilityConfidence and collective commitment support responsiveness to changing conditions.

The multiplier also sets execution velocity: the pace at which strategic priorities become coordinated action. In a healthy organisation, performance concerns are raised and addressed early, decisions and commitments are followed through, information moves across functions, teams coordinate their contributions towards shared priorities and employees stay engaged in delivering objectives. These conditions remove friction, so execution keeps its momentum.

The Effect on Strategy Execution

Consider two organisations implementing similar strategic priorities. Both have established objectives, KPIs, execution plans and review mechanisms. In the first, leaders encourage open reporting, departments coordinate their contributions and employees take ownership of commitments. In the second, employees avoid difficult conversations, departments protect their own interests and performance reviews become defensive.

Although both organisations have similar execution systems, their ability to convert those systems into results may differ significantly. The difference lies partly in the organisational health supporting execution.

Key insight: The effectiveness and pace of strategy execution depend on the organisational health of the people and teams responsible for delivering it. As health deteriorates, execution friction increases and momentum becomes harder to sustain.

Sustaining Performance Over Time

Organisational health also influences whether that momentum holds over time. Short-term results may be achieved through intensive intervention, individual effort or temporary pressure. Sustained performance requires a culture that consistently reinforces the behaviours necessary for collective execution.

When values are embedded in everyday practices, employees develop shared expectations about accountability, collaboration, communication and performance. These expectations reduce dependence on constant leadership intervention and strengthen execution discipline.

Strategic implication: A healthy organisation is better positioned to maintain performance discipline as leadership responsibilities, strategic priorities and operating conditions change.

2.2 — The Price of Poor Organisational Health

Poor organisational health creates costs that are often overlooked because they do not appear as separate items in financial or performance reports. These costs accumulate through everyday organisational practices and eventually affect enterprise performance.

Each hidden cost is the price of one weakened health trait. The table reads from the trait that weakens, to the cost it creates, to how that cost shows and what it does to performance.

High ProductivityEmployee DisengagementReduced initiative, limited discretionary effort and withdrawal from collective responsibilities.Lower contribution and weakened performance momentum.
Minimal PoliticsDeclining TrustDefensive communication, withholding information and protection of departmental interests.Reduced collaboration and slower problem resolution.

How These Costs Accumulate

These costs stay hidden within operating expenses, lost productivity and reduced contribution until they are calculated. Quantifying them lets executives establish the financial impact, identify the largest sources of loss, prioritise corrective action, build the business case for strengthening organisational health and track the value recovered. The examples below show how small, recurring losses accumulate into substantial annual costs.

Named Hidden CostIllustrative AssumptionHow the Cost Accumulates
Lost Time20 employees each lose 2 hours weekly to rework or unclear communication; 46 working weeks; R350 hourly cost.20 × 2 × 46 = 1,840 hours; 1,840 × R350 = R644,000/year.
Employee Disengagement30 employees each contribute 1 fewer productive hour per week; 46 weeks; R300 hourly cost.30 × 1 × 46 = 1,380 hours; indicative capacity value = R414,000/year.
Declining TrustEight managers each spend 3 extra hours per month on defensive reviews and duplicated checks; R600 hourly cost.8 × 3 × 12 = 288 hours; 288 × R600 = R172,800/year.
Emotional FatigueA 50-person team records an additional 1 absence day per person annually; R2,000 average daily employment cost.50 × 1 × R2,000 = R100,000/year in indicative absence-related employment cost.
Talent LossFour avoidable departures; estimated replacement and onboarding cost of R85,000 per employee.4 × R85,000 = R340,000/year, excluding any unmeasured disruption.

Note: Calculations are illustrative and should be adapted using organisational data and reasonable assumptions. Overlapping costs should be adjusted to avoid double counting.

Key insight: Repeated losses in time, engagement, trust, wellbeing and retention can accumulate into a material annual cost of poor organisational health.

3.1 — CXO Functional Bias in Organisational Health Diagnosis
Select each executive to open the examples

Executives interpret organisational health through the people-performance signals they encounter in their respective functions. Their responsibilities influence what they observe, what they consider important and how they explain employee behaviour.

Functional bias occurs when an executive’s professional perspective influences the interpretation of organisational health signals, potentially leading to an incomplete or inaccurate diagnosis.

The Five Observable Health Traits provide a common basis for examining these differences.

CXO Functional Bias and Debiasing Matrix

CEO · Minimal Politics · High Morale
Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “My executive team works well together, so collaboration across the organisation must be healthy.”

Debiasing technique — Test across levels: Compare executive perceptions with employee experiences of cooperation, conflict and information sharing.

High Morale

Potential biased interpretation: “Employees are quiet in meetings because they have nothing to contribute.”

Debiasing technique — Examine employee experiences: Establish whether employees believe their contributions are welcomed and taken seriously.

CFO · High Productivity · Low Turnover
High Productivity

Potential biased interpretation: “Employment costs are increasing without equivalent output; employees must be underperforming.”

Debiasing technique — Separate effort from conditions: Examine whether employees have clear expectations, appropriate support and a culture that reinforces contribution.

Low Turnover

Potential biased interpretation: “Turnover is costly, so retaining employees means our people performance is healthy.”

Debiasing technique — Examine retention quality: Assess whether the organisation is retaining capable, committed employees and understand the experiences influencing their decisions to stay.

COO · Minimal Politics · High Productivity
Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “Departments are protecting their interests because employees are unwilling to cooperate.”

Debiasing technique — Examine reinforced norms: Determine whether leaders reward functional achievement while neglecting collective contribution.

High Productivity

Potential biased interpretation: “Employees keep missing commitments because they lack discipline.”

Debiasing technique — Trace expectations and experiences: Examine whether accountability standards are clear, consistently reinforced and experienced as fair.

CHRO · Minimal Confusion · High Morale · Low Turnover
Minimal Confusion

Potential biased interpretation: “Everyone has a job description, so role expectations must be clear.”

Debiasing technique — Test lived expectations: Compare formal role descriptions with the expectations employees actually receive from their managers.

High Morale

Potential biased interpretation: “Our engagement survey scores are positive, so employee morale is strong.”

Debiasing technique — Triangulate evidence: Compare survey findings with participation, initiative, absenteeism and employee feedback.

Low Turnover

Potential biased interpretation: “Our turnover is low, which demonstrates that employees are satisfied.”

Debiasing technique — Challenge the assumption: Examine whether employees remain because of commitment, limited alternatives or other circumstances.

CTO/CIO · Minimal Confusion · High Productivity
Minimal Confusion

Potential biased interpretation: “The information is on the system, so people know what is expected.”

Debiasing technique — Test use, not availability: Establish whether employees find, understand and act on the information the systems hold.

High Productivity

Potential biased interpretation: “System activity and transaction volumes are up, so productivity is healthy.”

Debiasing technique — Separate activity from contribution: Compare system activity with completed commitments and the outcomes delivered.

CMO · High Morale · Minimal Politics
High Morale

Potential biased interpretation: “Our employer brand and internal campaigns are well received, so morale must be high.”

Debiasing technique — Look beyond the message: Compare campaign response with how employees describe their everyday experience of the organisation.

Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “Customer-facing teams resist marketing priorities because they do not see the bigger picture.”

Debiasing technique — Examine competing incentives: Determine whether teams are rewarded on targets that pull against shared customer priorities.

CCO · High Productivity · Low Turnover
High Productivity

Potential biased interpretation: “Sales targets are being met, so the team is performing well.”

Debiasing technique — Examine how results are achieved: Establish whether targets are met through collective effort or through a few individuals carrying an excessive load.

Low Turnover

Potential biased interpretation: “Salespeople always move on; turnover is part of the job.”

Debiasing technique — Challenge the norm: Compare departure reasons with the experiences of those who stay, and identify the leadership practices driving exits.

CPO · Minimal Politics · Minimal Confusion
Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “Business units bypass procurement because they do not respect process.”

Debiasing technique — Examine the experienced value: Establish whether employees experience procurement as an enabler of their delivery or as a barrier to it.

Minimal Confusion

Potential biased interpretation: “The procurement policy is documented, so everyone knows the rules.”

Debiasing technique — Test applied understanding: Ask requisitioners to explain, in their own words, the steps and approvals they must follow.

CRO · Minimal Politics · High Morale
Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “No issues are being escalated, so there is nothing to worry about.”

Debiasing technique — Test for silence: Establish whether employees feel safe raising concerns, or whether problems are being withheld.

High Morale

Potential biased interpretation: “Compliance rates are high, so people are committed to our standards.”

Debiasing technique — Separate compliance from commitment: Examine whether employees follow standards because they believe in them or because they fear the consequences.

CSO · Minimal Confusion
Minimal Confusion

Potential biased interpretation: “We communicated the strategy clearly; employees should know what is expected.”

Debiasing technique — Verify understanding: Ask employees to explain their priorities, responsibilities and expected contributions in their own words.

These examples illustrate potential functional biases. They do not imply that executives in these roles necessarily hold these assumptions.

Key insight: The Five Observable Health Traits reveal where people-performance vulnerabilities are emerging. Executives strengthen diagnosis by recognising how their functional perspectives may influence interpretation and applying appropriate debiasing techniques.

4.1 — Applying the Thermometer and the Code Together

Section 1 taught two instruments for grounding people performance. The Organisational Health Thermometer shows the condition of organisational health in each function. The Organisational Cultural Code sets the norms that change that condition. Leaders use them together: the thermometer shows where a trait is weakening and what that costs, and the Code sets the expected behaviour that strengthens it.

Applied across the executive team, the thermometer forms the Cross-CXO Health Architecture. Each quarter, employees in every function complete the thermometer independently. Each CXO interprets the consolidated score for their function and quantifies the loss, and EXCO brings the functional positions together into an enterprise view and agrees priorities. The Code then gives every leader the same norms to model, recognise and correct.

This section works through both. Worked Example 1 applies the thermometer in one organisation (4.2 to 4.4). Worked Example 2 sets out a complete Cultural Operating Code, the IBSL Code (4.5).

Demonstrable behaviours

5.1 — Application Overview

Section 4 established the Cross-CXO Health Architecture. Individual employees independently complete the Organisational Health Thermometer; their responses are consolidated into functional health scores. Each CXO interprets the consolidated results, investigates potential cultural vulnerabilities and estimates associated people-performance losses. This capstone application brings the functional positions together for collective executive decision-making.

Participants use only the CXO roles already assigned within their capstone group. They (1) interpret the corresponding functional assessments, (2) consolidate the scores and losses for those represented functions, and (3) determine remedial actions through Review–Realign–Restore, and (4) build the organisation’s Cultural Operating Code from its values.

Capstone instruction: Each participant assesses the function associated with their existing capstone CXO role. The group consolidates only the functions represented by its members. The 10 functional examples below are a reference set from which participants select their relevant roles; there is no requirement to assess or include all 10 functions. Unit Summary
Unit 11 synthesis
  • Values establish the principles that guide behaviour. Culture is how those principles become established ways of working, and organisational health shows how effectively the values are lived.
  • Organisational health determines how effectively strategic capabilities translate into results. When health deteriorates, the cost of delivering strategy rises.
  • The Five Observable Health Traits show where culture is influencing people performance, read from evidence, with awareness of functional bias and personal accountability.
  • The Cross-CXO Health Architecture measures functional health, quantifies what poor health costs, consolidates it into an enterprise position and sustains it through a Cultural Operating Code.
  • Organisational health improves when the executive team reads it from evidence, prices what poor health costs, acts on the lowest-scoring conditions with named accountability and models the norms of its Cultural Operating Code.
