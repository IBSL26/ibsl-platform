Unit 6 — Session Overview

Use this tab to orient yourself before each section. It contains the purpose, the tone and the key facilitation questions for the full unit.

◆ Unit Intent

Unit 6 sets up enterprise performance management on two operating systems: people performance, what people deliver (the Human Operating System), and organisational performance, what the organisation enables through its organisational conditions (the Organisational Operating System). Participants learn the S2R® Contribution Map™, which connects an enterprise Key Result to the contributions of business functions, people and organisational conditions, and FACES, the five functions through which those contributions are managed. In Section 5 each group builds both for its own Capstone strategy.

◆ Key Facilitation Questions
  • “If your organisation stopped producing its performance reports tomorrow, which decisions would leaders no longer be able to make?”
  • “When a target is missed, how do you distinguish an individual contribution problem from an organisational delivery problem?”
  • “Which functions must contribute to achieving this result?”
  • “If employees are meeting their performance commitments but system outages continue to delay customer resolution, who must take responsibility for the performance gap?”
  • “What should happen when performance reviews repeatedly identify the same approval delays or staffing shortages?”
◆ Tone & Watch Points

Avoid: turning the Contribution Map or FACES into a compliance checklist. They are design and management tools.

Watch for: a missed target read as proof of individual underperformance before the organisational conditions are examined. A missed target is evidence of a performance gap; it does not establish its cause.

Watch for: leaders who equate “we have a scorecard” with “we have a PM system.” Probe: “What happens after you look at the scorecard? What changes? Who decides what?”

Section 1 · Awareness — What

Foundations of Enterprise Performance Management System Design

What performance management must manage: the contribution of people and the conditions the organisation provides, connected to enterprise results.

Section learning outcomes
  • Convert performance evidence into accountable decisions and follow-through.
  • Manage human contributions and organisational conditions as interdependent drivers of results.
1.1 — Measurement vs Management
The core distinction

Performance management (PM) and performance measurement are distinct disciplines. Measurement answers: What happened? It provides data, results, scores and dashboards. Management answers: What do we do about it? It establishes the conversations, decisions, actions and accountability through which leaders respond to that evidence.

An organisation can measure a missed service target accurately without managing the cause. A dashboard may show that customer requests take twelve days against a standard of five. Effective management asks whether the delay reflects unclear individual expectations, insufficient capacity, a broken hand-off, slow approval rights, or a combination. Each explanation calls for a different response.

The design sequence

The design sequence is deliberate:

Step 1
Define success

Agree the result the organisation must achieve.

Step 2
Design how performance will be managed

Agree the conversations, decisions, actions and accountability that will respond to the evidence.

Step 3
Identify the evidence that management decisions require

Collect the data the management decisions need.

Step 4
Use the evidence

Recognise contribution, correct constraints and improve delivery.

Reports become useful because the organisation has decided who will act on them and how.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Ask: “If your organisation stopped producing its performance reports tomorrow, which decisions would leaders no longer be able to make?” Follow with: “When a target is missed, how do you distinguish an individual contribution problem from an organisational delivery problem?”

1.2 — Two Operating Systems Behind Enterprise Performance
Understanding what people deliver and what the organisation enables

Enterprise performance is the combined result of individual and collective contributions, supported by the organisational conditions required to deliver those results.

An organisation may have capable employees who consistently work towards their targets, yet still struggle to achieve its strategic outcomes. Equally, an organisation may have strong systems, sufficient resources and well-designed processes, but fail to achieve results because individuals are not delivering the contributions expected of them.

This distinction is fundamental to Enterprise Performance Management (EPM). A complete performance management system must evaluate what people contribute and whether the organisation provides the conditions necessary for those contributions to produce the intended results. We refer to these as two interconnected operating systems. Their results are people performance and organisational performance.

1.2.1 The Human Operating System▾

What individuals and teams contribute to organisational results.

The Human Operating System concerns the measurable performance of people within the organisation. It examines whether individuals and teams deliver the results expected of them and demonstrate the behaviours required to achieve those results. Human performance has two primary dimensions.

Dimension 1 · Performance against KPIs and objectives▾

These establish what an individual or team is expected to deliver. They may include strategic contributions expressed through OKRs, operational outputs measured through key performance indicators (KPIs), and specific performance commitments.

For example, a customer service employee may be expected to resolve 90% of enquiries within an agreed turnaround time, while a sales manager may be accountable for achieving a quarterly revenue target.

The performance management system must establish clear targets, evidence of achievement and a consistent basis for evaluating actual performance.

Dimension 2 · Performance against behavioural expectations▾

These establish how individuals are expected to perform their responsibilities. They include observable behaviours such as collaboration, accountability, adherence to organisational values, responsiveness, ethical conduct and leadership effectiveness.

For example, a manager may achieve departmental targets while consistently withholding information from other departments, undermining collaboration and delaying enterprise-wide delivery.

A complete assessment must therefore consider both the results achieved and the behaviours demonstrated.

The Human Operating System answers: Are individuals and teams delivering their expected results and demonstrating the behaviours required for sustained organisational performance?

1.2.2 The Organisational Operating System▾

Whether the organisation is equipped and configured to deliver its intended results.

The Organisational Operating System concerns the conditions that enable or constrain performance. Within S2R®, these conditions are examined through ESRG: Environment, Systems, Resources and Governance, each with three components. ESRG provides the structure for evaluating whether the organisation has the operating capability necessary to support its strategic ambition. The capacity, capability and behaviour of people sit in the Human Operating System. Unit 10 teaches how to leverage ESRG to develop the organisational capability that grounds performance.

E · Environment · FIT▾

Components: Facilities · Infrastructure · Technology.

The physical and technological setting in which the work takes place: facilities suited to the planned activity, infrastructure that reliably carries operational demand, and technology that is available and fit for the required work.

Example: a customer service team has clear targets, but repeated outages of the customer management system leave enquiries unresolved.

Performance management consideration: Are the facilities, infrastructure and technology supporting or obstructing the achievement of expected results?

S · Systems · SIP▾

Components: Standardisation · Integration · Process Efficiency.

How work is defined, connected and performed across the organisation: processes clearly defined and consistently applied, handovers that connect across functions, and process design that delivers the required output without unnecessary steps, delay or duplication.

Example: employees may be expected to process 100 applications daily, but disconnected handovers between departments limit actual throughput to 60 applications.

Performance management consideration: Are the systems and processes capable of supporting the required performance standards?

R · Resources · AIR▾

Components: Asset Availability · Investment · Resource Utilisation.

The productive assets and funding required to deliver results, and how productively they are deployed against priorities. Resource adequacy must be evaluated against the level of performance the organisation expects.

Example: a department may be required to double its service output with the same equipment and no additional budget.

Performance management consideration: Does the organisation have sufficient and appropriately deployed assets and funding to achieve its targets?

G · Governance · PAR▾

Components: Policies & Procedures · Authority · Risk Management.

The policies and procedures, decision authority and risk management that guide organisational execution. Governance determines whether decisions can be made at the appropriate level and time, and whether risks to delivery are identified and managed.

Example: a department may have the resources to complete a project, but repeated approval delays prevent it from meeting agreed milestones.

Performance management consideration: Do governance arrangements enable timely decisions, clear accountability and effective resolution of performance constraints?

The Organisational Operating System answers: Does the organisation have the environment, systems, resources and governance necessary to deliver its expected results consistently?

1.2.3 How the Two Operating Systems Interact▾

The two systems are interdependent. Human performance measures what individuals and teams contribute. Organisational performance examines whether ESRG provides the conditions required for those contributions to produce enterprise results.

Consider a customer service department with a target of resolving 90% of enquiries within 48 hours. At the end of the month, the department achieves only 65%. A conventional performance review may conclude that the employees are underperforming. An integrated EPM system examines both operating systems before determining the appropriate management response.

Human Operating SystemOrganisational Operating System — ESRG
Are employees meeting their individual resolution targets?Environment: Are the facilities, infrastructure and technology the team relies on available and reliable?
Are employees following the required service procedures?Systems: Is the customer management system functioning reliably?
Are employees demonstrating accountability and responsiveness?Resources: Are the equipment and funding sufficient for the volume of enquiries?
Are team leaders meeting their supervisory commitments?Governance: Are decisions and escalations being resolved within the required time?

The evidence may reveal that employees are performing their responsibilities correctly, but system failures and approval delays are preventing the department from achieving its targets. Alternatively, ESRG conditions may be adequate, while employees are consistently failing to meet agreed performance expectations.

Both situations produce disappointing results, but they require different management responses. This is why an enterprise performance management system must distinguish between human performance shortfalls and organisational operating constraints. It enables leaders to establish accountability fairly, direct interventions appropriately and avoid repeatedly holding individuals responsible for conditions outside their control.

The Enterprise Performance Principle▾

Organisational performance emerges from the interaction between what individuals and teams contribute and how effectively the organisation’s Environment, Systems, Resources and Governance enable those contributions to produce results.

An integrated EPM system must therefore establish two connected lines of performance accountability:

  • Human Performance: individual and team KPIs, OKRs and behavioural expectations.
  • Organisational Performance: ESRG effectiveness in enabling the achievement of enterprise outcomes.

Both must be visible, measured, reviewed and managed.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Begin by asking participants: “When an employee or department misses a performance target, how does your organisation determine whether the problem is with individual performance or the conditions under which the work is performed?”

Use the customer service example to demonstrate that a missed target is evidence of a performance gap, but does not automatically establish its cause.

Emphasise that leaders must examine both the contribution expected from people and the ESRG conditions required to enable delivery.

The central teaching point is that performance accountability must extend to those responsible for organisational operating conditions as well as those responsible for individual and team targets.

1.3 — The S2R® Contribution Map™
Connecting enterprise strategic outcomes to functional contributions
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Introduce the Contribution Map using the customer service example. Begin with the enterprise key result of improving 48-hour customer enquiry resolution from 65% to 90%.

Explain that achieving this outcome requires contributions from several functions, each with different responsibilities. Walk participants through the map, showing how Customer Service, IT, Operations and Workforce Planning contribute to the same enterprise result.

Emphasise three teaching points:

  1. Functional KPIs must have a clear connection to enterprise outcomes. A department can perform well against its own measures without making the contribution the enterprise requires.
  2. Functional contributions are interdependent. A weakness in one function can undermine the results of several others.
  3. Accountability must follow the contribution. Each function must be accountable for the performance commitments it controls, while collective enterprise results remain a shared leadership responsibility.
1.3.1 What Is the S2R® Contribution Map™?▾

Integrated human and organisational contributions.

The S2R® Contribution Map™ is a strategy-to-execution tool that identifies the interconnected contributions required from business functions, individuals and teams, and organisational operating conditions to achieve an enterprise strategic outcome.

It establishes a clear line of sight between the organisation’s strategic intent and the contributions required across people performance and organisational performance:

  • People performance: the results and behaviours individuals and teams must deliver.
  • Organisational performance: the organisational conditions (environment, systems, resources and governance) necessary to enable those contributions.

The map identifies the relationships and dependencies between functions, making visible how their combined contributions support enterprise results. Where appropriate, it also establishes measurable performance commitments or evidence that the required organisational conditions support delivery.

The central principle: enterprise results are achieved through connected human and organisational contributions, supported by clear functional accountability.

1.3.2 Why the Contribution Map Matters▾

An organisation achieves its strategic results through the combined contributions of its business functions. Each function performs different activities, possesses different capabilities and carries different responsibilities. However, these contributions must ultimately support the same enterprise strategic outcomes.

A common weakness in performance management is that departments establish their own KPIs and measure their performance independently. Finance measures financial performance, HR measures people-related outcomes, IT measures technology performance, and Operations measures operational efficiency. Each department may report satisfactory performance while the organisation continues to miss its strategic targets. This happens when functional performance is managed without sufficient clarity about how the contributions connect to enterprise results.

For example, an organisation may have an enterprise objective to improve customer service. Customer Service may focus on resolving enquiries, IT on maintaining system availability, Operations on processing requests, and HR on ensuring adequate staffing. All four functions may have relevant KPIs. However, unless their contributions are connected to the enterprise objective, leaders may struggle to establish whether the combined performance of these functions is sufficient to achieve the intended result.

The S2R® Contribution Map™ provides that connection.

1.3.3 The Logic of the Contribution Map▾

The map follows three connected steps.

Step 1 · Enterprise OKR — What must the organisation achieve?▾

Identify the agreed enterprise strategic objective and its measurable key result. This establishes the collective outcome towards which the organisation is working.

Step 2 · Required Functional Contributions — What must each function deliver?▾

Identify the specific contribution each business function must make towards achieving the enterprise outcome. These contributions must reflect the interdependencies between functions.

Step 3 · Human and Organisational Contributions — What is required to deliver each functional contribution?▾

For each function, identify:

  • People performance: the results and behaviours required from individuals and teams, together with measurable performance commitments wherever practical.
  • Organisational performance: the specific organisational conditions necessary to enable delivery, together with appropriate performance measures or evidence that those conditions are established and functioning.

Only the organisational conditions relevant to the required contribution are included. Not every organisational condition needs a numerical metric.

1.3.4 How the Contribution Map Connects People Performance and Organisational Performance▾

The S2R® Contribution Map™ integrates people performance and organisational performance into the design of enterprise performance. For every required functional contribution, the map identifies two interconnected dimensions.

People Performance · What must people deliver?▾

The map establishes the expected results and behaviours of individuals and teams, supported by measurable performance commitments wherever practical.

Organisational Performance · What must the organisation enable?▾

The map identifies the specific organisational conditions necessary for people to deliver their contributions. These requirements may be expressed through numerical performance standards or other evidence of organisational readiness and effectiveness.

For example, maintaining reliable customer management systems requires IT employees to monitor and resolve incidents promptly. It also requires reliable infrastructure and sufficient technical support capacity. The human contribution can be assessed through incident-resolution performance. Organisational capability can be assessed through system availability and evidence of adequate technical support arrangements.

The two dimensions are connected but require distinct management attention. The Contribution Map therefore establishes what must be delivered by people, what must be enabled by the organisation, and how both contribute to the enterprise result.

From the Contribution Map to FACES▾

The S2R® Contribution Map™ establishes what each function must contribute, what people must deliver and which organisational conditions must support their performance. It also identifies the measures and evidence required to assess these contributions.

The next challenge is to ensure that these commitments are consistently delivered and that performance gaps trigger appropriate management action. The FACES Framework establishes the five management functions required to achieve this across people performance and organisational performance.

1.4 — The FACES Framework
Five functions of an integrated Enterprise Performance Management system
1.4.1 The FACES Framework▾

The FACES Framework defines five interconnected functions that an effective Enterprise Performance Management (EPM) system must fulfil. Each function applies to people performance and organisational performance:

  • People Performance: what individuals and teams deliver through their KPIs, OKRs and demonstrated behaviours.
  • Organisational Performance: how effectively the organisational conditions enable the achievement of intended results.
FACES — comparing human and organisational performance
F — Foster a Results Culture▾

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursEstablish a culture of individual and team performance ownership. Employees understand their targets, take responsibility for commitments and demonstrate expected behaviours. Leaders reinforce performance through regular feedback, recognition of dependable contributors and consistent responses to underperformance. Achieving results through behaviours that undermine colleagues, customers or organisational values is not acceptable performance.

Organisational PerformanceEstablish organisational responsibility for enabling results. Leaders must demonstrate the same commitment to maintaining effective operating conditions that they expect employees to demonstrate towards their targets. Recurring system failures, inadequate resources or governance delays cannot be continually tolerated while employees are held accountable for missed results. A results culture requires leaders to address constraints in the organisational conditions that repeatedly undermine delivery.

A — Align Performance▾

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursConnect individual and team performance to enterprise strategic outcomes. Strategic priorities must translate into meaningful KPIs, OKRs and behavioural expectations. Employees should understand how their work contributes to broader organisational results. Alignment also includes collaboration where successful delivery depends on contributions across departments.

Organisational PerformanceAlign the organisational conditions with enterprise strategic requirements. The working environment, systems, resources and governance arrangements must be configured to support the results the organisation intends to achieve. For example, a strategic commitment to faster customer service must be supported by efficient workflows, sufficient capacity and timely decision-making. Targets become difficult to achieve when organisational capability is misaligned with strategic ambition.

C — Create Measurement Transparency▾

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursMake performance expectations and assessment criteria clear and credible. Employees must understand their KPIs, targets, weightings, behavioural standards and rating criteria. Performance assessments should rest on observable evidence. Transparency allows employees to understand their progress and builds confidence in the fairness of performance evaluations.

Organisational PerformanceMake organisational performance conditions measurable and visible. Leaders need evidence of whether the organisational conditions are supporting or constraining delivery. This includes system availability, process efficiency, capacity against demand, cross-functional coordination and decision turnaround times. A missed departmental target reveals a performance gap; evidence on the organisational conditions helps establish why that gap exists and where intervention is required.

E — Embed Shared Accountability▾

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursClarify responsibility for individual contributions and collective results. Individuals remain accountable for their KPIs, OKRs and behaviours. Managers are accountable for setting expectations, providing feedback and addressing performance concerns. Where results require teamwork, each person’s contribution and responsibilities must be clear. Shared accountability should strengthen ownership without making responsibility ambiguous.

Organisational PerformanceAssign accountability for organisational enablement and constraint resolution. Responsibility for each organisational condition must sit with the appropriate leader. Operations may own process efficiency, IT may own system reliability, resource owners may own capacity, and executives may own governance decisions. Where one function’s performance depends on another, the dependency, accountable owner and escalation arrangements must be explicit. This forms part of the foundation for the S2R® Contribution Map™.

S — Support Continuous Development▾

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursUse performance evidence to strengthen individual and team capability. Reviews should identify development needs linked to actual performance requirements. These may include technical skills, planning, decision-making, leadership, collaboration or behavioural improvement. Development actions should have clear objectives and follow-up reviews to determine whether performance has improved.

Organisational PerformanceUse performance evidence to strengthen organisational operating capability. Recurring constraints in the organisational conditions should trigger improvements to workflows, technologies, resource allocation, operating conditions or governance arrangements. For example, repeated approval delays may require redesigned decision rights, while persistent capacity shortages may require resource reallocation. Organisational improvement must be monitored to establish whether the intervention strengthens delivery.

1.4.2 How FACES Works When the Enterprise Result Falls Short▾

Assume the organisation reviews its performance and finds that only 65% of customer enquiries are being resolved within 48 hours, against the year-end target of 90%. The review reveals different performance conditions across the four functions.

Customer Service▾

Performance EvidenceFollow-through on outstanding enquiries is below the agreed standard.

How FACES Is AppliedOutstanding cases are reviewed against assigned responsibilities. Employees receive feedback on missed commitments, recurring obstacles are escalated, and subsequent follow-through results establish whether performance is improving.

IT▾

Performance EvidenceIncident-response commitments are being achieved, but system availability remains below the required standard.

How FACES Is AppliedIncident-response performance and system availability are examined separately. IT leadership investigates recurring outages, assigns responsibility for correcting the underlying infrastructure problems and reviews subsequent availability results.

Operations▾

Performance EvidenceProcessing performance is affected by approval delays and some employee processing gaps.

How FACES Is AppliedProcessing records distinguish employee-related delays from approval bottlenecks. Employees receive support where performance gaps are identified, while the appropriate decision owners review approval arrangements that are slowing delivery.

HR / Workforce Planning▾

Performance EvidenceStaffing schedules are completed as required, but actual staffing coverage remains insufficient.

How FACES Is AppliedCompleted schedules are compared with actual staffing requirements. Workforce Planning coordinates available staffing adjustments, while unresolved capacity requirements are submitted to the appropriate resource decision-makers.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Walk participants through the four functions, showing how the same FACES principles are applied differently according to the contributions required.

Draw attention to the distinction between people performance accountability and organisational enablement accountability.

Conclude by reinforcing the connection:

The S2R® Contribution Map™ establishes the contributions, performance measures and enabling conditions required for enterprise results. FACES establishes the management disciplines through which those contributions are aligned, evaluated, acted upon and continuously strengthened.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Performance Management as the Execution Bridge

Understanding why enterprise performance depends on connecting strategic outcomes, human contributions and organisational operating capability through an integrated management system.

Section learning outcomes
  • Establish management disciplines that sustain the connection between strategy and performance.
  • Evaluate whether performance practices build genuine commitment or merely compliance.
2.1 — Performance Management as the Execution Bridge
Four execution bridges

The S2R® Contribution Map™ establishes the contributions required to achieve enterprise results. FACES defines the five functions through which human and organisational performance are managed. The next challenge is sustaining the connection between these contributions and the intended results throughout execution.

Strategy is delivered over time. Performance expectations must remain clear, progress must be visible, and emerging gaps must lead to timely management responses. Without these disciplines, the connection between strategic intent and actual delivery gradually weakens.

This is why performance management serves as an execution bridge: it provides the disciplines that keep strategy connected to performance throughout delivery.

Four interconnected bridges make this possible:

Bridge 1
Decomposing the Big Picture

Connecting strategic outcomes to actionable performance expectations.

Bridge 2
Defining the Logic of Success

Establishing how successful performance is understood and evaluated.

Bridge 3
Calibrating the Rhythm

Maintaining visibility of progress throughout the execution period.

Bridge 4
Structuring the Dialogue

Converting performance evidence into decisions and action.

Together, these bridges explain why performance management is essential to sustaining the connection between strategy, human contribution, organisational capability and enterprise results.

2.1.1 How the Four Execution Bridges Work Together▾

The four bridges work together to sustain the connection between strategy and results. Each addresses a particular risk that can weaken execution across people performance and organisational performance.

1. Decomposing the Big Picture▾

Why It MattersEnterprise strategy must remain connected to the contributions through which it is delivered. As priorities and operating demands change, the relationship between functional performance and enterprise outcomes must remain clear.

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursMaintains the line of sight between enterprise objectives and individual or team expectations. Employees understand how their contributions support the intended result, including where delivery depends on other functions.

Organisational PerformanceMaintains alignment between strategic requirements and the organisational conditions needed to support them. Systems, resources, workflows and decision arrangements must remain appropriate to the demands of execution.

What Happens When the Bridge WeakensFunctions become increasingly focused on their own activities and targets. Individual performance may appear satisfactory while the collective contribution to enterprise results weakens.

2. Defining the Logic of Success▾

Why It MattersPerformance must be evaluated against a shared understanding of successful delivery. Completing an activity or achieving an isolated target does not necessarily establish that the intended contribution has been achieved.

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursEstablishes clear expectations for results, performance standards, behaviours and evidence of achievement. Employees and managers have a credible basis for evaluating contribution.

Organisational PerformanceEstablishes how effective organisational enablement will be recognised. Relevant organisational conditions are assessed through appropriate measures or qualitative evidence of their effectiveness.

What Happens When the Bridge WeakensDifferent functions interpret success differently. People performance may be rated positively while organisational constraints remain unresolved, creating a misleading picture of execution readiness and achievement.

3. Calibrating the Rhythm▾

Why It MattersExecution unfolds over time. Changes in performance, demand and operating conditions must become visible early enough for leaders to influence the outcome.

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursProvides regular visibility of individual and team progress, emerging performance gaps and development needs. Review frequency reflects the nature of the contribution and its effect on results.

Organisational PerformanceProvides visibility of changing organisational conditions, such as system reliability, staffing capacity, process efficiency and decision delays. Monitoring frequency reflects the nature and significance of each condition.

What Happens When the Bridge WeakensPerformance deterioration is recognised too late. Employees continue working against targets while emerging organisational constraints accumulate, leaving limited opportunity for recovery.

4. Structuring the Dialogue▾

Why It MattersPerformance evidence creates value when it informs decisions and action. Reviews must establish what is influencing results, who is responsible and what response is required.

People Performance — KPIs, OKRs & BehavioursEnables evidence-based conversations about contribution, achievement, behaviour, performance gaps and improvement. Employees and managers establish appropriate responses and follow-through.

Organisational PerformanceEnables leaders to examine constraints in the organisational conditions, determine their effect on delivery and assign decisions or interventions to the appropriate leaders. Cross-functional dependencies are addressed collectively.

What Happens When the Bridge WeakensPerformance meetings become reporting exercises. The same gaps recur because evidence does not consistently lead to decisions, coordinated interventions and accountable follow-through.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Take the bridges one at a time through the table: why each matters, what it sustains for the people, what it sustains for the organisational conditions, and what happens when it weakens.

Ask: “Which of the four bridges is weakest in your organisation, and what do you see happening as a result?”

2.2 — Compliance vs Commitment Architecture
The design choice that shapes performance behaviour

The four execution bridges establish the disciplines required to sustain the connection between strategy and results. However, their effectiveness is influenced by the way an organisation approaches performance expectations, accountability and performance conversations.

This introduces an important distinction between compliance-driven and commitment-driven performance management architectures.

Compliance-driven architecture

Emphasises adherence to prescribed expectations, monitoring performance and correcting deviations.

Commitment-driven architecture

Emphasises shared understanding of expected results, ownership of contributions, constructive performance dialogue and collective responsibility for improvement.

Both approaches recognise the importance of standards and accountability. Their differences become visible in how performance is experienced and how the organisation responds when results fall short.

2.2.1 Four Dimensions of Compliance and Commitment▾
DimensionCompliance-Driven ArchitectureCommitment-Driven Architecture
PurposeEnsure minimum standards are met and deviations corrected.Generate shared ownership of results and continuous improvement.
OwnershipLeader holds accountability; team is subject to it.Team co-owns accountability; leader facilitates.
ToneAssessment and judgement; hierarchical.Inquiry and problem-solving; collaborative.
PM ExperienceAnxiety-producing; done to the individual.Energy-generating; done with the individual.
2.2.2 Accountability for People Performance and Organisational Performance▾

The distinction between compliance and commitment applies to both human and organisational performance.

People Performance▾

A compliance-driven approach tends to emphasise whether employees have met prescribed KPIs, OKRs and behavioural standards.

A commitment-driven approach maintains these expectations while strengthening employees’ understanding of their contribution, ownership of results and participation in performance improvement.

Organisational Performance▾

A compliance-driven approach may establish requirements for systems, resources, processes and governance, but concentrate primarily on whether prescribed arrangements have been followed.

A commitment-driven approach also examines whether those arrangements are effective in supporting the intended results and whether emerging constraints are being addressed.

This distinction becomes particularly important when performance falls short. An employee may miss a target because of inadequate follow-through, an organisational constraint or a combination of both. A credible performance management system distinguishes between these causes before determining the appropriate response.

Commitment strengthens accountability when employees remain responsible for their contributions and organisational leaders remain equally responsible for the enabling conditions within their authority.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE

Teach the two architectures first, then the four dimensions, then how each applies to the people and to the organisational conditions.

Ask: “When a target is missed in your organisation, which of the two architectures does the review conversation sound like?”

Section 3 · Extrapolating — Where

Executive Performance Integration and the Evolution of Performance Management

Where does enterprise performance management become vulnerable? How executive performance perspectives and the evolution of performance management practice shape what leaders see and how they respond.

Section learning outcomes
  • Diagnose executive performance blind spots and determine corrective responses.
  • Redesign performance practices towards continuous, developmental and team-based management.
3.1 — Unity in Diversity: Executive Performance Perspectives
Different executive perspectives, one enterprise result

Section 2 established the four execution bridges that sustain the connection between strategy and results. It also examined how compliance-driven and commitment-driven approaches influence accountability, performance behaviour and management responses.

Section 3 examines where enterprise performance management becomes vulnerable, through two areas:

  • Unity in Diversity: how executive performance perspectives influence what leaders notice, prioritise and interpret.
  • The Continuous Performance Management Shift: how performance management practices are evolving to strengthen timely feedback, development and collective performance.

Enterprise performance management depends on the collective contribution of executive leaders who hold different responsibilities. Each executive function develops a particular understanding of performance based on its mandate, expertise, operating priorities and the evidence it routinely examines. These perspectives are necessary because enterprise performance is multidimensional.

This reflects the principle of functional bias, introduced earlier in CESI: leaders may interpret organisational situations primarily through the priorities and assumptions associated with their professional responsibilities. Within enterprise performance management, functional bias can influence:

  • What executives notice: the performance indicators and developments that attract their attention.
  • What executives prioritise: the results, risks and improvements they consider most important.
  • How executives interpret evidence: the conclusions they draw about organisational performance and its underlying causes.

The challenge for executive teams is to bring these perspectives together so that functional performance information supports a coherent understanding of enterprise results.

Unity in Diversity means integrating different executive performance perspectives into one collective understanding of enterprise progress.

3.1.1 The Ten Executive Performance Hot Zones▾

The ten executive performance perspectives below show the blind spots each may create and the integration required to support enterprise results. The executive team must establish whether the performance reported by individual functions is sufficient to produce the intended collective outcome. This requires attention to people performance and organisational performance.

People Performance▾

Executives examine whether individuals and teams are delivering their agreed KPIs, OKRs and behavioural expectations. They also consider whether the required contributions are being coordinated effectively across functions.

Organisational Performance▾

Executives examine whether the relevant organisational conditions are enabling those contributions. Where organisational constraints affect delivery, the appropriate executive owners must take responsibility for resolving them.

Enterprise · Enterprise Integration▾

The executive team brings the evidence together to determine:

  • whether functional performance is supporting the intended enterprise result;
  • where human contribution or organisational capability is weakening delivery;
  • which executive decisions or coordinated interventions are required.

The S2R® Contribution Map™ provides the established relationships between contributions. The four execution bridges provide the disciplines for sustaining those relationships. Executive integration ensures that the resulting performance evidence is interpreted and acted upon collectively.

3.2 — The Continuous Performance Management Shift
How performance management practices are evolving

Organisations are increasingly recognising the limitations of performance management systems built primarily around periodic assessments and individual ratings. Three important shifts are influencing how performance management is approached.

3.2.1 Three Shifts in Performance Management Practice▾
1. From Annual to Continuous▾

Traditional OrientationPerformance is formally evaluated at annual or biannual intervals.

Evolving OrientationRegular check-ins, periodic reviews and timely feedback support performance throughout execution.

Implication for People and Organisational PerformanceHuman contributions and relevant organisational conditions are reviewed frequently enough to identify emerging gaps and influence results.

2. From Rating-Centric to Development-Centric▾

Traditional OrientationPerformance conversations concentrate heavily on ratings and historical achievement.

Evolving OrientationPerformance evidence informs development, capability strengthening and future improvement.

Implication for People and Organisational PerformanceIndividual development is considered alongside improvements to organisational processes, systems, resources and governance arrangements.

3. From Individual to Team Performance▾

Traditional OrientationPerformance assessment concentrates primarily on individual achievement.

Evolving OrientationIndividual accountability is complemented by attention to team and cross-functional contributions.

Implication for People and Organisational PerformanceHuman collaboration and organisational dependencies become more visible within the performance management system.

These developments reinforce the importance of managing performance while execution is unfolding. They also broaden the performance management conversation:

  • Employee development remains important, but organisations must also improve the operating conditions that influence performance.
  • Individual accountability remains necessary, but enterprise results often require coordinated contributions across teams and functions.
  • Periodic assessments remain relevant, but they must be supported by timely performance conversations that allow leaders to respond to emerging challenges.

Key insight: continuous performance management strengthens the organisation’s ability to recognise, interpret and respond to performance conditions while there is still an opportunity to influence results.

4.1.2 Interpreting the Contribution Map▾

The Contribution Map establishes the relationship between three elements.

1
Functional Contribution — What must the function deliver?

Each function has a defined contribution to the enterprise key result.

2
People Performance — What must individuals and teams deliver?

The map identifies the results and behaviours required from people, supported by measurable performance expectations wherever practical.

3
Organisational Performance — What conditions must the organisation provide?

The map identifies the relevant organisational conditions necessary to enable the human contribution.

Only the organisational conditions that materially affect delivery are included. Some require numerical performance measures, such as system availability or staffing coverage. Others require evidence that an arrangement or capability exists and is functioning, such as documented approval authority or established escalation procedures.

4.2 — Managing the Contributions Through FACES
Applying FACES across the four functions

The Contribution Map™ has established the contributions required from Customer Service, IT, Operations and Workforce Planning, including the expected people performance and the organisational conditions necessary to enable delivery. The next requirement is to manage these contributions consistently throughout execution.

FACES provides the five management functions through which performance expectations are aligned, evidence is examined, accountability is maintained and improvement is supported. The following example demonstrates how each FACES function is achieved in practice across the four business functions.

4.2.1 Applying FACES Across the Four Functions▾
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE
  1. Connect FACES to the Contribution Map. Say: “In the first part of this example, we established what each function must contribute and what the organisation must enable. We now examine how these contributions are managed. FACES provides the five management functions required to sustain delivery.”
  2. Demonstrate how FACES operates across functions. Begin with Customer Service and work through the five FACES functions. Then select IT to demonstrate how the same management functions apply to different performance responsibilities. Emphasise that FACES applies to both employee contributions and organisational enabling conditions.
  3. Examine shared accountability. Ask: “If Customer Service employees are meeting their performance commitments but system outages continue to delay customer resolution, who must take responsibility for the performance gap?” Guide participants to distinguish employee accountability from the organisational responsibility for system reliability.
  4. Connect management action to improvement. Ask: “What should happen when performance reviews repeatedly identify the same approval delays or staffing shortages?” Emphasise that performance management must lead to appropriate decisions, assigned responsibilities and follow-through.
  5. Consolidate the learning. Say: “The Contribution Map establishes the contributions, performance measures and enabling conditions required for enterprise results. FACES establishes the management disciplines through which those contributions are aligned, evaluated, acted upon and continuously strengthened.”
FACES across the four functions · worked example
F — Foster a Results Culture▾

Customer ServiceCustomer enquiries have clear ownership through to resolution. Employees discuss outstanding commitments, explain delays and recognise colleagues who consistently deliver reliable service. Managers demonstrate the same ownership by addressing obstacles raised by their teams.

ITSystem incidents are openly reviewed to understand what happened, how the team responded and what must improve. Employees raise emerging reliability risks early, while IT leadership follows through on recurring infrastructure problems.

OperationsProcessing delays and errors are discussed openly, with attention to their consequences for customer resolution. Employees raise potential bottlenecks early, and managers respond to reported constraints so that they do not recur.

HR / Workforce PlanningStaffing gaps are discussed as performance risks requiring action. Workforce Planning and operational managers jointly examine whether available capacity can meet expected demand and raise emerging shortages before service delivery is affected.

A — Align Performance▾

Customer ServiceIndividual resolution and follow-through commitments are linked to the enterprise 48-hour target. The enquiry management system and escalation arrangements are checked against the requirements for timely resolution.

ITIncident-resolution and communication commitments are linked to the required system availability. Technical support arrangements are matched to the service hours and reliability requirements established in the Contribution Map.

OperationsProcessing turnaround and accuracy expectations are agreed in relation to the time available for customer enquiry resolution. Workflows and approval authority are checked to ensure they support the required turnaround.

HR / Workforce PlanningStaffing forecasts and schedules are based on expected enquiry volumes and the service capacity required. Staffing coverage requirements are agreed with the operational functions that depend on them.

C — Create Measurement Transparency▾

Customer ServiceResolution and follow-through results are visible against agreed standards. Outstanding enquiries show their assigned owners, elapsed time and any unresolved dependencies on other functions.

ITIncident-response results and system availability are reported together. The evidence distinguishes how effectively employees respond to incidents from whether the infrastructure is delivering the required reliability.

OperationsProcessing reports show turnaround, accuracy and the causes of delays. Approval waiting times are visible alongside employee processing performance.

HR / Workforce PlanningStaffing schedules, forecast demand and actual staffing coverage are reviewed together. The evidence shows whether scheduling commitments have been fulfilled and whether sufficient qualified employees are available.

E — Embed Shared Accountability▾

Customer ServiceEach enquiry has an assigned owner responsible for follow-through. Delays requiring IT, Operations or staffing support are recorded against the responsible function, with agreed escalation and resolution arrangements.

ITIncident owners are responsible for response and communication commitments. IT leadership is responsible for system reliability and for securing decisions or resources needed to correct infrastructure constraints.

OperationsProcessing responsibilities are assigned to employees and supervisors. Approval delays are referred to the managers who control the relevant decision rights, with unresolved constraints escalated to the Operations Executive.

HR / Workforce PlanningWorkforce Planning is responsible for forecasts, schedules and coordinating staffing adjustments. Operational managers confirm their staffing requirements, while decisions on additional capacity are referred to the appropriate resource authority.

S — Support Continuous Development▾

Customer ServiceRepeated follow-through gaps are examined using actual enquiry records. Coaching addresses employee capability gaps, while recurring problems with case tracking or escalation lead to improvements in the supporting arrangements. Subsequent results establish whether performance has improved.

ITIncident trends identify technical skills requiring development and recurring infrastructure weaknesses requiring correction. Subsequent incident-response and availability results show whether the improvements have strengthened service reliability.

OperationsProcessing errors inform targeted employee development. Recurring workflow and approval delays inform process or governance improvements. Changes are evaluated against turnaround and accuracy performance.

HR / Workforce PlanningDifferences between forecast and actual demand inform improvements to workforce planning. Recurring staffing shortages lead to adjustments in scheduling, deployment or capacity arrangements, with staffing coverage used to evaluate their effectiveness.

Unit Summary
Unit 6 synthesis
Awareness — What

Performance measurement answers what happened; performance management decides what to do about it. Enterprise performance comes from people performance (results against KPIs and OKRs, and behaviours) and organisational performance (the organisational conditions: environment, systems, resources and governance). The S2R® Contribution Map™ connects an enterprise Key Result to the contribution each function, its people and the organisation must make. FACES names the five functions that manage those contributions across both systems.

Intelligence — Why

Performance management is the execution bridge that keeps strategy connected to performance throughout delivery. Four bridges sustain it: Decomposing the Big Picture, Defining the Logic of Success, Calibrating the Rhythm and Structuring the Dialogue. Compliance-driven and commitment-driven architectures shape how accountability works for people and for the organisation when results fall short.

Extrapolating — Where

Each executive brings a natural performance focus and a potential blind spot. Unity in Diversity integrates the ten executive performance perspectives into one view of enterprise progress. Three shifts are moving performance management from annual to continuous, from rating-centric to development-centric, and from individual to team performance.

Integration — Collective

The worked example mapped one enterprise Key Result across Customer Service, IT, Operations and Workforce Planning: each function’s contribution, what its people must deliver and the organisational conditions that enable them, each with a measure or evidence. FACES then showed how those contributions are managed in practice.

Application — In Practice

Each group built an S2R® Contribution Map™ for one of its own enterprise Key Results, applied FACES to manage each function’s contribution and set the WARD check-up for the Key Result. The agreed record feeds the team’s Capstone S2R Blueprint.

“A performance management system that only measures has not yet become a management system. The moment measurement becomes the basis for a decision, a conversation, and an accountable intervention — that is the moment execution begins.”
