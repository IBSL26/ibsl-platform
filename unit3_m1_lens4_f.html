Unit 5 — Session Overview

Use this tab to prepare before each section. It contains the unit intent, key facilitation questions, and tone and watch points for the full unit.

◆ Unit Intent

Unit 5 establishes the human operating system of execution. Strategy moves through people, and functional expertise, bias and emotion shape how each leader interprets it. The unit builds a shared enterprise interpretation and the behaviours that sustain commitment.

SCARF explains resistance, ACE-IT sets the behaviour standard and COMPASS locates the hot zones. The Section 5 exercise, in which leaders map how their roles connect, is the centrepiece.

The 4 Checks of Mind, Heart, Hands and Habit give leaders one discipline for leading people through the changes the strategy requires: 3S tests Mind, SCARF reads Heart, STAT tests Hands and ACE-IT holds Habit. The Section 5 exercise opens with the Change Transition Map.

◆ Key Facilitation Questions
  • “What is the dominant emotion your team carries into the current strategy, and how does it change what people invest?”
  • “Which SCARF trigger does the current strategy activate most for your people?”
  • “For the most important change in your strategy, which check would your people fail first: Mind, Heart, Hands or Habit?”
  • “Which COMPASS zone would flare first in your organisation under pressure? What is the early warning?”
  • “What behavioural change will you model in the next month so others can see alignment in action?”
◆ Tone & Watch Points

Watch for: compliance mistaken for commitment. Probe: “Would they still act this way if no one were checking?”

Watch for: installation reported as adoption. Probe: “Would the new practice hold on a busy day with no one checking?”

Watch for: emotion treated as a side topic. Connect each emotional state to an execution consequence.

Watch for: commitments that describe intentions. Each behavioural change must be observable by the team.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

What is Aligning Heart & Mind?

Interrogative Anchor · Before any strategy can be executed, it must travel through people. People arrive at every strategy meeting carrying a personality profile, emotional defaults, and personal motives that determine how much energy they will invest in the organisation’s goals.

Section learning outcomes
  • Examine how functional expertise, cognitive bias and emotion shape the interpretation of strategy.
  • Recognise what people need in order to adopt a strategic change, and the behaviours that sustain it.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1 · Awareness

Section intent: Establish the foundational insight that strategy is first and foremost a human interpretation problem. Leaders must feel this before they can act on it. Resist the urge to move to solutions quickly — create space for recognition.

Opening provocation: “Think of a strategy that was perfectly designed and then failed in execution. What broke? Was it the plan — or the people who had to carry it?” Pause. Use silence productively. This question surfaces the lived experience that anchors the entire unit.

On the Five Principles: Work through these sequentially. Each builds on the previous. Do not allow leaders to skip to SCARF or ACE-IT without first establishing the foundational logic.

1.1 — The Human Terrain of Strategy Execution
Foundation
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.1

The Functional Bias Problem: Ask leaders to honestly name their primary perspective. The CFO thinks in capital efficiency. The COO in delivery flow. The CHRO in capability. None is wrong — but when these operate without deliberate integration, strategy fragments invisibly.

Key facilitation question: “When your leadership team last debated a strategic priority — were you all interpreting the same data from the same perspective? Or from the perspective of your function?”

Strategy enters an organisation through the people responsible for interpreting and acting on it. Before strategy becomes operational activity, it passes through two human filters:

Cognition — Mind

How individuals interpret what strategy means. Shaped by professional expertise, mental shortcuts, past experience, and risk perception.

Emotion — Heart

How individuals experience the strategy. Shapes engagement energy, morale, friction load, psychological safety, and values alignment.

The Result

Whether strategy produces clarity or confusion, commitment or compliance, momentum or hesitation. Execution begins inside the human system.

The deeper challenge behind execution friction: Strategy is fundamentally a human interpretation problem. The Functional Bias Problem reveals that organisations do not interpret strategy as a single, shared reality. When perspectives diverge, even highly capable executives can unintentionally pull the organisation in different directions.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: which filter, Mind or Heart, shapes their first response to a new strategic priority, and the implications.

1.2 — The Five Principles of Aligning Heart & Mind
Expand Each Principle
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.2

Pacing: Teach one principle at a time and give its insight before inviting reflection. Do not let the group race through. Principle 2 (Misalignment Fragments Effort) generates the most immediate recognition — use it as a pivot.

After all five: “Which principle describes the most critical gap in your leadership team right now? Choose one.” The choice itself is diagnostic.

Principle 1
Strategy Moves Through People
▼

The human entry point of execution. Alignment of the mind (cognition) and heart (emotion) is the first execution system.

Cognitive alignment focuses on making biases visible so they can be managed deliberately. In practice: identify dominant decision biases at executive level, name how those biases shape the S2R® process, establish shared decision principles that counter predictable distortions.

Emotional alignment requires naming the current emotional state through which strategy is being experienced. Leaders operate from a dominant state of being (Joy, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Disgust, Surprise). Each state influences engagement energy, morale drain, friction load, psychological safety, and values alignment.

➤ Facilitation insight: Ask leaders to name the dominant emotion in their current team right now. If they cannot name it, they cannot manage it — and it will manage them.
Principle 2
Misalignment Fragments Effort
▼

The cost of ignoring the human system. When interpretation and emotional experience are not aligned, effort begins to fragment. Everyone remains active, yet the organisation moves with split effort.

Chinese Whispers

Intent mutates as it travels downward. Frontline execution bears little resemblance to boardroom intent.

Clarification Debt

60–80% of leadership time spent re-explaining, correcting, realigning. Leaders are patching.

Silent Disengagement

People comply outwardly but withdraw inwardly. Looks like cooperation. Feels like exhaustion.

Data: Low-alignment organisations experience 41% lower productivity and 48% higher turnover. This drift is dangerous because it is gradual, rationalised, and expensive (Gallup; PMI).

Principle 3
Alignment Must Be Designed
▼

Alignment rarely emerges naturally. It must be intentionally designed through structured interventions that make interpretation and emotional climate visible. Two diagnostic assessments support this design:

OCEAVL Assessment

Surfaces psychological tendencies influencing executive decision-making. Dimensions: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Emotional Stability, Values Alignment, Learning Orientation. Available on demand — Excel or HTML format with facilitated report generation.

Emotional Climate Assessment

Identifies the prevailing state of being across six core emotional categories: Joy, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Disgust, Surprise. Allows leaders to stabilise engagement before demanding execution. Available on demand.

➤ Facilitation insight: These assessments are diagnostic tools. Their power lies in the conversation they generate. Frame them as leadership mirrors.
Principle 4
Human Reactions Are Predictable
▼

Reactions to strategic change follow predictable psychological patterns. The SCARF Model (David Rock) explains five social conditions that trigger emotional responses during change. The human brain processes social change using the same threat circuitry as physical danger — any new goal, system, or way of working is evaluated emotionally before it is evaluated rationally.

The five SCARF triggers are explored in detail in section 1.5. The SCARF Tool is available on demand for leaders who want to monitor observable behaviours and proactively strengthen their alignment of heart and mind.

➤ Facilitation insight: The power of SCARF lies in the recognition. When a leader can name which trigger is driving resistance, they can address the root cause.
Principle 5
Execution Must Be Visible in Behaviour
▼

Strategic alignment ultimately becomes visible through behaviour. Without observable behaviour, alignment remains theoretical. With behavioural standards, alignment becomes visible, measurable, and sustainable.

The ACE-IT Framework defines five universally desired behaviours: Accountability, Commitment, Engagement, Integrity, Transparency. These behaviours are independent of an organisation’s stated values — they represent the minimum behavioural standard for any leadership team serious about execution. Explored in detail in section 1.7.

➤ Facilitation insight: Ask leaders: “If someone external observed your team for one week, which ACE-IT behaviour would they see most clearly? Which would be absent?” The honest answer is a diagnostic.
◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the principle that describes the most critical gap in their leadership team, and the evidence.

1.3 — Change Management: The 4 Checks of Mind, Heart, Hands and Habit
Expand Each Check
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.3

Section intent: Establish change management as a leadership discipline with a clear test of success. Leaders leave 1.3 able to say which check a change has reached.

Opening provocation: “Think of a change your organisation announced well and still struggled to land. On the day after the announcement, what did you believe was done?” Use the answers to separate the event from the transition.

Set-up for the unit: Each leader writes down one live change their strategy requires of a named group. They carry it through every later part and into the Section 5 exercise.

Key facilitation question: “For that change, which check would your people fail first?” Take three answers. Note which checks are named and which are left out, and return to the list in 1.6.

Watch for: leaders who treat the four checks as four communications. Only the first is about the message.

1.1 showed that strategy reaches people through two filters, Mind and Heart. 1.2 showed that alignment has to be designed. This part applies both to the moment a strategy asks people to work differently.

Change management is the leadership discipline of moving people from current practice to the practice the strategy requires.

The event and the transition

A change is an event. It is announced on a date, and on that date the structure, the system or the target is different. The transition is what each affected person goes through afterwards: letting go of a familiar practice, working out what the new one asks of them, and becoming able to do it. The event belongs to the organisation. The transition belongs to the person.

A leadership team debates a change for months before announcing it. Leaders finish their own transition on the day everyone else begins theirs. The distance between the two is where impatience on one side meets resistance on the other. Change management is the work of leading people across that distance.

The 4 Checks of Mind, Heart, Hands and Habit

A change is adopted when it passes four checks for the people it affects. Mind and Heart are the two filters from 1.1. Hands and Habit extend the same path to the point where the new practice is real. Each check asks one question and has one tool. Expand each check.

Check 1 · Mind
They understand the change, why it matters and what they do differently.
▼

Understanding is tested by what people can say back in their own words. An announcement shows that the leader has spoken. The check shows what the people have understood, after each of them has passed the message through their own expertise, shortcuts, experience and sense of risk.

Tool: the 3S Check (1.4).

✓ Passed when: people in the same team describe the change in the same way.
⚠ Failed when: people repeat the slogan and cannot say what they personally do differently.
Check 2 · Heart
They want it to succeed.
▼

Wanting is a decision each person makes. It is the ignition point described in Section 2: “This matters to me.” Most changes threaten something for the people they touch: standing, certainty, control, belonging or fairness. Until that threat is addressed, a person who understands the change holds back their energy.

Tool: the SCARF Model (1.5).

✓ Passed when: people bring problems and ideas to the change.
⚠ Failed when: the room is quiet, effort is minimal and people say they are “waiting for alignment”.
Check 3 · Hands
They are able to do it.
▼

Ability is practical. It rests on four things: skill, time, authority and tools. A person can understand a change and want it, and still be unable to do it on an ordinary working day. From a distance, that person looks the same as one who is unwilling.

Tool: the STAT Check (1.6).

✓ Passed when: people do the new work unaided.
⚠ Failed when: workarounds appear, decisions are passed upward and the new practice is done last.
Check 4 · Habit
It has become the way they work.
▼

A practice becomes habit when it is repeated, noticed and expected. Until then it depends on effort and attention, and both fall away under pressure. The five ACE-IT behaviours are what hold a new practice in place after the launch has passed.

Tool: ACE-IT (1.7).

✓ Passed when: the practice holds when the leader is absent.
⚠ Failed when: the old way returns under the first pressure.

Why the order matters

Each check depends on the one before it. A person cannot want a change they have misread. They will not perform a change they do not want, and skill offered to an unwilling person is left unused. A practice cannot become habit while most people are unable to perform it.

The practical rule follows. A change stalls at the first check it fails, and that check is where the leader’s effort belongs. Effort spent further along the path is lost.

  • A team that understands a change and resents it has stalled at Heart. More explanation will not move it.
  • A team that wants a change and has no authority to act has stalled at Hands. More encouragement will not move it.
  • A team that did it well for a month and drifted back has stalled at Habit. A relaunch will not hold it.

The leader’s four jobs

Leading the transition means doing one job at each check. Each job is taught with its tool in the four parts that follow.

➤ Insight: For the last change you announced, which check has it reached today? If you cannot name the check, the change has been announced and left to chance.
◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: one change their strategy requires of a named group, and how far the leaders and the people affected were through their own transition on the day it was announced. They carry this change through the unit.

1.4 — The 3S Check: Shift, Stake, Step
Three Questions
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.4

Live demonstration: Ask two leaders to write the Shift, the Stake and the Step for the same change, without conferring, and post them in the chat. The difference between the two versions is the lesson. Two leaders with different versions pass on two different changes.

On the Stake: Check that each Stake gives both sides. Where only the gain is given, ask: “What happens to this team in a year if nothing changes?”

Watch for: Explain rated as complete because an announcement went out. Probe: “What would a front-line employee say back to you?”

Watch for: a Step written for the organisation. Ask the leader to say it in the first person, as a member of the affected group.

3S is the Mind check. It asks whether the people affected have understood the change as the leader intended it. Understanding is interpretation. Each person rebuilds the message through their own expertise, mental shortcuts, past experience and risk perception. The Functional Bias Problem from 1.1 applies in full: finance hears a cost decision, operations hears a process change, and the front line hears a judgement on how they have worked until now.

Leaders are poorly placed to judge this for themselves. After months inside a change, its logic feels obvious to them, and an obvious message feels like a clear one. The 3S Check replaces that feeling with a test. Mind passes when the people affected can say three things about the change in their own words.

Shift
“What is changing?”
What It Is
The Shift names the practice that ends and the practice that begins, for a named group, from a named date. It is concrete enough to be observed: someone watching the work could tell whether it is happening. A direction such as “more customer-focused” leaves every person to decide what it means, and each will decide differently.
Weak
“We are becoming more customer-focused.”
Clear
“From March, agents resolve complaints on the first call.”
Shows Up As
People describe the old practice under a new name • Different teams describe different changes
⚠ Execution: Chinese Whispers. The change mutates as it travels, and each function executes its own version.
➤ Leader’s move: State the Shift as from and to: what stops, what starts, for whom, from when.
Stake
“What do we gain if we change, and what do we lose if we stay as we are?”
What It Is
The Stake is why the change matters, and it has two sides. The gain says what the change is for. The loss says what standing still will cost. Both are needed, because people weigh a loss more heavily than an equal gain. A change described only by its benefits leaves the people affected holding the one visible loss: the practice they are asked to give up. Naming what is lost by staying put places a second loss on the scales. The Stake is stated in terms the affected group cares about. An enterprise benefit that never reaches their work reads as someone else’s reason.
Weak
“It supports our strategy.”
Clear
“We keep the customers who now leave after a two-day wait. If we stay as we are, we keep losing them.”
Shows Up As
“Because head office said so” • People can describe the change and cannot say what it is for
⚠ Execution: Compliance without conviction. The change is carried out while it is watched and set aside under pressure.
➤ Leader’s move: State the gain and the loss together, in the group’s own terms.
Step
“What do I do differently?”
What It Is
The Step is the person’s own part. It is said in the first person and is small enough to do next week. The Shift belongs to the organisation and the Step belongs to the person, so each role affected needs its own. A change with a clear Shift and no Step is understood as something that will happen to other people.
Weak
“Embrace the new process.”
Clear
“I decide on the call, within my limit.”
Shows Up As
“I am waiting to be told” • People agree with the change and carry on as before
⚠ Execution: Agreement without movement. The change is endorsed in meetings and absent from daily work.
➤ Leader’s move: Give each affected role its own Step, and ask people to say it back.

How to run the 3S Check

  1. Write your own Shift, Stake and Step for the change.
  2. Ask three people from the affected group to say theirs, without prompting.
  3. Compare their versions with yours and with each other.

Reading the result

What you hearWhat it tells you
The versions match.Mind passes. Move to the Heart check.
Same Shift, different Stake.People know what is changing and disagree on why. Restate the Stake in their terms.
Shift and Stake are clear, and there is no Step.People see the change as someone else’s. Give each role its own Step.
Different Shifts.Chinese Whispers. Return to one message, said in the same words by every leader.

Where a version differs from yours, the person’s own filter has reshaped it. The difference is information. It shows which part of the message to restate, and to whom.

The leader’s job at this check · Mind — Explain

The job: give people a version of the change they can repeat. State the Shift, the Stake and the Step in the same words each time, in every forum, until people can say them back. Repetition feels excessive to the leader long before the message has landed.

Complete when: three people from the affected group give the same Shift, Stake and Step.

Sounds like: “From March, complaints are resolved on the first call. We are losing customers who wait two days for an answer. Your part is to decide on the call, within your limit.”

⚠ When skipped: people fill the gap with their own version, and Clarification Debt builds: leadership time is spent re-explaining a change that was never understood the first time.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the Shift, the Stake and the Step for that change, and which of the three their people would find hardest to say back.

1.5 — The SCARF Model: Five Triggers of Human Resistance
Expand Each Trigger
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.5

Framing: Open by naming SCARF as the Heart check.

On pacing: Give each trigger with its question. Then pause: “In your last major strategy implementation — which of these triggers did you observe most? How did you know?”

Key distinction: The SCARF triggers activate universally. Leaders who manage it well are distinguished by awareness and deliberate counter-strategy.

On involvement: Involvement is about how the change is done. The decision that it will be done stays with the leader. Leaders who blur the two invite a debate they did not intend.

SCARF is the Heart check. When a trigger fires, a person who understands the change still withholds their energy from it.

Expand each trigger to explore what activates it, how it shows up in behaviour, its execution impact, and the counter-strategy that addresses it.

Status
“Will this reduce my importance?”
Activated By
New leaders • New KPIs exposing performance • New systems shifting expertise • Changed reporting lines
Shows Up As
Defensive behaviour • Undermining decisions • Withholding information or cooperation
⚠ Execution: Political friction • Slowed decisions • Misalignment masked as professionalism
➤ Counter: Explicitly acknowledge existing credibility. Involve senior leaders in co-designing new structures — status is protected when people have agency in the change.
Certainty
“Do I know what success looks like?”
Activated By
New goals • Ambiguous ownership • Changing metrics or timelines • Shifting review processes
Shows Up As
Repeated clarification requests • Delay disguised as “waiting for alignment” • Reversion to old habits
⚠ Execution: Loss of momentum • Paralysis under pressure • Risk avoidance
➤ Counter: Create visible milestones early. The brain seeks pattern — give it enough structure to move without removing all ambiguity.
Autonomy
“Am I losing control of my work?”
Activated By
New systems • Standardised processes • Centralised decision rights • Increased oversight or reporting
Shows Up As
Passive compliance • Minimal effort delivery • Resistance through bureaucracy
⚠ Execution: Low engagement energy • Mechanical execution • Fragile performance
➤ Counter: Standardise the what and offer bounded choice on the how. Bounded choice preserves autonomy while honouring the mandate.
Relatedness
“Do I still belong here?”
Activated By
Team restructuring • New leaders or external hires • Cross-functional initiatives • Remote or hybrid shifts
Shows Up As
Silo behaviour • Reduced collaboration • Guarded communication
⚠ Execution: Trust erosion • Coordination breakdown • Rework and duplication
➤ Counter: Create visible connection rituals during transitions. People need to feel part of the tribe before they will share resources or risk vulnerability.
Fairness
“Is this being applied equitably?”
Activated By
Uneven workload • Selective enforcement of rules • Inconsistent rewards • Unclear decision rationale
Shows Up As
Cynicism • Gossip replacing dialogue • Emotional withdrawal
⚠ Execution: Values misalignment • Morale drain • Cultural decay
➤ Counter: Proactively explain rationale behind decisions — especially asymmetric ones. Transparency about trade-offs is perceived as fairness even when outcomes differ.
The leader’s job at this check · Heart — Involve

The job: give people a hand in shaping how the change lands. The leader decides what changes and opens up how it is done. Name plainly what people give up. Identify the live SCARF trigger and apply its counter. Involve their managers first, because managers carry the change into every team conversation.

Why it works: a part in the decision answers each trigger. Being consulted restores Status. Seeing the plan early restores Certainty. Choosing how restores Autonomy. Working on it together restores Relatedness. Hearing the reasons restores Fairness.

Complete when: the people affected can point to a part of the change they shaped.

Sounds like: “You know these calls better than I do. Where should the decision limit sit?”

⚠ When skipped: Silent Disengagement. People comply, do the minimum and wait to be chased.

SCARF Tool

Available on demand for leaders who want to actively monitor observable behaviours during strategy discussions or change initiatives. Helps identify the underlying social concerns influencing reactions — enabling leaders to adjust their approach in real time.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: the SCARF trigger most often activated during strategy implementation, the evidence and the counter-strategy.

1.6 — The STAT Check: Skill, Time, Authority, Tools
Four Elements
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.6

Demonstration: Take one leader’s change and run the four questions in front of the group. Stay longest on Authority. Ask: “What can this person now decide without asking you?”

Return to 1.3: Revisit the checks leaders named as most likely to fail. Ask whether any of the “resistance” they described could be a Hands gap.

Watch for: training offered as the answer to every Hands gap. Ask which STAT element the training closes.

STAT is the Hands check. It asks whether people who understand a change and want it are able to carry it out on an ordinary working day. Mind and Heart sit inside the person. Hands sits largely in the conditions around the person, and those conditions are set by leaders.

This is the check most easily misread. A person who is unable to perform a change looks, from a distance, like a person who is unwilling: the new practice is late, partial or missing. A leader who reads that as resistance answers with more persuasion, when the gap is a missing decision right or a system that still fits the old way. STAT separates the two. Hands passes when all four elements are in place.

Skill
“Do they know how to do it?”
What It Is
Skill is the practised ability to do the new work to standard. It is built by doing the work with feedback. A briefing transfers information, and information becomes skill only after a person has performed the practice several times and been corrected.
Shows Up As
Errors and rework • Reliance on one or two experts • People reverting to the old method “to get it done”
⚠ Execution: Quality falls at the moment the change needs to prove itself, and the old way looks safer.
➤ Leader’s move: Teach, practise on real work and coach on the job. Pair the experts with the rest.
Time
“Do they have the hours to do it?”
What It Is
Time is the working capacity to perform the new practice properly. Most changes are added to a full day. When nothing is removed, the new work competes with the old, and the old work has the deadlines, the habits and the measures on its side.
Shows Up As
The new practice is done last • Done in a rush • Dropped on a busy day
⚠ Execution: The change is alive in quiet weeks and absent in busy ones.
➤ Leader’s move: Take old work away before adding new work, and say what stops.
Authority
“Are they allowed to decide and act?”
What It Is
Authority is the permission to decide and act as the change requires. Many changes move decisions closer to the customer or the front line. Until the limit of the new decision is stated, the safest course for the individual is to ask permission, and the decision travels back up to where it used to sit.
Shows Up As
Escalations • People ask permission for what the change asks them to do
⚠ Execution: The change runs slower than the practice it replaced, and leaders become the bottleneck.
➤ Leader’s move: Set the decision limit, state it openly, and back the first decisions made under it. The first decision that is overruled teaches everyone to keep asking.
Tools
“Do they have the systems and resources?”
What It Is
Tools are the systems, information and resources the new practice depends on. A practice has to be possible in the system people use every day. Where the system, the form or the budget still fits the old way, people build workarounds, and a workaround is the old practice surviving inside the new one.
Shows Up As
Spreadsheets beside the system • Manual steps • Requests waiting on another team
⚠ Execution: Two versions of the truth, extra effort and errors that stay hidden.
➤ Leader’s move: Provide access and budget before the start date, and retire the old form or route.

One missing element is enough to stop people who understand and want the change. Training closes a Skill gap. It leaves a Time, Authority or Tools gap exactly where it was.

The leader’s job at this check · Hands — Equip

The job: make the new practice possible on an ordinary working day. Run the STAT Check with the people affected, since they know what is missing. Supply it, and remove the old work, rules and approvals that stand in the way.

Complete when: a person can perform the new practice from start to finish without asking permission or using a workaround.

Sounds like: “What do you need from me to do this on Monday?”

⚠ When skipped: willing people fail, and their failure is read as resistance. Heart, which had passed, begins to fail as well.

➤ Insight: Which of the four is your organisation quickest to supply, and which is it slowest to grant?
◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: STAT for that change: the missing element, the evidence and what is theirs to supply.

1.7 — ACE-IT: The Observable Behaviour Standard
Expand Each Behaviour
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.7

Framing: Open by naming ACE-IT as the Habit check.

Key framing: ACE-IT is a behavioural standard, independent of the organisation’s stated values. It describes the minimum visible behaviours required for strategic alignment to be real and visible.

After all five: “If you audited your last leadership review against these five behaviours — where would you find the biggest gap between the ideal and the observable?”

Closing Section 1: Ask each leader to state, for the change they are carrying, the check they expect it to fail first and the job that follows. This sentence is the bridge into Section 2.

ACE-IT is the Habit check. These five behaviours are what hold a new practice in place.

A
Accountability
▼
Definition
Taking full ownership for results. Acknowledging missteps and driving corrective action.
Desired Behaviour
Defines clear ownership • Acknowledges missteps • Holds self and others to measurable standards
✓ Observable: Commitments tracked • Reviews end with named owners • “We missed it, here’s the fix” language common
C
Commitment
▼
Definition
Staying anchored to agreed strategic intent even when pressure rises. Following through despite discomfort.
Desired Behaviour
Aligns daily choices to long-term direction • Protects priorities from noise • Closes loops they open
✓ Observable: Strategic priorities stay consistent • Leaders decline non-strategic work • Promises kept visibly
E
Engagement
▼
Definition
Actively involving self and others in sense-making, dialogue, and execution. Listening to understand.
Desired Behaviour
Invites participation and dissent • Connects roles to purpose • Creates two-way dialogue
✓ Observable: Team meetings are two-way • Feedback loops visible • People quote the shared “why”
I
Integrity
▼
Definition
Acting consistently with values, facts, and truth — even when inconvenient. Declaring conflicts of interest early.
Desired Behaviour

How ACE-IT holds a new practice

Habit is the last check and the one asked earliest. The question is put before the change starts: how will the five behaviours hold the new practice in place? A change with no answer has no plan for the day after launch.

AccountabilityOne named person owns the practice and its result.

The leader’s job at this check · Habit — Reinforce

The job: make the new practice the expected practice. Show it yourself first. Notice it when it happens, recognise it by name and correct a slip early. Put it into the regular review so that it is asked about every time. What leaders ask about, people repeat. A practice that is never asked about is understood to be optional.

Complete when: the practice holds through a full review period with the leader absent.

Sounds like: “I saw you close that case on the call. That is the standard.”

⚠ When skipped: the old way returns under the first pressure, and the organisation concludes that the change was a phase.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why Does Heart & Mind Alignment Determine Execution Outcomes?

Ignition Point · Within the Strategy2Results® process, Aligning Heart & Mind functions as the ignition point of execution — the moment where strategy transitions from a document describing intent into a system capable of producing movement.

Section learning outcomes
  • Explain why shared understanding and commitment convert strategy into execution.
  • Distinguish commitment from compliance and explain why change delivers value only when it is adopted.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2 · Intelligence

Section intent: Move from describing the concept of alignment to understanding its strategic necessity. Leaders must understand why this is the mechanical conversion point of strategy. This section should create a shift from intellectual understanding to operational conviction.

The energy analogy: The Potential vs. Kinetic Energy logic is highly effective with CXO-level audiences. Engineers, finance leaders, and operators all recognise this from their domain. Use it as an anchor before moving to the compliance/commitment distinction.

2.1 — The Ignition Point of Strategy Execution
Foundation Logic
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2.1

On the ignition metaphor: An ignition point is where fuel becomes power. A strategy document is stored potential. The moment a person decides “This matters to me” is when kinetic energy begins. Without that moment, the fuel stays fuel.

Discussion prompt: “Think of a time when you personally chose to go beyond compliance and genuinely committed to a strategic direction. What caused that shift? What would it take to create that same shift in your team?”

A strategy document contains strategic potential. It articulates direction, ambition, and possibility. However, potential by definition represents energy at rest. For strategic potential to convert into kinetic energy — movement, execution, and results — human action must occur. No system executes itself.

Execution begins the moment an individual decides: “This matters to me. I am willing to invest energy in making this succeed.” Until that moment occurs across the organisation, strategy remains theoretical. Aligning Heart & Mind activates the human energy required to convert intention into action. This is the ignition point.

Without this ignition point, strategy remains defined but not delivered. With it, the organisation shifts from supervised effort to self-driven momentum — the strategic payoff that makes alignment the highest-leverage leadership intervention available.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants write a reflection in their own file: a time they committed to a strategic direction beyond compliance, what caused the shift and what would create it in their team.

2.2 — Three Foundational Logics
Why It Matters Operationally
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2.2

Logic II (Compliance vs. Commitment) typically generates the highest impact with CXO audiences. Leaders recognise compliance-driven execution immediately — they have managed it and been frustrated by it. Ask: “How much of your leadership time is currently spent enforcing compliance?” The honest answer is usually sobering.

I. Potential vs. Kinetic Energy Logic

A strategy document is strategic potential — energy at rest. For it to become kinetic energy (movement, execution, results), a human decision must activate it. The alignment of heart and mind is precisely the conversion mechanism. Every day that decision is not made is a day strategic potential sits unused. The organisation does not move until people decide to move it. Leadership cannot shortcut this decision — it can only create the conditions for it.

II. Compliance vs. Commitment Logic
Compliance — Mind Only

People act because authority requires it, rules enforce it, or systems monitor it. Produces visible output short-term. Requires continuous supervision. Creates hidden costs: leadership time consumed by follow-ups, teams dependent on instruction, decision-making slows as control increases. Mechanical and brittle.

Commitment — Mind & Heart

People act because they understand strategic intent, believe the direction is meaningful, and feel personal ownership. Generates behaviours compliance cannot sustain: discretionary effort, adaptive problem-solving, resilience when conditions change. Execution continues even when leaders are not present.

III. The Strategic Payoff of Alignment

When both cognition and emotion are aligned: people act with ownership, coordination improves across functions, and leaders spend less time correcting and more time advancing strategy. This shift reduces rework, lowers escalation, and preserves leadership capacity for decisions that truly require executive attention. Aligning Heart & Mind is the mechanical conversion point where strategic potential becomes strategic movement.

2.3 — Why Change Must Be Led: Installed and Adopted
The Value Logic
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2.3

Key distinction: Installation is a project milestone and adoption is a behaviour. Ask which of the two the leadership team currently reports on.

Link to 2.2: Return to the energy logic. An installed change is potential energy. Adoption is the point at which it becomes kinetic.

Discussion prompt: “Name one change that is installed and still awaiting adoption. What has the wait cost in rework, supervision or lost benefit?” Ask for a figure, even a rough one.

Watch for: the gap blamed on the people affected. Ask which check failed, and whose job that check was.

2.2 described a strategy as potential energy that a human decision converts into movement. A change follows the same logic. On the day it is installed, its value is still potential. The value is earned later, when people work the new way without being watched.

Installed

The new system, structure or KPI is in place, and people have been told.

Installation is a project outcome. It can be planned, funded and delivered on a date, and it is easy to report as success.

What you see: the launch is complete, the training register is signed and the dashboard is live.

What it yields: cost. The organisation has paid for the change and is still working the old way.

Adopted

People work the new way without supervision. The change has passed all four checks.

Adoption is a behavioural outcome. It arrives group by group, at the pace of each group’s slowest check, and no launch date can declare it.

What you see: the new practice holds on a busy day, people correct each other, and the old workaround has gone.

What it yields: value. The benefit the strategy counted on is being earned.

The adoption gap

The time between installed and adopted is the adoption gap. While it lasts, the organisation runs two practices at once: the new one it has built and the old one people still use. Supervision rises, because compliance has to be checked. The benefits counted when the strategy was approved remain unearned.

The gap also has a cost that outlasts the change itself. Every change that is installed and left unadopted teaches the organisation that a change can be waited out. The next change starts with less belief, and Heart is harder to pass.

The gap closes one check at a time. Compliance can install a change. Commitment is what carries it through to Habit, and adoption holds when leaders notice and reinforce the new practice.

Three signals a change is installed and awaiting adoption

  1. Use of the new practice falls when the leader is absent.
  2. Reports are complete and the result has not moved.
  3. People call it “the new system” and describe their own work as unchanged.

Data: 81% of those who planned for reinforcement met or exceeded their project objectives (Prosci, Best Practices in Change Management, 12th edition).

3.3 — Change Readiness Across the Organisation
Readiness Scan
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 3.3

Process: Participants rate their own change on their own, using the rating guide. Then pair participants who share an affected group to compare their ratings.

Key facilitation move: Where two leaders rate the same group differently, leave it unresolved and name it: “You are leading the same people through the same change with two different readings.”

On the worked scan: Draw attention to the team leaders. They score best on Mind and are the group most likely to block the change. Understanding a change and wanting it are separate checks.

Watch for: one plan for every group. Ask which group the plan was written for.

COMPASS shows where the organisation is exposed. Readiness shows how far each group of people has come through the 4 Checks for one specific change. The two are read together.

Readiness belongs to a group and a change together. The same group can be ready for one change and unready for another. The same change finds different groups at different checks, because it asks something different of each: one group gives up a decision, another gains one, a third carries on as before and feels overlooked.

How to run the scan

  1. Name one change and list the groups it affects.
  2. Run the 4 Checks for each group. Rate each check from 1 (fully in place) to 5 (missing).
  3. Find each group’s first check that scores 4 or 5. Leadership effort for that group starts there.

Reading the scan

Contact-centre agents

Start at Hands. Agents understand the change and broadly accept it. They have no decision limit and no access to the refunds system. Further communication adds nothing for this group until authority and tools are in place.

Team leaders

Start at Heart. Team leaders understand the change best of all. It moves decisions from them to their agents, which fires Status and Autonomy. Until that is addressed, they are unlikely to coach agents to use the new authority, and the agents’ Hands gap will stay open.

Back-office specialists

Start at Mind. Specialists have the ability and have not been told what the change means for their own work. A strong Hands score has no effect while Mind fails.

One change, three groups, three starting points. A single plan written for every group serves one of them.

Three misreadings to avoid

  1. Reading quiet as ready. A group that complies and stays silent scores 3 on Heart. Silence is the absence of information.
  2. Reading a Hands gap as resistance. Late or partial practice looks the same whether people are unwilling or unable. Run STAT before concluding.
  3. Averaging the scores. A group strong on three checks and failing one is stalled at that one. The first failing check decides, and an average hides it.

Readiness and COMPASS

Now place each group in its COMPASS zone from 3.1. Low readiness inside a High Flammable Zone marks where a change is most likely to cross the escalation threshold and become a Burning Platform. Those groups come first.

Building Collective Intelligence Through Shared Interpretation

Application · This exercise highlights how Collective Intelligence emerges when leaders understand each other’s alignment responsibilities. The goal: move from individual interpretations to a shared understanding of how roles jointly stabilise all seven COMPASS domains. The exercise starts from two real changes your strategy requires. Steps 1 to 3 are Portfolio work. Step 4 is Capstone work: the group’s confirmed outputs feed each team’s Capstone Blueprint.

Section learning outcomes
  • Formulate how one strategic change will be led and how leadership roles connect to support it.
  • Demonstrate commitment through a behavioural change that strengthens collective alignment.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 5 · Application

Order: Steps 1 and 2 are individual. Step 3 is a paired exchange. Step 4 is the plenary synthesis. Do not rush Steps 1–3. The quality of the plenary depends entirely on the depth of individual reflection and peer dialogue that precedes it.

Facilitation principle: Your role in the plenary is to name patterns. When leaders disagree about connectivity, name it explicitly: “That disagreement tells us something important about how alignment responsibilities are currently experienced in this team.”

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Step 1

Briefing: Each person works independently and completes the map once for each change. Leaders use the change they carried through the unit as their first map.

Watch for: “Staff” or “the business” as the affected group. Ask for the team whose Monday morning changes.

Watch for: a committee or a function named as owner. Hold out for one person.

The map turns the 4 Checks into a plan for one change: who it affects, where it will stall, what they need and who answers for it.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

After the lesson, each participant completes the Change Transition Map alone in Step 1 of their own file, as portfolio work:

  1. They choose two changes the strategy requires of people: one practice people must start and one they must stop, taken from the Start and Stop lists the group confirmed in Unit 3. Their page shows the two lists.
  2. They read the worked example.
  3. They complete the eight fields for each change: the change, who is most affected, Mind · 3S, Heart · SCARF, Hands · STAT, Habit · ACE-IT, the weakest check and the leader who owns it.

The maps become part of the participant’s Learning Portfolio. They reach you when the participant selects Submit to Facilitator at the end of the unit. Check that each map names a specific group, a specific practice and one person as owner.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Step 2

Briefing: Each person works independently, with the two changes they mapped in Step 1 in view. They complete the table for their own role first, then for the two roles they depend on most. The “How I Create Collective Intelligence” column must go beyond job description — it should describe the alignment mechanism the role provides to the organisation.

Watch for: Participants who list tasks; push them to name alignment contributions. Redirect: “That’s what you do — what happens to the organisation’s alignment when you do it well? And when you don’t?”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Each participant completes the table alone in Step 2 of their own file, as portfolio work: their own role first, then the two roles they depend on most. Each row names the role, the two COMPASS domains it most directly activates and one statement on alignment.

The table becomes part of the participant’s Learning Portfolio. Check that each row names a specific COMPASS domain and a concrete collaboration behaviour.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Step 3

Process: Each leader shares their map and their table with the colleague(s) whose role they selected. Four exchange questions: (1) Do you agree with how I described your alignment contribution? (2) How else do you create Collective Intelligence that I missed? (3) Where do our interpretations diverge? (4) Do you agree with the weakest check and the owner I named for each change?

Key facilitation move: Where leaders disagree, do not resolve it. Name it explicitly: “That disagreement is diagnostic. It tells you something about how these roles are currently experienced in the organisation.”

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants work in pairs from closely related roles, as portfolio work. Each shares the map and the table, the pair works through the four exchange questions, and each participant types their own notes in their own file.

Then ask each pair where their interpretations diverged. Treat divergence as diagnostic and leave it unresolved.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Step 4

Synthesis question: “Looking across all the tables — which COMPASS domain appears most frequently as underactivated or poorly understood? That is your team’s most critical alignment vulnerability.”

Second synthesis question: “Which group appears on the most maps? What sequence does this team agree for the changes landing on it?”

Closing commitment: Each leader names one specific behavioural change in the next 30 days to strengthen their alignment contribution. Ask each leader to tie the commitment to one of their mapped changes: the behaviour they will show first. Make these visible: each participant posts the commitment in the chat. Follow up at the next leadership forum.

◆ PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Participants work as a group, in Step 4 of their own file. This is Capstone work. The group agrees each entry and one member acts as scribe. Every member then types the agreed entries into their own page, in the session or after it.

  1. The group agrees the COMPASS domain, or domains, most often underactivated.
  2. The group agrees where alignment is already strong.
  3. The group agrees which group of people appears on the most maps, and the order and pace for the changes landing on it.
  4. Each participant reads the record and selects Confirm. Print gives a copy of the record.

The confirmed work feeds the team’s Capstone Blueprint. Each participant then writes one 30-day behavioural commitment in their own file, as portfolio work. Close by asking each participant to post that commitment in the chat.

Unit Outcome: The organisation leaves this session with aligned leadership interpretation, emotional commitment to the strategy, clear visibility of the conditions that will enable or constrain execution, and a transition mapped for two of the changes the strategy requires — the human foundation for the next stage of the Strategy2Results® journey.

Unit Summary
Unit 5 synthesis
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Unit Summary

Closing the unit: Use this summary to close the session, to brief sponsors or observers beforehand, or to re-anchor the group at the start of a later session. Close with one pointed question: “Which COMPASS domain is this team most likely rating too generously — and which executive role has quietly stopped carrying its alignment responsibility there?” It is also the reference point for connecting this unit to what follows — Performance Management Setup and the translation of strategy into individual accountability across the enterprise.

This session was about the human conditions that decide whether a strategy ever moves. We have all watched sound strategies stall because the people meant to execute it never aligned in how they interpreted it or whether they were committed to it. Aligning Heart & Mind treated execution as first a human problem: strategy moves through people before it moves through systems.

Here is a recap of how we moved from fragmented functional interpretations to a single, committed leadership signal.

1 — Awareness: Strategy Enters Through Two Human Filters

We began with the What. Strategy enters through Cognition (Mind), how each leader interprets it, and Emotion (Heart), how each leader experiences it. When these diverge across a team, the Functional Bias Problem fragments execution into competing interpretations. The unit established Five Principles:

  • Strategy Moves Through People — direction is carried by interpretation.
  • Misalignment Fragments Effort — surfacing as Chinese Whispers, Clarification Debt, and Silent Disengagement.
  • Alignment Must Be Designed — diagnosed with the OCEAVL Assessment and the Emotional Climate Assessment.
  • Human Reactions Are Predictable — the SCARF Model (Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness) trips the same threat circuitry as physical danger.
  • Execution Must Be Visible in Behaviour — the ACE-IT standard: Accountability, Commitment, Engagement, Integrity, Transparency.
2 — Intelligence: Why Alignment Is the Ignition Point

This stage explored the Why. Heart & Mind alignment is the ignition point that converts strategic potential energy (the document at rest) into kinetic energy (movement and results). Three logics make the stakes operational:

  • Potential vs Kinetic Energy — the organisation does not move until people decide to move it.
  • Compliance vs Commitment — compliance (Mind only) yields short-term output but is mechanical and consumes leadership time; commitment (Mind + Heart) generates discretionary effort, adaptive problem-solving, and resilience when leaders are not present.
  • The Strategic Payoff — aligned teams act with ownership, coordinate across functions, and free leaders to advance strategy.
3 — Extrapolating: Where Misalignment Concentrates

Next, the Where. Misalignment concentrates in seven High Flammable Zones, mapped by the COMPASS framework:

  • Clarity of Strategic Direction
  • Organisational Alignment
  • Management Discipline
  • People & Capability
  • Allocation of Resources
  • Sensing & Adaptation
  • Systems & Execution

Each domain carries a signal narrative and a diagnostic question, and is rated 1 (strong alignment) to 5 (severe misalignment). A lit zone is either a High Flammable — a live leverage point — or a Burning Platform, escalated and consuming resources. Mapping where the signals are lit tells leaders where alignment work earns the highest return.

4 — Integration: Collective Intelligence in the Converging Zone

The session culminated in the Converging Zone, where functional interpretations integrate into a shared leadership signal and the team begins operating as a single leadership system. Each CXO role carries a distinct alignment responsibility, mapped to two primary COMPASS activations:

  • CEO — anchors Clarity of Strategic Direction and Organisational Alignment
  • CFO / CPO — activate Allocation of Resources
  • COO / CTO / CRO — drive Systems & Execution
  • CHRO — carries People & Capability
  • CMO / CSO — maintain Clarity and Sensing & Adaptation

When any role drops its responsibility, the COMPASS framework loses an anchor and the rest cannot fully converge. Collective Intelligence is the combined signal.

5 — Application: Mapping the Transition

Each participant mapped the transition for two changes the strategy requires, set out how their role connects to the two roles they depend on most, and tested both with a colleague. The group then agreed the COMPASS domain most often underactivated, where alignment is already strong, and the sequence for the changes landing on the same group. The confirmed work feeds each team’s Capstone Blueprint.

Across the Unit — Leading People Through Change

We also took on the discipline of leading people through change. A change is an event, and the transition is what each person goes through afterwards. A change is adopted when it passes the 4 Checks:

  • Mind — people can say the Shift, the Stake and the Step.
  • Heart — no SCARF trigger is holding them back.
  • Hands — they have the skill, time, authority and tools.
  • Habit — the ACE-IT behaviours hold the new practice in place.

A change stalls at the first check it fails, and the leader’s job is to explain, involve, equip and reinforce until it passes all four.

Aligned hearts and minds turn a strategy from a document the organisation has read into a direction the organisation is willing to move on — the human foundation for Performance Management Setup and the translation of strategy into individual accountability across the enterprise.
