Performance Measurement

Making Execution Visible, Correctable & Continuous

Strategy fails when progress is not consistently examined. This unit establishes the review discipline that keeps strategy execution visible, surfaces early warning signals, and converts performance data into organisational intelligence through the Review–Realign–Restore cycle.

Key learning outcomes Interpret performance evidence in relation to strategic objectives and the wider organisational context. Distinguish meaningful strategic progress from activity, compliance and short-term fluctuation. Exercise balanced executive judgement when performance differs from agreed expectations. Derive relevant decisions, organisational learning and leadership action from performance evidence.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

What is Performance Measurement?

Establishing performance measurement as an execution visibility discipline and introducing the Review–Realign–Restore Cycle as the mechanism that keeps strategy correctable.

Section learning outcomes
  • Distinguish meaningful strategic progress from activity.
  • Explain how performance evidence converts into realignment and corrective action.
1.1 — The Execution Visibility Infrastructure
Are we actually moving closer to our Success in Practice?

Your performance management system has been set and the mechanisms for establishing performance are in place. Your commitments have been secured. Now the most important discipline of execution begins.

Strategy execution fails at the level of visibility. Performance measurement is the discipline that makes execution visible. It is the execution visibility infrastructure of the strategy: it shows whether the organisation is actually moving closer to its Success in Practice.

The pattern in many organisations

In many organisations, enormous effort goes into designing strategy. Leaders invest months clarifying direction, defining Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), setting targets, and aligning teams around ambitious outcomes. Scorecards are built. Key performance indicators (KPIs) are cascaded. Performance frameworks are communicated.

Yet several months later, when leadership teams pause and reflect, the same uncertainty often appears. Work is happening. Teams are busy. Initiatives are underway. Leaders still cannot confidently answer a more fundamental question: “Is the work being done actually advancing the strategy?” This is the gap that performance measurement exists to close.

The visibility infrastructure

Performance measurement creates the visibility infrastructure that allows leaders to observe whether the organisation is genuinely progressing toward its strategic outcomes. Without this infrastructure, strategy remains largely interpretive. Each function develops its own sense of progress. Activity becomes mistaken for advancement. Energy is expended, but the connection to the enterprise result becomes difficult to see.

Performance measurement restores that line of sight. It allows the organisation to move from activity visibility to progress visibility.

From activity to progress

Most organisations are extremely good at measuring activity: how many projects were launched, how many meetings were held, how many deliverables were completed. These indicators provide evidence that work is occurring and create a sense of operational movement. But activity alone does not indicate whether the organisation is actually moving closer to the strategic outcome it set out to achieve.

Performance measurement therefore asks a different set of questions. The question moves from "What work was done?" to "What changed because of the work?" The organisation examines evidence of progress. This shift transforms performance management from an administrative reporting exercise into an execution intelligence system.

1.2 — The Four Strategic Purposes of Performance Measurement
Click each to explore

Performance measurement creates execution rhythm. Most strategic outcomes are the result of cumulative progress built over time. Regular review cycles allow leaders to notice the subtle signals that indicate whether execution is strengthening or weakening. Momentum may begin to accelerate in one area while slowing in another. Resource constraints may appear. Decision bottlenecks may surface. Capability gaps may become visible. When these signals are visible early, leaders can respond while the situation is still manageable.

This rhythm prevents the most common leadership surprise in organisations: discovering performance failure only at the end of the reporting period. Performance measurement ensures that strategy is observed while it is unfolding, as well as evaluated after the fact.

At its core, performance measurement serves four strategic purposes within the organisation. Select each to explore, and ask yourself where your organisation is strongest and where it is weakest.

Purpose 01
Creates Visibility
▼

Leaders can observe where the organisation is gaining momentum and where it is stalling. Strategic priorities that once existed only as intentions begin to appear as measurable progress signals. Without measurement, leaders are flying blind: confident in effort but uncertain about direction.

Ask yourself: Where in your organisation is execution happening in the dark? Where are you most likely to be surprised at the end of a reporting period?
Purpose 02
Establishes Accountability
▼

Strategic completion requires tracking every component of the declared goal, including the parts that bring no visible victory. What has been accomplished is recorded as evidence that commitments are being kept. Leaders and teams understand which part of the strategy they are responsible for advancing.

Accountability without measurement is aspiration without a feedback loop: it depends entirely on personal commitment, with no organisational structure behind it.

Ask yourself: If your key performance indicators (KPIs) disappeared from your scorecard but the targets remained unchanged, how many would still be actively owned?
Purpose 03
Execution Intelligence
▼

At this point, performance measurement begins to evolve beyond data collection. It becomes execution intelligence. Data on its own rarely improves performance. Numbers simply describe what has happened. Insight emerges only when those numbers are interpreted through disciplined conversation. Performance measurement therefore transforms data into decision insight.

Each review cycle helps leaders answer three essential questions:

  1. What progress has been achieved since the last review?
  2. What obstacles are preventing further progress?
  3. What adjustment will restore momentum toward the target?

Through these conversations, performance management becomes a learning system. Teams develop a deeper understanding of how strategy unfolds in practice. Leaders strengthen their ability to guide execution through uncertainty and complexity.

Ask yourself: What did your organisation actually learn from its last strategy cycle? What did it learn about how execution works inside your system?
Purpose 04
Enables Timely Correction
▼

Early signals allow teams to intervene while progress is still recoverable. Problems are addressed when they are small enough to resolve. Without early signals, problems are discovered only when targets have already been missed.

This purpose is why measurement frequency matters: a signal detected in week four carries a different cost from the same signal detected in week twelve.

Ask yourself: What is the average gap in your organisation between when a performance problem begins and when leadership first becomes aware of it?
1.3 — From Performance Measurement to Execution Recovery
The Review–Realign–Restore Cycle (R3)

In part 1.2, you saw that performance measurement serves four strategic purposes: creating visibility, establishing accountability, generating execution intelligence and enabling timely correction. These four purposes work together to help leaders understand how strategy is progressing and where intervention is required.

But identifying a performance gap does not automatically resolve it. Leaders need a disciplined process for interpreting what the evidence reveals, identifying what needs attention and ensuring that corrective decisions translate into improved execution.

Within the S2R® system, this process is called the Review–Realign–Restore Cycle (R3). The cycle provides a recurring leadership rhythm through which performance evidence is examined, execution gaps are addressed and progress is restored.

Stage 1 — Review: Establish Execution Reality

Review brings together the first three purposes of performance measurement: visibility, accountability and execution intelligence.

The KPI owner assesses progress against agreed targets and submits evidence of what has been achieved. The leader independently reviews the same KPI, bringing a broader organisational perspective. Together, they establish a shared understanding of execution reality.

Three questions guide the conversation:

  1. What progress has been achieved against the agreed target?
  2. What does the evidence tell us about performance?
  3. Where is actual progress falling short of expectations?
Outcome: A shared, evidence-based understanding of progress and any emerging performance gaps.
Stage 2 — Realign: Diagnose and Address Execution Gaps

Once Review establishes where progress is falling short, Realign determines what is preventing the expected performance.

The leader activates the S2R® Accountability Loop (part 1.4) to investigate whether the gap arises from:

  • Ownership: the individual understands the responsibility but has lost connection to its purpose or accountability.
  • Capability: the individual is committed but lacks the skills or support required to deliver.
  • Role or structural constraints: the responsibilities, resources or organisational conditions are preventing effective execution.

The leader and KPI owner identify an appropriate intervention, which may include renewed ownership, coaching, additional support or structural adjustments.

Outcome: A clear understanding of the underlying performance constraint and an agreed corrective response.
Stage 3 — Restore: Rebuild Execution Momentum

Restore activates the fourth purpose of performance measurement: timely correction.

The agreed intervention is translated into concrete execution commitments. The leader and KPI owner establish what must happen next, who is responsible and when progress will be reviewed. At this stage, they:

  • Agree on corrective actions and expected progress.
  • Reaffirm responsibilities and accountability.
  • Establish follow-up timelines and support requirements.
  • Update the KPI alignment state to reflect whether clarity, capability and ownership have been restored.

Restoring momentum requires renewed commitment to the intended outcome, supported by the conditions necessary to achieve it.

Outcome: A clear recovery pathway, renewed execution ownership and agreed follow-through.
How the cycle remains continuous

The next Review examines whether the corrective actions have produced the expected improvement. If gaps remain, the cycle continues until execution returns to the required trajectory.

1.4 — The Accountability Loop
Diagnosing the source of performance gaps

Many organisations respond to performance gaps with one default response: they push harder. More urgency is applied. More pressure is introduced. Deadlines are reinforced. Pressure alone rarely solves execution challenges because pressure does not identify the underlying cause of the problem.

When a gap is identified, the leader activates the Accountability Loop: a structured diagnostic process designed to restore traction and ownership without collapsing into unproductive performance pressure. Most performance gaps emerge from one of three underlying conditions, and each calls for its own recovery.

The leader achieves the recovery through the GROW model you used for the Alignment Dialogue in Unit 7: Goal, Reality, Options and Will. The goal of the conversation is ownership of the KPI: an alignment score of 9 or 10. Select each root cause to explore its recovery.

Root Cause A
Lack of Accountability
▼

The individual understands the task but has lost the motivational connection to it. The work may feel detached from a meaningful outcome. Competing priorities may have diluted attention. Personal energy may have shifted elsewhere.

Recovery: Reconnect purpose and ownership. The leader revisits the broader organisational context of the KPI. The conversation reconnects the task to the enterprise outcome it supports, and the individual is reminded how their contribution fits into the wider organisational map. By restoring that line of sight, motivation and commitment can often be reactivated.

Achieving the recovery through GROW:

  • GoalReconnect the KPI to the enterprise outcome it supports, and agree what an alignment of 9 or 10 would look like.
  • RealityExplore what has drawn attention away from the KPI and how the individual now sees the target.
  • OptionsThe individual names how they will restore focus on the KPI among competing priorities.
  • WillThe individual restates ownership, the actions and the follow-up date, and the leader asks the alignment question again.
Root Cause B
Lack of Skill
▼

The individual is committed but lacks the capability required to achieve the expected level. The challenge may involve technical expertise, analytical capability, leadership skills, or process knowledge that was initially underestimated.

Recovery: Develop capability, with no added pressure. Targeted coaching may be introduced. Peer support may be arranged. Formal training may be provided. In some cases the timeline attached to the KPI may also require adjustment if the capability development process requires additional time.

Achieving the recovery through GROW:

  • GoalAgree the result the KPI requires, the capability it calls for, and what an alignment of 9 or 10 would look like.
  • RealityEstablish which part of the task the individual cannot yet do, and what they can already do well.
  • OptionsChoose together among targeted coaching, peer support, formal training and an adjusted timeline.
  • WillAgree the development plan, any adjusted milestones and the follow-up, and ask the alignment question again.
Root Cause C
Misfit of Role or Structural Constraint
▼

The issue lies beyond motivation and skill development. The individual’s strengths do not align with the specific responsibilities, or the role requires capabilities that the individual does not naturally bring to the task.

Recovery: Structural alignment. The task may be redistributed across the team. Responsibilities may be adjusted. In some situations the individual may be repositioned into a role that better aligns with their strengths.

Achieving the recovery through GROW:

  • GoalAgree the outcome the KPI must deliver, whoever delivers it.
  • RealityEstablish where the demands of the role and the strengths of the individual, or the structure around them, do not match.
  • OptionsConsider redistributing the task, adjusting responsibilities or, in a separate role conversation, repositioning the individual.
  • WillAgree the structural change, who decides it and by when, and ask the alignment question again with whoever now owns the KPI.
Keep the two conversations separate

The performance conversation must remain separate from the role conversation. Performance discussions focus on progress toward outcomes. Role alignment discussions focus on placing individuals where their capabilities can generate the greatest organisational value. By maintaining this distinction, the organisation protects both fairness and clarity.

The correct root cause determines the correct recovery action: pressure alone will not solve a skill gap, and coaching will not solve a structural misfit.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why Performance Measurement Determines Execution Velocity

Understanding why the discipline of review conversations is the factor that determines whether strategy execution sustains momentum or quietly stalls.

Section learning outcomes
  • Explain why review discipline determines the pace of execution.
  • Recognise the early signs that review discipline is weakening and the cost each carries.
2.1 — Four Consequences of Weak Review Discipline
Click each to explore
Execution velocity

Execution velocity is the rate at which an organisation converts strategic intent into measurable results. That rate is shaped by how quickly leaders detect progress or deviation, understand its causes, make decisions and translate those decisions into action.

Performance measurement provides the evidence. Review discipline converts that evidence into timely decisions.

This is why organisations can have well-designed strategies, clearly assigned KPIs and detailed execution plans, yet still experience slow progress. The measures may exist, but the rhythm of examining them, resolving constraints and adjusting execution may be weak.

Four consequences of weak review discipline

When performance reviews occur inconsistently or superficially, several predictable patterns begin to emerge. These are the foreseeable consequences of allowing the review rhythm to weaken.

Consequence 01
Early Warning Signals Are Missed
▼

Execution rarely collapses suddenly. Performance deterioration typically begins with small, subtle indicators: a milestone that slips by a few days, a process bottleneck beginning to slow delivery, a capability gap emerging in one team, a dependency between functions that introduces unexpected delays. These signals often appear months before the final KPI target is at risk. But if leaders are not regularly examining performance evidence, these signals remain invisible. By the time the problem becomes visible at the end of the reporting period, the opportunity for early intervention has already been lost.

Ask yourself: In your experience, what is the typical lag between when a performance problem begins and when leadership acts? What would closing that lag by half be worth?
Consequence 02
Gradual Diffusion of Accountability
▼

Accountability is sustained through visibility. When review conversations disappear or become irregular, the psychological signal shifts. Targets remain written on the scorecard, but they no longer appear in leadership dialogue. KPI owners begin to interpret the absence of review as an indication that the target is no longer an active organisational priority. The KPI quietly moves from being operational to being aspirational. Work continues, but the urgency associated with the strategic outcome begins to fade. People assume progress is occurring somewhere in the system, yet no one feels the immediate responsibility to ensure that it actually is.

Ask yourself: Which KPIs in your current scorecard are operational and which have quietly become aspirational? What made the difference?
Consequence 03
Loss of Organisational Learning
▼

Every review conversation contains valuable insight about which approaches accelerate progress, where operational friction exists, and which cross-functional dependencies are working and which are slowing things down. When review conversations are reduced to occasional reporting exercises, results are recorded but the organisation never pauses to understand why those results occurred. Without reflection, improvement becomes accidental. The organisation’s execution capability stagnates even as it accumulates more strategic ambition.

Ask yourself: What does your organisation know now about how execution works inside your system that it did not know eighteen months ago?
Consequence 04
Erodes Leadership Credibility
▼

When leaders set ambitious targets and communicate strategic priorities, they create an expectation that those priorities will remain visible in the leadership conversation. If those targets are not revisited through structured performance discussions, a subtle message spreads: the strategy announcement was more symbolic than operational. Teams become less attentive to new strategic commitments because they have learned that priorities often quietly fade. Consistent performance reviews counteract this by demonstrating that strategic priorities are ongoing leadership commitments.

Ask yourself: What is the current level of strategic commitment in your organisation? How much of that is shaped by what leaders consistently review, and how much by what they announce?

Section 3 · Extrapolating — Where

Where Does Performance Measurement Break Down Across Functions?

Examining where the collective intelligence for execution discipline of the leadership team unintentionally weakens the performance measurement discipline through four characteristic fractures.

Section learning outcomes
  • Identify where the review and interpretation of performance evidence break down across functions.
  • Assess which breakdown is most prevalent in their own organisation.
3.1 — Four Fractures in Performance Measurement
Select each gap

Performance measurement is ultimately a leadership conversation discipline. Most organisations have agreed goals and KPIs; the breakdown usually occurs in how leaders review and interpret the measurement together. When that discipline weakens, several predictable fractures appear across the organisation. Select each gap below to examine where it occurs and what it costs.

Gap 01
The Horizontal Coordination Gap
▼

One of the most common breakdowns occurs when performance reviews happen inside functions, with no cross-functional view. Each department reviews its KPIs independently. Finance examines financial indicators. Operations reviews throughput. Marketing examines campaign performance. Individually, these conversations may be rigorous and technically sound.

But strategy execution rarely moves through a single function. Enterprise outcomes depend on interdependent execution across multiple functions. When performance reviews remain confined within functional boundaries, leaders see partial performance signals and miss the full execution pattern. Each function may report strong progress while the overall strategic outcome remains inconsistent.

The leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, closes this gap by introducing periodic cross-functional performance reviews where the executive team collectively examines how the KPIs across functions interact to produce enterprise outcomes.

Gap 02
The Evidence Quality Gap
▼

Performance measurement loses power when review conversations rely on weak or inconsistent evidence. In some cases KPI owners present narrative explanations with no verifiable indicators. In others, the evidence reflects activity alone, with no evidence of progress toward the outcome. Sometimes information arrives too late to support timely decisions.

When evidence quality declines, review conversations shift away from execution insight and toward interpretation debates. Leaders begin discussing whether the data can be trusted, and the review loses sight of what the data reveals about progress. Over time, the credibility of the performance system weakens.

Closing this gap requires the leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, to reinforce a simple but powerful principle: every performance review must be anchored in verifiable evidence that shows observable movement toward the outcome, is timely enough to inform decisions, and is clearly connected to the KPI, target, or Key Result being assessed.

Gap 03
The Review Rhythm Breakdown
▼

Even well-designed performance systems weaken if the organisation does not maintain a consistent rhythm of review conversations. Strategy execution unfolds over time. Progress appears gradually through incremental movement across weeks and months. If review conversations occur too infrequently, leaders lose visibility into the trajectory of that movement. Small deviations accumulate unnoticed until they become significant performance gaps.

Irregular review cycles also weaken behavioural discipline. KPI owners begin to treat measurement as a reporting event.

Healthy performance measurement requires a predictable organisational rhythm. Leaders know when reviews will occur. KPI owners know when evidence must be prepared. Teams expect progress to be examined regularly. This rhythm creates a cultural signal: execution progress is observed continuously.

Gap 04
The Learning Suppression Gap
▼

A subtle but corrosive breakdown appears when performance reviews focus exclusively on evaluation. If review conversations concentrate only on whether targets were achieved, teams often become cautious about discussing obstacles openly. People instinctively defend results and leave the deeper causes unexplored. This dynamic suppresses organisational learning.

Yet strategy execution is inherently experimental. Organisations are constantly discovering what works, where friction exists, and how coordination across functions actually unfolds. Healthy performance reviews therefore balance accountability with curiosity. Leaders examine evidence rigorously, yet they also explore what the organisation is learning from execution patterns.

Questions shift from “Why was the target missed?” to “What is the system teaching us about how execution actually works?” When this learning dimension becomes part of review conversations, performance measurement evolves from a scoring mechanism into a capability-building system.

4.1 — The Enterprise Performance Intelligence Circle (EPIC)
Select each signal

The Enterprise Performance Intelligence Circle (EPIC) is the executive team’s structured review of enterprise execution. The question that guides EPIC is: “Is the chain of execution across our functions holding together?”

Many organisations conduct performance reviews. Few organisations convert those reviews into collective strategic intelligence. EPIC ensures that insights emerging from PM review sheets are brought together across functions in a structured conversation that allows the leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, to observe execution as a living system.

In EPIC, each member of the executive team brings three signals from their own PM review sheet: where their Key Results stand, what is starting to break, and what execution is teaching them. Each signal answers one question, and the three are shared in that order. Progress comes first because it gives the warnings their context and the learning a system to learn about.

Signal 1 · Collective Trajectory: “Where are we on execution?”

What each leader brings. The current percentage progress toward their Key Results and KPIs, taken from the PM ratings and evidence in their PM review sheet, and whether momentum is accelerating, stable or slowing. The percentage involves no estimate and no narrative opinion.

How each leader says it. “We are at [X]% progress, supported by [evidence], and momentum is [accelerating, stable or slowing].”

Examples:

  • The COO reports that operational readiness KPIs show 72% progress toward the strategic Key Result, supported by throughput improvements and cycle-time reductions documented in the review sheets.
  • The CFO reports that financial indicators show 65% progress toward the quarterly profitability milestone, supported by margin improvements and cost optimisation evidence.

What the executive team sees. Which functions show strong forward momentum and which show slower movement. The executive team sees the collective trajectory of strategy execution, and uneven progress becomes visible at once.

Signal 2 · Early Warnings: “What is starting to break?”

What each leader brings. One or two warning signals that emerged in their PM review conversations, with the evidence from the review sheets. An early warning appears before a KPI or Key Result is formally off target.

How each leader says it. “An emerging risk is [signal], supported by [evidence].”

Examples of early warnings:

  • An operational process beginning to slow delivery times
  • A capability gap that may affect upcoming milestones
  • A supply constraint that could delay implementation of strategic initiatives
  • A change in customer feedback signalling emerging dissatisfaction

What the executive team sees. Where a signal in one function is connected to activity in another. What looked like an isolated operational issue shows itself as a cross-functional execution dependency, and the leadership team, acting as the collective intelligence for execution discipline, can act on it before it becomes a measurable performance gap.

✎ Reflection — Your Early Warning Signal

Which one or two early warning signals have emerged in your area? What evidence supports each, and what could it affect?

Signal 3 · Execution Learning: “What is execution teaching us?”

What each leader brings. One key learning from their PM reviews, with the evidence behind it: a capability insight, a coordination improvement, a leadership practice that strengthened execution, or a structural obstacle that slowed progress.

How each leader says it. “A key execution learning is [insight], supported by [evidence].”

Examples:

  • The CHRO highlights that performance review discussions revealed a leadership capability gap affecting accountability conversations.
  • The CTO shares that system visibility improvements significantly accelerated operational decision-making.
  • The CMO highlights a pattern in customer experience data that revealed which service interactions most strongly influence trust.

What the executive team sees. How execution actually works inside the organisation. Over time, the organisation builds a body of practical execution intelligence that improves how strategy is implemented.

✎ Reflection — Your Execution Learning

What is one key execution learning from your performance reviews? What evidence supports it, and does it apply to your function or to the enterprise?

What EPIC produces
  1. A clear snapshot of enterprise progress toward the strategic Key Results.
  2. Emerging risks visible early enough for coordinated intervention.
  3. Execution learning captured to strengthen future performance.

Through this practice, performance measurement becomes the organisation’s radar system for strategy execution.

► Scenario

The CXO, the Chief Operating Officer, is responsible for the Objective and Key Result (OKR): “Increase on-time delivery of customer orders from 82% to 95%.” The Key Result supports the enterprise Objective of improving customer retention.

At the start of the quarter, in the Alignment Dialogue, the Head of Logistics rated their alignment with this Key Result at 9. The latest Review of the Review–Realign–Restore Cycle shows that on-time delivery has stalled at 87%, six weeks before the deadline, and that alignment has fallen. The CXO activates the Accountability Loop to determine which of the three root causes is behind the gap.

The conversation
CXO · Chief Operating Officer

Thank you for making time. Let us start with the evidence. Your self-assessment rates the on-time delivery Key Result at 2, and so does mine. On-time delivery is at 87%. We started at 82%, and the target is 95%, six weeks from now.

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

Yes. We reached 87% in the first two months. Since then it has not moved.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

When we agreed this Key Result at the start of the quarter, you rated your alignment at 9. On a scale of 1 to 10, where is it today?

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

A 5, if I am honest.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

What has moved it from 9 to 5?

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

Since March, most of my week has gone to the warehouse move. The CEO asked me to lead the move to the northern warehouse, and that is where the pressure is. My shift supervisors run the daily dispatch meetings now. I read their summaries when I can.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

Which of the agreed activities have been carried out?

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

The route planning software is live, the two extra drivers have started and the new carrier contracts are signed. The loading schedule redesign is behind. I built the first version of the schedule myself in the first month, and that is what took us from 82% to 87%. The next version needs a few days of my time with the dispatch data, and I have not given it those days.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

Do you have what you need to finish it?

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

Yes. The team has the people, and the system gives me all the data I need. Running dispatch is the job I know best.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

How do you see the 95% target now?

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

Honestly, it has started to feel like a head office number. The warehouse move is what the CEO asks about every week. Nobody has asked me about on-time delivery since the quarter started.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

The 95% matters, and I need the loading schedule redesign done. From next week I will add on-time delivery to the weekly operations report, so the number is in front of everyone.

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

I will try to fit it in around the warehouse move.

CXO · Chief Operating Officer

Good. Have the redesign live in three weeks. We will look at the number again at the end of the month.

Head of Logistics · owner of the Key Result

Understood.

Accountability Loop Diagnosis

The CXO must determine whether the root cause is:

Root Cause A
Lack of Accountability

The individual understands the task but has lost motivational connection. Recovery: reconnect to purpose and enterprise context.

Root Cause B
Lack of Skill

The individual is committed but lacks the capability required. Recovery: coaching, training, timeline adjustment.

Root Cause C
Misfit / Structural Constraint

Strengths do not align with responsibilities. Recovery: structural alignment, redistribution, or repositioning.

The GROW Model in the Accountability Loop

The GROW model of Unit 7 is the basis for analysing the conversation. Each stage asks a question, and the individual does the thinking, so the answers reached are their own.

G
Goal

What the individual wants to reach. In the Accountability Loop: ownership of the Key Result again, an alignment score of 9 or 10.

“What would a 9 or 10 look like for you on this Key Result?”

R
Reality

What is true today. The leader explores what stands behind the score and the gap, and listens without defending the target.

“What has moved your alignment from where it was?”

O
Options

What could be done. The individual generates the ways forward, matched to the root cause.

“What could you do? What support would make the difference?”

W
Will

What will be done, and by whom. The actions, the owner and the follow-up are agreed, and the alignment question is asked again.

“With that agreed, where is your alignment now, on the scale of 1–10?”
