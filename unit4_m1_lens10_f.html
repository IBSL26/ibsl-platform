Section 1 · Awareness — What

Values, Culture & Organisational Health

How organisational values become lived culture, and how that culture shows in the condition of organisational health.

Section learning outcomes
  • Evaluate how organisational values translate into cultural norms and practices that shape organisational health.
  • Diagnose organisational health strengths and vulnerabilities across observable behaviour and its underlying conditions.
1.1 — Organisational Values: What We Stand For

Organisational values are the fundamental principles that guide how an organisation conducts itself in pursuit of its purpose and strategic objectives.

They establish the standards of behaviour expected of leaders and employees, influencing how people are treated, responsibilities are fulfilled and performance is achieved.

Values serve two important organisational purposes:

1. Establish Behavioural Expectations. Values define the standards of conduct expected across the organisation. They guide how people interact, exercise authority, fulfil responsibilities and collaborate in delivering strategic priorities.

2. Provide a Basis for Accountability. Values establish the principles against which organisational conduct can be assessed. They enable leaders to evaluate how results are achieved, alongside the results themselves.

Translating Values into Behavioural Standards

For values to influence organisational conduct, they must be expressed through observable behaviours.

Organisational ValueExpected Behavioural Standard
IntegrityAct honestly, communicate transparently and uphold ethical commitments.
AccountabilityTake ownership of responsibilities, honour commitments and report progress accurately.
RespectTreat others fairly, listen constructively and recognise different perspectives.
ExcellenceMaintain high standards, address performance gaps and pursue continuous improvement.
CollaborationShare information, coordinate efforts and contribute to collective outcomes.

These behavioural standards clarify what organisational values mean in practice. However, the existence of values and behavioural standards does not guarantee their consistent application.

Their influence depends on whether leaders and employees repeatedly demonstrate, reinforce and uphold them through everyday organisational practices. It is through this consistent application that organisational values become embedded in organisational culture.

1.2 — Organisational Culture: How We Do Things

Organisational culture is the system of shared behavioural norms that determines how people conduct themselves and work together.

Culture develops as organisational values are translated into behavioural expectations and repeatedly demonstrated through everyday interactions and ways of working.

Over time, these behaviours become accepted as “the way we do things around here”. The organisation’s actual culture therefore reflects the behaviours that have become normal, regardless of its stated values.

The Cultural Operating Code

The deliberate translation of organisational values into shared cultural norms forms the foundation of a Cultural Operating Code, which establishes how organisational values are consistently lived and reinforced.

A Cultural Operating Code makes explicit the behavioural expectations that govern how people work together, exercise accountability, communicate and contribute to organisational priorities.

When consistently practised and reinforced, these norms become embedded in the organisation’s culture. The culture established through these shared norms and everyday practices determines how organisational values are experienced and reflected in organisational health.

1.3 — Organisational Health: The Resulting Condition
Select each part to open it

Organisational health reflects how effectively organisational values are lived out within the culture that has been established.

It is evident in the quality of collective working relationships, clarity of expectations, employee commitment, accountability and the organisation’s ability to function effectively in pursuit of strategic objectives.

Patrick Lencioni describes a healthy organisation as one that is whole, consistent and complete, with its management, operations, strategy and culture working together coherently.

Within Strategy2Results®, organisational health provides an indication of how effectively organisational values have become embedded in the culture experienced by employees.

The Five Observable Health Traits

The Five Observable Health Traits provide visible indicators of the condition of organisational health.

Health TraitObservable Organisational Condition
1. Minimal PoliticsEmployees collaborate openly, with limited personal agendas, territorial behaviour and internal competition.
2. Minimal ConfusionPeople understand organisational direction, priorities, responsibilities and performance expectations.
3. High MoraleEmployees demonstrate commitment, motivation and confidence in their working environment.
4. High ProductivityCollective effort is coordinated effectively and translates into meaningful performance outcomes.
5. Low TurnoverThe organisation retains capable employees through a working environment that supports commitment and contribution.

These traits describe what organisational health looks like when it is strong. However, the traits are only the visible surface of organisational health. To understand how they are produced, we examine the underlying conditions through the Organisational Health Pyramid.

The Organisational Health Pyramid

The Organisational Health Pyramid explains how organisational health develops from the inside outward. It illustrates how trust influences employees’ experiences, how those experiences shape beliefs, how beliefs influence actions and how actions produce organisational results.

The Visible Traits
1. Trust — The Foundation

Trust forms the foundation of organisational health. It develops through consistent leadership behaviour, including keeping commitments, communicating transparently, acknowledging mistakes and treating people fairly.

When trust is established, employees develop confidence in their leaders and colleagues. They become more willing to communicate openly, raise concerns and contribute to collective objectives.

Leadership implication: Organisational health begins with the trust established through consistent leadership conduct.

2. Experiences — Shaped by Trust

Employees experience organisational culture through everyday interactions, meetings, performance conversations, leadership decisions and recognition practices.

These experiences communicate what the organisation genuinely values and the behaviours it considers acceptable. For example, the way leaders respond to mistakes influences whether employees experience the organisation as supportive of learning or inclined towards blame.

Leadership implication: Everyday organisational experiences reinforce or weaken the values leadership seeks to establish.

3. Beliefs — Formed by Experiences

Through repeated experiences, employees develop beliefs about how the organisation operates. They form assumptions about whether leaders listen, whether commitments matter, whether effort is recognised and whether honest communication is encouraged.

Over time, these beliefs influence how employees interpret organisational expectations and their own responsibilities.

Leadership implication: Repeated organisational experiences establish the beliefs that influence collective behaviour.

4. Actions — Driven by Beliefs

Employees’ beliefs influence how they behave and contribute to organisational performance. When employees believe their contributions are valued, they are more likely to share ideas, collaborate and take initiative.

When they believe accountability is consistently reinforced, they are more likely to take ownership of responsibilities and honour commitments.

Leadership implication: The beliefs established through organisational culture influence the behaviours employees demonstrate.

5. Results — The Visible Traits

The actions employees consistently demonstrate produce collective organisational outcomes. These outcomes become visible through the Five Observable Health Traits: Minimal Politics, Minimal Confusion, High Morale, High Productivity and Low Turnover.

At the apex of the pyramid, organisational results reflect the cumulative influence of trust, experiences, beliefs and actions. A commitment to continuous improvement helps sustain these results as the organisation evolves.

Leadership implication: The visible condition of organisational health reflects the underlying cultural experiences and behaviours established throughout the organisation.

Connecting the Five Traits and the Pyramid

The Five Observable Health Traits describe what we see. They indicate the visible condition of organisational health.

The Organisational Health Pyramid explains how that condition develops. It examines the underlying trust, experiences, beliefs and actions that influence organisational results.

When trust deteriorates, employees’ experiences may create scepticism, their beliefs may become defensive and their actions may shift towards self-protection. Over time, these patterns can weaken the visible traits of organisational health.

Strengthening organisational health therefore requires leadership attention to the underlying cultural conditions that shape everyday employee experiences.

Section Closing Insight

Values establish the principles that guide organisational behaviour. Culture reflects how those principles become established ways of working. Organisational health reveals how effectively those values are lived through the culture that has developed.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why Organisational Health Determines Execution Performance

How organisational health multiplies execution capability, what poor health costs the enterprise, and how health sustains performance over time.

Section learning outcomes
  • Determine how organisational health can be leveraged to strengthen strategy execution capability and sustain enterprise performance.
  • Quantify the enterprise cost of poor organisational health to direct leadership attention to its performance implications.
2.1 — Organisational Health as a Performance Multiplier

Organisational health influences how effectively an organisation converts its strategic intentions into results. An organisation may have clear priorities, capable employees and established performance systems, yet their effectiveness depends partly on the culture through which people work together.

When organisational health is strong, people collaborate, fulfil commitments, address problems early and focus on collective priorities. When it deteriorates, mistrust, confusion, internal competition and disengagement can reduce the effectiveness of otherwise sound strategic systems.

The Multiplier Effect

Organisational health acts as a multiplier of strategy execution capability. It influences the effectiveness of existing capabilities:

Strategic CapabilityInfluence of Organisational Health
Strategic DirectionShared commitment strengthens alignment around organisational priorities.
Execution AccountabilityTrust and ownership support consistent delivery of commitments.
Performance ManagementOpenness enables constructive performance conversations and corrective action.
Enterprise CollaborationShared responsibility strengthens coordination across organisational functions.
Organisational AdaptabilityConfidence and collective commitment support responsiveness to changing conditions.

Key insight: The effectiveness of a strategy depends partly on the organisational health of the people and teams responsible for delivering it.

2.2 — Organisational Health and Execution Velocity

Execution velocity refers to an organisation’s ability to convert strategic priorities into coordinated action and sustained results. Organisational health influences this velocity through the quality of collaboration, communication, accountability and collective commitment.

In a healthy organisation:

  • Performance concerns are raised and addressed early.
  • Decisions and commitments are followed through.
  • Information moves effectively across organisational functions.
  • Teams coordinate their contributions towards shared priorities.
  • Employees remain engaged in delivering organisational objectives.

These conditions reduce unnecessary friction and support consistent performance momentum.

The Effect on Strategy Execution

Consider two organisations implementing similar strategic priorities. Both have established objectives, KPIs, execution plans and review mechanisms. In the first, leaders encourage open reporting, departments coordinate their contributions and employees take ownership of commitments. In the second, employees avoid difficult conversations, departments protect their own interests and performance reviews become defensive.

Although both organisations have similar execution systems, their ability to convert those systems into results may differ significantly. The difference lies partly in the organisational health supporting execution.

Strategic implication: As organisational health deteriorates, execution friction increases and performance momentum becomes more difficult to sustain.

2.3 — The Price of Poor Organisational Health

Poor organisational health creates costs that are often overlooked because they do not appear as separate items in financial or performance reports. These costs accumulate through everyday organisational practices and eventually affect enterprise performance.

Hidden CostOrganisational ManifestationPerformance Consequence
Lost TimeRepeated work, unclear communication, unnecessary meetings and unresolved disagreements.Reduced productivity and slower execution.
Employee DisengagementReduced initiative, limited discretionary effort and withdrawal from collective responsibilities.Lower contribution and weakened performance momentum.
Declining TrustDefensive communication, withholding information and protection of departmental interests.Reduced collaboration and slower problem resolution.
Emotional FatiguePersistent frustration, uncertainty, interpersonal tension and self-protective behaviour.Reduced morale, commitment and employee effectiveness.

How These Costs Accumulate

The costs of poor organisational health often remain hidden within everyday operating expenses, lost productivity and reduced employee contribution. Quantifying these losses enables executives to establish their financial impact, identify the most significant sources of performance loss and prioritise corrective interventions. By calculating the costs associated with lost time, employee disengagement, declining trust, emotional fatigue and talent loss, organisations can build a measurable business case for strengthening organisational health and track the value recovered through improvement. The following examples demonstrate how seemingly small, recurring losses can accumulate into substantial annual costs.

Named Hidden CostIllustrative AssumptionHow the Cost Accumulates

Employee Disengagement30 employees each contribute 1 fewer productive hour per week; 46 weeks; R300 hourly cost.30 × 1 × 46 = 1,380 hours; indicative capacity value = R414,000/year.
Declining TrustEight managers each spend 3 extra hours per month on defensive reviews and duplicated checks; R600 hourly cost.8 × 3 × 12 = 288 hours; 288 × R600 = R172,800/year.

Note: Calculations are illustrative and should be adapted using organisational data and reasonable assumptions. Overlapping costs should be adjusted to avoid double counting.

Key insight: Repeated losses in time, engagement, trust, wellbeing and retention can accumulate into a material annual cost of poor organisational health.

2.4 — Organisational Health and Sustained Performance

Organisational health influences the ability to sustain performance over time. Short-term results may be achieved through intensive intervention, individual effort or temporary pressure. Sustained performance requires a culture that consistently reinforces the behaviours necessary for collective execution.

When values are embedded in everyday practices, employees develop shared expectations about accountability, collaboration, communication and performance. These expectations reduce dependence on constant leadership intervention and strengthen execution discipline.

Organisational health supports:

  • Consistent execution of organisational priorities.
  • Constructive performance accountability.
  • Effective collaboration across functions.
  • Employee commitment and capability retention.
  • Organisational learning and continuous improvement.

Strategic implication: A healthy organisation is better positioned to maintain performance discipline as leadership responsibilities, strategic priorities and operating conditions change.

Section Closing Insight

Organisational health influences how effectively strategic capabilities translate into results. When health deteriorates, execution friction increases, performance systems lose effectiveness and the cost of delivering strategy rises.

3.2 — CXO Functional Bias in Organisational Health Diagnosis
Select each executive to open the examples

Executives interpret organisational health through the people-performance signals they encounter in their respective functions. Their responsibilities influence what they observe, what they consider important and how they explain employee behaviour.

Functional bias occurs when an executive’s professional perspective influences the interpretation of organisational health signals, potentially leading to an incomplete or inaccurate diagnosis.

The Five Observable Health Traits provide a common basis for examining these differences.

CXO Functional Bias and Debiasing Matrix

CEO · Minimal Politics · High Morale
Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “My executive team works well together, so collaboration across the organisation must be healthy.”

Debiasing technique — Test across levels: Compare executive perceptions with employee experiences of cooperation, conflict and information sharing.

High Morale

Potential biased interpretation: “Employees are quiet in meetings because they have nothing to contribute.”

Debiasing technique — Examine employee experiences: Establish whether employees believe their contributions are welcomed and taken seriously.

CFO · High Productivity · Low Turnover
High Productivity

Potential biased interpretation: “Employment costs are increasing without equivalent output; employees must be underperforming.”

Debiasing technique — Separate effort from conditions: Examine whether employees have clear expectations, appropriate support and a culture that reinforces contribution.

Low Turnover

Potential biased interpretation: “Turnover is costly, so retaining employees means our people performance is healthy.”

Debiasing technique — Examine retention quality: Assess whether the organisation is retaining capable, committed employees and understand the experiences influencing their decisions to stay.

CHRO · Minimal Confusion · High Morale · Low Turnover
Minimal Confusion

Potential biased interpretation: “Everyone has a job description, so role expectations must be clear.”

Debiasing technique — Test lived expectations: Compare formal role descriptions with the expectations employees actually receive from their managers.

High Morale

Potential biased interpretation: “Our engagement survey scores are positive, so employee morale is strong.”

Debiasing technique — Triangulate evidence: Compare survey findings with participation, initiative, absenteeism and employee feedback.

Low Turnover

Potential biased interpretation: “Our turnover is low, which demonstrates that employees are satisfied.”

Debiasing technique — Challenge the assumption: Examine whether employees remain because of commitment, limited alternatives or other circumstances.

COO · Minimal Politics · High Productivity
Minimal Politics

Potential biased interpretation: “Departments are protecting their interests because employees are unwilling to cooperate.”

Debiasing technique — Examine reinforced norms: Determine whether leaders reward functional achievement while neglecting collective contribution.

High Productivity

Potential biased interpretation: “Employees keep missing commitments because they lack discipline.”

Debiasing technique — Trace expectations and experiences: Examine whether accountability standards are clear, consistently reinforced and experienced as fair.

CSO · Minimal Confusion
Minimal Confusion

Potential biased interpretation: “We communicated the strategy clearly; employees should know what is expected.”

Debiasing technique — Verify understanding: Ask employees to explain their priorities, responsibilities and expected contributions in their own words.

These examples illustrate potential functional biases. They do not imply that executives in these roles necessarily hold these assumptions.

Key insight: The Five Observable Health Traits reveal where people-performance vulnerabilities are emerging. Executives strengthen diagnosis by recognising how their functional perspectives may influence interpretation and applying appropriate debiasing techniques.

Section 4 · Integration — Collective

Cross-CXO Health Architecture

Grounding People Performance Through Collective Leadership Accountability

Section learning outcomes

4.1 — Grounding People Performance Through Leadership Accountability

Organisational health is shaped by the cultural conditions leaders establish and reinforce within their teams. The extent to which employees experience trust, clarity, commitment and accountability influences how they behave, collaborate and contribute to performance.

Every executive therefore has a responsibility to understand the health of their function and identify where prevailing cultural norms may be undermining people performance. Organisational health also extends beyond individual functions: employees interact across departments, and behaviours reinforced by one leadership team can strengthen or weaken performance elsewhere.

Sustaining organisational health requires both functional accountability and collective executive responsibility. To fulfil this responsibility, each CXO should periodically examine the organisational health of their function against the Five Observable Health Traits, identify cultural vulnerabilities and quantify associated people-performance losses.

To strengthen objectivity and minimise leadership bias, the Organisational Health Thermometer is circulated to individual employees within each function. Employees independently assess the cultural conditions they experience. Their responses are consolidated into a functional health score, which the responsible executive receives for interpretation, investigation of cultural vulnerabilities and quantification of associated people-performance losses.

This integrated approach forms the Cross-CXO Health Architecture: a structured mechanism connecting functional leadership accountability with collective responsibility for organisational health and sustained people performance. A quarterly assessment and review rhythm allows leaders to examine changes and reinforce healthy cultural practices.

4.3 — From Individual Assessments to Functional Health Scores

After employees independently submit their thermometer responses, the assessments are consolidated by function. For each trait, add the valid individual trait totals and divide by the number of valid responses. Add the five functional trait averages to obtain the functional score out of 100. The leader receives this consolidated score and the trait-level results; the scores come from the employees’ assessments.

Functional Trait Score = Sum of Individual Trait Totals ÷ Number of Valid Responses

Functional Health Score (/100) = Sum of Five Functional Trait Scores (/20 each)

4.6 — Sustaining the Cross-CXO Health Architecture

Organisational health requires consistent leadership attention because cultural conditions develop through repeated behaviours and experiences. Each quarter, the thermometer is circulated to individual employees in each function for independent assessment. The responses are consolidated confidentially and each CXO receives the functional health score and trait-level results. The executive interprets the findings, investigates cultural vulnerabilities and quantifies associated people-performance losses. EXCO then consolidates the functional positions to understand enterprise health and shared priorities for strengthening people performance.

4.7 — From Values to a Cultural Operating Code
Worked example: the IBSL Code

Organisational health reflects the behaviours that have become normal in each function. Stated values shape those behaviours only when people can see what each value requires of them, practise it and be held to it. A Cultural Operating Code makes this explicit: for each organisational value, it names one norm built from three observable actions that every leader and employee can recognise, use and be assessed against.

The Code connects the health architecture to daily leadership practice. When executives model, recognise and correct behaviour against the same norms, the conditions the Organisational Health Thermometer measures begin to change: shared accountability reduces politics, explicit expectations reduce confusion, and consistent consequences protect morale.

◆ Facilitator guidance

Teach the three steps with one IBSL value before showing the full Code: start with the behaviour; the acronym only packages it.

Use the three tests on one IBSL norm so participants see how a norm holds under pressure. The groups build their own Code from their organisation’s values in part 5.8.

How a value becomes a norm
  1. Define the behaviour. Describe what a leader who genuinely lives the value does differently from one who only states it. Write the answer as a specific, observable behaviour.
  2. Build the acronym. Take the first letters of three action words that capture the behaviour. Each word is an action a person performs. The acronym is short enough to use in a corridor conversation.
  3. Write the norm statement. One sentence explaining what the value means in daily practice, specific enough that a new employee reading it on the first day knows which behaviour will be recognised and which will be corrected.
What makes the Code real
  • Demonstrable: every value is translated into observable, specific behaviours.
  • Assessed: the norms appear in 360-degree feedback, so how results are achieved is assessed alongside what is achieved.
  • Enforced: consequences apply consistently regardless of seniority.
  • Maintained: norms are explicit, modelled by executives, reinforced in reviews and revisited periodically.
Three tests for every norm
  • The New Employee Test: Would a new employee reading this norm on the first day know exactly which behaviour will be recognised and which will be corrected?
  • The Pressure Test: Does the norm hold when a senior leader breaches it, when a high performer contradicts it, and when telling the truth is costly?
  • The Mirror Test: Would every executive be comfortable being assessed against this norm in their own 360-degree feedback?
Worked example · The IBSL Cultural Operating Code

IBSL’s values: Intentionality · Integrity · Collaboration · Accountability · Excellence · Human-Centricity. Each value carries one acronym-based norm.

Owning results and reinforcing transparency

Upholding rigour, learning and measurable impact

Placing people at the core of leadership and execution
