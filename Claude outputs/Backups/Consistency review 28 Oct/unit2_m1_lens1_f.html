Strategy Visioning & Success in Practice (SiP)

Collective Intelligence of Execution · Facilitator Guide

This unit helps leaders to determine the organisation’s strategy architecture, articulate its strategic intent and define the Success in Practice (SiP) of the chosen Strategy Intent.

Key learning outcomes Design a coherent strategic direction that connects organisational purpose, stakeholder value and long-term ambition. Articulate a shared future position and define what organisational success will look like in practice. Test strategic ambition against organisational identity and operating realities to strengthen its feasibility. Align enterprise decisions and leadership interpretation around a common strategic direction.

Unit 2 — Session Overview

Use this tab to prepare before each section. It contains the unit intent, key facilitation questions, and tone and watch points for the full unit.

◆ Unit Intent

Before the organisation can define where it is going, leaders must agree on what strategy actually means, in the precise, strategic sense, translated to operational reality, that makes every subsequent decision coherent.

Three connected outputs carry the unit: the Strategy Architecture, the Strategy Intent Statement and Success in Practice. Section 4 shows them completed once for Kestrel Point Services. Protect the time in Section 5 for each group to build them for its own company, in that order.

How the unit runs: Teach the whole unit from the deck first. Participants then go to the portal and complete their own page, in the session or after it.

◆ Key Facilitation Questions

Four questions anchor the unit. Ask each one where it belongs as you teach from the deck.

  • Sections 1, 4 and 5 · Strategy Architecture: “How well defined is each part of our strategy architecture today?”
  • 1.3, Section 3, 4.3 and 5.1 · Success in Practice: “If our Strategy Intent is successful, what should we see in practice?”
  • Section 2 · Decision Filter: “When a new initiative, investment or opportunity appears, what tells us whether it belongs in our strategy?”
  • Section 5 · Strategy Intent Statement: “Does this statement faithfully express the strategic ambition contained in our Architecture?”
◆ Tone & Watch Points

These four points guide how the groups complete the work on the portal, after the teaching.

5.1 Step 2 · Strategy Intent Statement. The group condenses the Architecture into one clear statement. Say: “The Architecture contains the depth. The Intent must provide the clarity.”

5.1 Step 3 · Success in Practice. The group describes observable reality: what people see and experience when the strategy works. Ask: “What is happening when the strategy is working?”

5.1 Step 3 · Balance. The group gives each SiP domain its place in the picture of success and uses the SiP Flammables as the balance check.

5.1 · Group record. The group chooses a scribe for the work and can choose a different scribe for each assignment. Each member reads the record and selects Agree on their own page.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

What is Strategy?

Interrogative Anchor · What is the Strategy Architecture? What is the Strategy Intent Statement? What is Success in Practice?

Section learning outcomes
  • Frame strategic direction in terms of the value and success the organisation intends to create.
  • Differentiate strategic direction from the future position it seeks while maintaining coherence between them.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1 · Awareness

Section intent: This section establishes the conceptual bedrock of the entire unit. Before any co-creation can happen, participants must share a single, precise definition of strategy and understand its three building blocks: the Strategy Architecture, the Strategy Intent Statement and Success in Practice. Do not rush this. Every word in the definition has strategic consequence.

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Opening the Session · Defining Strategy in Your Own Words

Ask: Before showing any content, ask each participant to define strategy in their own words: "Write your definition of strategy in one sentence — right now, without consulting anyone."

Collect: Collect 3–4 responses. Note the differences on a whiteboard. This gap between answers is exactly the problem this unit solves. Use it as the opening provocation.

Keep: Ask every participant to keep their definition. The Unit Summary returns to it.

📋 PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Each participant enters their definition of strategy in the box at the top of Section 1 of the participant file: "Opening the Session — Your Definition of Strategy".

Facilitator Script

Set the frame: Before defining strategy, introduce Strategy Architecture as the underlying logic that gives a strategy its structure.

Explain: "Before we can express strategy clearly, we need to understand what sits underneath it. We call this the Strategy Architecture — the interconnected choices and logic that determine what the organisation will do, why, how and when; how it will create, deliver and sustain value; for whom; and within what environment."

Emphasise that the purpose of this section is to unpack the architecture before trying to produce a strategy statement. A strategy can sound compelling while important parts of its underlying logic remain weak, disconnected or undefined.

As participants work through the concepts, ask them to consider: "How well defined is each part of our strategy architecture today?"

Bridge to the definition: "We are now going to unpack the Strategy2Results® definition of strategy. Each concept in the definition represents part of the architecture. Once we understand the parts and how they connect, we can begin constructing a coherent strategic position."

1.1 — Define the Strategy Architecture
9 Concepts · Click to expand each
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.1

Taking participants through the 9 concepts: Take the concepts one at a time: what each one means, its strategic implication and what fails when it is missing. Then ask: "Which of these nine concepts is least visible in how your organisation currently talks about strategy?"

Key concept to linger on: The distinction between Thinking & Logic (the cognitive engine) and Process (the structured container). Most organisations have process but neglect thinking quality — the definition forces participants to see this gap.

The Value Triad (Creates · Delivers · Sustains): Draw explicit attention to this concept. Ask: "Is your current strategy as strong on Sustains as it is on Creates and Delivers? Where is the investment in sustainability of value?" This often surfaces an uncomfortable silence.

"Strategy is the output of iterative thinking, logic and process to determine what, why, how and when an organisation creates, delivers and sustains value to its stakeholders while navigating the forces within its operating environment."

Every word carries strategic weight. Expand each concept below.

1.2 — Defining the Strategy Intent Statement
4 Dimensions
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.2

Positioning the statement: The Strategy Architecture holds the depth and the supporting logic. The Strategy Intent Statement is crisp, directional and easy to express. Participants derive their own statement in Section 5; here they learn what it brings forward.

Taking participants through the 4 dimensions: Walk through WHAT, WHY, HOW, WHEN one at a time as a group. For each, ask one diagnostic question from the content and take 2–3 responses. HOW and WHEN typically reveal the most significant gaps — give them additional time.

HOW — key facilitation moment: Ask: "Does your operating model currently match your strategy — or is it a legacy structure that predates your current direction?" This often opens the most honest conversation of the entire Awareness section.

WHEN: Ask: "Have you actually mapped the sequence of moves needed to achieve your strategy — or does the plan treat all initiatives as running in parallel?"

1.3 — Success in Practice (SiP): From Intent to Future Reality
4 Observable Domains
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.3

The Strategy Intent Statement establishes the ambition. Success in Practice defines what must be seen, experienced and executed for that ambition to become reality within a given time horizon. Defining this together creates a shared picture of success, reducing the risk of different functions interpreting the same strategic intent differently.

Position participants inside the future created by the Strategy Intent. Ask them to imagine that the intent has been successfully realised and describe the observable organisational reality.

The central question: "If our Strategy Intent is successful, what should we see in practice?"

Facilitator emphasis: Keep participants focused on observable reality. The SiP describes what is happening when the strategy is working. Initiatives, projects and activities stay out of it. The aim is shared construction: leaders should leave with a common understanding of what success looks like across the organisation.

"If our Strategy Intent is successful, what should we see in practice?"

Success is considered across four observable domains:

D1 · Customer Experience & Value

What customers and other value recipients experience when the strategic intent is working.

D2 · Operational Capability & Execution Rhythm

How the organisation operates, coordinates, delivers and adapts when the capabilities required by the intent are functioning effectively.

D3 · People & Culture Dynamics

How people understand the strategic intent, make decisions, collaborate, take ownership and behave when the intent is embedded in the organisation.

D4 · Enterprise Value Creation

How the organisation benefits across its measures of enterprise success.

Together, the four domains create a shared organisational picture of success against which decisions, trade-offs and execution can be aligned.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

The Importance and Relevance of SiP

Ignition Point · Why do the Strategy Intent and SiP matter once they are defined? How do they shape alignment, choices and execution?

Section learning outcomes
  • Reconcile leadership perspectives into a shared strategic ambition and picture of success.
  • Apply strategic direction as a consistent basis for enterprise decisions.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2 · Intelligence

Section intent: The Strategy Intent and SiP become useful when they begin to shape how the organisation aligns, makes choices and executes.

Explain that once leaders have established a shared strategic ambition and defined what success looks like in practice, those two outputs become common reference points across the organisation.

They serve three important functions:

  • Strategic Alignment — keeping people and functions oriented around the same ambition and picture of success.
  • Decision Filter — providing a basis for determining which choices, opportunities and investments support the strategy.
  • Execution Coherence — helping different functions understand how their individual contributions must connect to produce the enterprise outcome.
2.1 — Role of Strategy Intent & SiP in the S2R® Architecture
3 Functions
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2.1

Walk participants through each function and demonstrate how Strategy Intent and SiP work together. The Intent anchors the ambition and strategic choices; SiP anchors the organisational reality those choices are intended to produce.

As you move through the three functions, invite participants to consider: "What happens in an organisation when this function is weak or absent?"

Use the Decision Filter to make the application particularly practical. Ask: "When a new initiative, investment or opportunity appears, what tells us whether it belongs in our strategy?"

The Strategy Intent provides the test of strategic fit. SiP provides the test of strategic contribution: whether the choice moves the organisation closer to the success it has defined.

Section 3 · Extrapolating — Where

Examining Success in Practice

Locate Hot Zones · Depth within each SiP domain · Balance across all four SiP domains

Section learning outcomes
  • Specify observable evidence of success across the four SiP domains.
  • Interpret gaps between intended success and current conditions to identify strategic misalignment.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 3 · Extrapolating

Section intent: We have established that Success in Practice answers a central question: "If our Strategy Intent is successful, what should we see in practice?" This section takes that question deeper.

Each of the four SiP domains covers an important part of organisational reality. Broad statements about customer experience, operational capability, people and culture, or enterprise value fall short. Leaders need to be able to recognise what success would actually look like when it begins to show up in practice.

This is where observable indicators become useful. The indicators help us examine each SiP domain more deeply. They show the kinds of conditions we would expect to observe when that aspect of the Strategy Intent is working, as well as the signals that may indicate something is missing.

There is also a second discipline: balance across the four domains. A leadership team can develop a strong picture of customer success while overlooking the operational capability required to deliver it. It can describe an enabling culture without connecting that culture to enterprise value. It can define strong financial outcomes without describing the customer, operational and people conditions required to produce them.

This section therefore develops two disciplines: Depth within each SiP domain. Balance across all four SiP domains.

We begin by examining the observable indicators within each domain. We then examine what happens when one domain becomes dominant and begins to distort the organisation's overall picture of success.

3.1 — SiP Observable Indicators

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 3.1

Each SiP domain is examined through a set of observable indicators. These indicators help leaders move from a broad description of success to the specific conditions through which that success would become visible in the organisation.

For every indicator, examine two positions:

When Working describes what we would expect to observe when that aspect of the SiP is functioning effectively.

Signal of Absence describes what may become visible when that condition is weak, missing or disconnected from the Strategy Intent.

Use both positions to deepen the discussion. Sometimes the absence of a condition provides the clearest evidence of why it matters.

The indicators sit alongside the organisation's SiP. They help leaders test, deepen and make observable the success they have defined.

3.2 — SiP Flammables: When the Picture of Success Becomes Distorted
4 Risk Patterns
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 3.2

The four SiP domains are designed to describe one organisational reality.

In practice, leadership teams naturally bring different perspectives into the conversation. A commercial leader may see success primarily through customers and growth. An operations leader may emphasise efficiency, process and delivery. A people leader may focus on culture and capability. A finance leader may foreground financial and enterprise outcomes.

Each perspective contributes something important.

The strategic risk emerges when one perspective becomes so dominant that it begins to define success for the entire organisation.

This is what the SiP Flammables are designed to expose.

They provide a balance test for the SiP. They help leaders recognise when a legitimate dimension of success has become disproportionately influential and the enterprise picture has started to narrow into a functional one.

The four SiP domains describe one organisational reality. When one perspective becomes dominant, the enterprise picture narrows into a functional one. Expand each pattern to see what it creates, the indicators that reveal it and the strategic risk if it is not balanced.

4.1 — Step 1: The Strategy Architecture
14 Elements · One Integrated Architecture · Worked Example
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 4.1

Open two or three elements in full, starting with WHAT, then show the Strategy Architecture under its five headings.

Point out how the elements depend on one another in the example: WHAT chooses responsiveness; HOW supplies the mechanism through integrated client teams and one operating rhythm; WHEN puts the request system in place before decision rights move to the hubs; SUSTAINS VALUE and THE FORCES both name silos as the main threat.

Show how each "What this element is saying" is written: one or two sentences, in the company's own words, carrying the actual strategic choice.

The elements are interconnected: a strong answer to one element can expose a weakness or contradiction in another. The value an organisation intends to create must be supported by its HOW. Its WHEN must reflect the capabilities it needs to build. Its approach to sustaining value must respond to the forces within its operating environment.

How each element works. Each element has four questions (THE FORCES has five). They are answered with actual strategic choices. Then one or two sentences state what the element is saying. Open each element to read it.

1 · ITERATIVEContinuously refined
What core hypotheses does the strategy depend on?
Clients will value speed and consistency above the lowest price.
How will reality test those hypotheses?
Quarterly client reviews and monthly service data test that hypothesis.
What evidence would trigger refinement?
Two consecutive quarters of falling retention in key accounts would trigger a review.
How and when will strategic choices be revisited?
The executive team revisits its strategic choices every quarter and in full each year.
What this element is sayingThe strategy rests on one hypothesis, that clients value speed and consistency above the lowest price, and the executive team tests it against service data every quarter.
2 · THINKING & LOGICThe cognitive engine
What alternative possibilities were explored?
Competing on price, adding new service lines and becoming the most responsive partner.
What assumptions underpin the preferred direction?
Key accounts will pay for reliability, and responsiveness can be built without new headcount.
What must be true for this strategy to work?
Requests must move between functions without delay, and leaders must decide at the right level.
What evidence supports or challenges the logic?
Client feedback names slow response as the first reason for leaving; price comes third.
What this element is sayingPrice competition and new service lines were weighed and set aside. The evidence points to responsiveness, which holds only if requests and decisions move across functions without delay.
3 · PROCESSStructured container
Who must participate in shaping the strategy?
The executive team, the regional hub managers and five key account clients.
What inputs and evidence are required?
Client feedback, response-time data, margin per contract and the reasons clients left.
What decision stages will be used?
Diagnose, choose, test with two hubs, then roll out to all eight.
How will decisions and rationale be recorded?
One strategy log records each decision, the evidence behind it and who agreed it.
What this element is sayingThe strategy is shaped with the hub managers and key clients, moves from diagnosis to a two-hub test before full roll-out, and every decision is recorded with its evidence.
4 · WHATScope & definition
What business are we actually in?
Keeping corporate buildings and their technical systems running without disruption.
What value are we choosing to create?
Fast, consistent and effortless service that clients can rely on first.
What will we offer?
Integrated facilities and technical maintenance, with one client team for each key account.
What will we deliberately not offer or pursue?
Once-off construction projects and low-value contracts that absorb leadership attention.
What this element is sayingKestrel Point is in the business of keeping client buildings running, and it chooses to win on fast, consistent and effortless service through one client team per key account.
5 · WHYPurpose & rationale
Why does the organisation exist?
To let clients focus on their own business while their buildings simply work.
Why should stakeholders choose or support us?
One call reaches a team that already knows the site and resolves the request.
Why are these strategic choices appropriate?
Responsiveness is what clients reward with renewals and new sites.
What unifying rationale makes the choices coherent?
Being the partner clients rely on first ties every choice together.
What this element is sayingKestrel Point exists so that clients can focus on their own business, and every choice serves one rationale: to be the partner clients rely on first.
6 · HOWMechanism & model
What operating mechanism makes the strategy work?
Integrated client teams supported by one cross-functional operating rhythm.
Which capabilities are essential?
Request triage, first-contact resolution, cross-functional decision-making and account management.
Which processes and structures must support it?
Weekly cross-functional execution reviews, decision rights at hub level and a single request system.
Which partnerships or ecosystem relationships are required?
Specialist subcontractors under shared service standards, and a technology partner for the request system.
What this element is sayingValue is delivered through integrated client teams, decision rights at hub level and one weekly cross-functional rhythm, supported by a single request system and partners held to shared standards.
7 · WHENTiming & sequencing
What must happen first, next and later?
First responsiveness, next cross-functional decision-making, then scalable service models.
What determines when we move, hold or exit?
Hubs move once their response times are stable; contracts that stay below margin are exited.
Which dependencies shape the sequence?
The request system must be live before hub-level decision rights can work.
What timing assumptions could change the strategy?
That key accounts renew on service quality over the three years of the strategy.
What this element is sayingResponsiveness comes first, cross-functional decision-making next and scalable service models last, with the request system in place before decision rights move to the hubs.
8 · CREATES VALUEOrigination layer
What new or improved value will come into existence?
Faster resolution, fewer repeat visits and proactive maintenance that prevents breakdowns.
Through which activities is that value created?
Redesigning service routines and building a proactive engagement model with key accounts.
What capabilities or assets enable creation?
Site knowledge held in client teams and the request system data.
How will we know that genuine value has been created?
Clients renew, expand to new sites and refer Kestrel Point to others.
What this element is sayingNew value comes from faster resolution and maintenance that prevents breakdowns, built on site knowledge held in client teams.
9 · DELIVERS VALUEDistribution & access layer
How will created value reach each intended recipient?
Through the client team for the site, reachable through one number and one portal.
Which channels and access mechanisms matter?
The service desk, the client portal and the on-site team.
What experience must recipients have?
Requests are acknowledged at once and resolved at first contact wherever possible.
What could prevent value from reaching them?
Slow internal handovers and decisions that wait for escalation.
What this element is sayingValue reaches clients through one client team, one number and one portal, and the main threat to delivery is slow handovers between functions.
10 · SUSTAINS VALUEDurability & compounding layer
What makes this value durable over time?
Relationships with key accounts and a track record of consistent service.
What must be continually invested in or renewed?
The skills of client teams, the request system and service standards.
What could erode the value?
Functional silos, excessive reporting cycles and contracts that run below margin.
How will relevance and viability be sustained?
Annual service reviews with each key account and reinvestment in scalable service models.
What this element is sayingValue lasts through key account relationships and consistent service, and it is protected by removing silos, cutting reporting that delays action and exiting contracts that run below margin.
11 · STAKEHOLDERSFull universe of value recipients
Who has a material stake in our actions?
Corporate clients, employees, subcontractors, the board and the communities around client sites.
What value does each stakeholder group receive or contribute?
Clients receive reliable buildings, employees clear ownership, subcontractors steady work, the board stable margins.
Where do stakeholder interests reinforce or conflict?
Faster service can conflict with margin when it requires extra visits.
Whose interests could be overlooked by this strategy?
Subcontractors, who carry the service standards on site without shaping them.
What this element is sayingThe strategy serves clients, employees, subcontractors and the board, and it must hold the tension between faster service and margin while bringing subcontractors into the service standards.
12 · NAVIGATINGActive, skilful movement
How will the organisation sense changes in its environment?
Monthly service data per hub and quarterly client reviews.
How will signals be interpreted and translated into decisions?
The weekly execution review turns signals into decisions with named owners.
Where can external forces be used to advantage?
Clients moving to fewer, larger service partners favour an integrated provider.
What enables deliberate course correction?
Decision rights at hub level and a quarterly review of the strategic choices.
What this element is sayingKestrel Point reads its environment through service data and client reviews, turns signals into owned decisions every week, and uses the client shift to fewer, larger partners to its advantage.
13 · THE FORCESWhat must be navigated
Which competitive forces materially shape the strategy?
Low-price competitors and clients who bring maintenance in-house.
Which macro and regulatory forces matter most?
Rising labour costs and stricter building safety regulation.
Which disruptive forces could alter the model?
Remote monitoring technology that lets competitors respond faster.
Which social or environmental forces create risk or opportunity?
Clients expect greener buildings and lower energy use.
Which internal forces (culture, behaviour patterns, institutional habits, legacy identity) work for or against the strategy?
Functional silos and a habit of escalating decisions upwards.
What this element is sayingPrice competition, labour costs, safety regulation, remote monitoring technology and greener buildings shape the strategy, and the strongest force against it is internal: silos and the habit of escalation.
14 · OPERATING ENVIRONMENTThe bounded context
What is the defined operating context for this strategy?
Facilities and technical services for mid-sized corporate clients in one regional market.
Which industry and market conditions shape it?
A mature market in which clients are consolidating suppliers.
Which regulatory, technological and social conditions matter?
Building safety regulation, remote monitoring technology and client expectations of greener buildings.
Where would this strategy stop making sense if the context changed?
If clients moved to the lowest price as their first choice, the strategy would need to change.
What this element is sayingThe strategy is set for a mature regional market of mid-sized corporate clients who are consolidating suppliers, and it holds as long as clients put service ahead of price.

The Architecture holds the depth. The Strategy Intent Statement gives the clarity. These are the four dimensions Kestrel Point brought forward from its Architecture:

Kestrel Point is in the business of keeping client buildings running, and it chooses to win on fast, consistent and effortless service through one client team per key account.

WHY — Purpose & Rationale

Kestrel Point exists so that clients can focus on their own business, and every choice serves one rationale: to be the partner clients rely on first.

How the statement was deduced

  • WHAT gave the ambition: fast, consistent and effortless service that clients rely on first.
  • WHY gave the reason it holds together: clients reward responsiveness with renewals and new sites.
  • HOW gave the mechanism: integrated client teams and one cross-functional operating rhythm.
  • WHEN gave the order: responsiveness first, scalable service models after, within 2027–2029.
By the end of 2029, Kestrel Point will be the service partner its corporate clients rely on first, delivering fast, consistent and effortless facilities and technical service through integrated client teams and one cross-functional operating rhythm, building responsiveness first and scalable service models after.

D1 · Customer Experience & Value

If the intent succeeds, what should customers and other value recipients see and experience?

What will customers or value recipients experience differently?
Clients reach a team that knows their site, and requests are acknowledged at once.
What value or result will they consistently receive?

Step 1 · Strategy Architecture

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Step 1 · Strategy Architecture

Step 1 is the foundation. The group establishes the thinking and logic underneath its company's strategic position before expressing that position concisely.

Remind participants that the elements are interconnected: the value the company intends to create must be supported by its HOW; its WHEN must reflect the capabilities that need to be built; its approach to sustaining value must respond to the forces in its operating environment.

Encourage groups to answer with actual strategic choices and to leave broad aspirations out. After the questions of an element, the group writes what the element is saying in one or two sentences and asks: "Is this what we mean?"

When all 14 are written, the group reads them as one strategic system: "What is this architecture collectively saying about our company's strategy?" The six questions in the lesson beside the boxes guide that reading: whether the choices reinforce one another, where tensions remain, whether the HOW supports what has been chosen, whether timing reflects capability and environment, whether value is created, delivered and sustained, and whether the architecture represents the position the group intends.

The 14 elements follow the Strategy2Results® definition of strategy. OUTPUT, the first concept in 1.1, is what the 14 elements produce: the Strategy Architecture. Click each element to see the guide and questions participants see beside the boxes.

1 · ITERATIVEContinuously refined▼

Guide shown to participants

Strategy is never finished in one pass. It is a hypothesis that loops: form → test against reality → revise → repeat. This rules out strategy as an annual ritual and rules in strategy as a living capability.

The four questions

  1. What core hypotheses does the strategy depend on?
  2. How will reality test those hypotheses?
  3. What evidence would trigger refinement?
  4. How and when will strategic choices be revisited?
2 · THINKING & LOGICThe cognitive engine▼

Guide shown to participants

Thinking = divergent, exploratory, hypothesis-generating. Logic = convergent, analytical, assumption-testing. Both are required in sequence.

The four questions

  1. What alternative possibilities were explored?
  2. What assumptions underpin the preferred direction?
  3. What must be true for this strategy to work?
  4. What evidence supports or challenges the logic?
3 · PROCESSStructured container▼

Guide shown to participants

Process turns ad hoc thinking into a repeatable, auditable, scalable method. It defines who is involved, what inputs are needed, what tools are used, and what decisions get made at each stage.

The four questions

  1. Who must participate in shaping the strategy?
  2. What inputs and evidence are required?
  3. What decision stages will be used?
  4. How will decisions and rationale be recorded?
4 · WHATScope & definition▼

Guide shown to participants

What value are we creating? What business are we actually in? What do we offer and deliberately not offer? This sets the boundary of the organisation's field of play.

The four questions

  1. What business are we actually in?
  2. What value are we choosing to create?
  3. What will we offer?
  4. What will we deliberately not offer or pursue?
5 · WHYPurpose & rationale▼

Guide shown to participants

Why does this organisation exist? Why would stakeholders choose us? The North Star that makes all other decisions coherent and gives the strategy its internal logic.

The four questions

  1. Why does the organisation exist?
  2. Why should stakeholders choose or support us?
  3. Why are these strategic choices appropriate?
  4. What unifying rationale makes the choices coherent?
6 · HOWMechanism & model▼

Guide shown to participants

How do we actually deliver the value? What capabilities, processes, structures and partnerships make it possible? This is where strategy meets the operating model.

The four questions

  1. What operating mechanism makes the strategy work?
  2. Which capabilities are essential?
  3. Which processes and structures must support it?
  4. Which partnerships or ecosystem relationships are required?
7 · WHENTiming & sequencing▼

Guide shown to participants

When do we move? When do we hold? When do we exit? Timing determines whether the right move wins or fails. Sequencing determines whether capability is built in the right order.

The four questions

  1. What must happen first, next and later?
  2. What determines when we move, hold or exit?
  3. Which dependencies shape the sequence?
  4. What timing assumptions could change the strategy?
8 · CREATES VALUEOrigination layer▼

Guide shown to participants

Value comes into existence through creation: product/service development, innovation, capability building, IP, brand equity — activities that generate new or improved worth.

The four questions

  1. What new or improved value will come into existence?
  2. Through which activities is that value created?
  3. What capabilities or assets enable creation?
  4. How will we know that genuine value has been created?
9 · DELIVERS VALUEDistribution & access layer▼

Guide shown to participants

Creating value means nothing if it does not reach intended recipients. Delivery is the full system of transmission: channels, distribution, customer experience, partnerships and pricing.

The four questions

  1. How will created value reach each intended recipient?
  2. Which channels and access mechanisms matter?
  3. What experience must recipients have?
  4. What could prevent value from reaching them?
10 · SUSTAINS VALUEDurability & compounding layer▼

Guide shown to participants

Sustaining requires investment in the conditions that make value possible: relationships, infrastructure, culture, financial health and continued relevance.

The four questions

  1. What makes this value durable over time?
  2. What must be continually invested in or renewed?
  3. What could erode the value?
  4. How will relevance and viability be sustained?
11 · STAKEHOLDERSFull universe of value recipients▼

Guide shown to participants

Broader than customers: everyone with a stake in the organisation's actions, including employees, investors, communities, regulators, partners and future generations.

The four questions

  1. Who has a material stake in our actions?
  2. What value does each stakeholder group receive or contribute?
  3. Where do stakeholder interests reinforce or conflict?
  4. Whose interests could be overlooked by this strategy?
12 · NAVIGATINGActive, skilful movement▼

Guide shown to participants

Navigation implies agency, skill and continuous course correction. Read the environment, interpret it and make deliberate moves through it — putting the forces to use.

The four questions

  1. How will the organisation sense changes in its environment?
  2. How will signals be interpreted and translated into decisions?
  3. Where can external forces be used to advantage?
  4. What enables deliberate course correction?
13 · THE FORCESWhat must be navigated▼

Guide shown to participants

Competitive, macro, disruptive, social and environmental, and internal forces shape strategic viability: rivals, substitutes, regulation, economics, technology, business models, ESG, demographic shifts, culture and legacy identity.

The five questions

  1. Which competitive forces materially shape the strategy?
  2. Which macro and regulatory forces matter most?
  3. Which disruptive forces could alter the model?
  4. Which social or environmental forces create risk or opportunity?
  5. Which internal forces (culture, behaviour patterns, institutional habits, legacy identity) work for or against the strategy?
14 · OPERATING ENVIRONMENTThe bounded context▼

Guide shown to participants

The full system within which the organisation exists: industry, economy, regulation, technology, social context and physical environment. Strategy only makes sense within a defined environment.

The four questions

  1. What is the defined operating context for this strategy?
  2. Which industry and market conditions shape it?
  3. Which regulatory, technological and social conditions matter?
  4. Where would this strategy stop making sense if the context changed?

Step 2 · Strategy Intent Statement

◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Step 2 · Strategy Intent Statement

The group crystallises its Architecture into one Strategy Intent Statement. The Architecture holds the depth. The Intent gives the clarity.

Protect the Intent from overload: participants may try to carry every important point from the Architecture into the statement. The Architecture already holds that detail. A strong Intent lets a leader understand the company's strategic position without a paragraph of explanation.

The group reads its statement against its Architecture: "Does this statement faithfully express the strategic ambition contained in our Architecture?" Where the statement exposes a contradiction, the group resolves it in the Architecture before finalising the Intent.

The four dimensions brought forward from the architecture:

WHAT

The value the organisation has chosen to create and its field of play.

WHY

The rationale for those choices and why they matter.

HOW

The operating model, capabilities and mechanisms through which the value will be delivered.

WHEN

The timing, sequencing and strategic moves through which the ambition will be pursued.

"Does this statement faithfully express the strategic ambition contained in our Architecture?"

Unit Summary
Unit 2 synthesis
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Unit Summary

Closing the unit: Read each of the five summary blocks aloud, or ask participants to read them in turn. The summary is deliberately concise — its role is to crystallise the arc of the unit.

Return to the opening question: Go back to the definitions participants wrote at the start of Awareness. Ask 2–3 participants — "Read your original definition of strategy. What would you change in it now — and why?" The difference between their opening answer and their closing answer is the measure of what Unit 2 produced.

Tangible outputs check: Before closing, confirm with the group that the following have been produced or are in progress:

  • A Strategy Architecture from each group
  • A Strategy Intent Statement from each group
  • Four SiP statements from each group, agreed by every member

Closing commitment: Ask each participant to complete this sentence privately — "Based on what Unit 2 revealed, the one thing I am committed to doing differently before our next session is…" Invite 2–3 to share. These become the accountability anchors for Unit 3.

Transition to Unit 3: Remind participants — "What we built today is the strategic foundation: the Architecture, the Intent and the Success in Practice. Unit 3 maps that Success in Practice through KISS and defines the OKRs that will measure our progress toward it."
