Section 1 · Awareness — What

Foundations of Performance Management

Establishing the conceptual ground: what performance management actually is, how it differs from measurement, and the functional architecture that makes it work.

Section learning outcomes
  • Distinguish the management of performance from its measurement.
  • Exercise consistent judgement on performance status and the leadership response it requires.
1.1 — Measurement vs Management
The core distinction

Performance management (PM) and performance measurement are distinct disciplines. This is one of the most important distinctions in strategy execution — and one most organisations get wrong.

Measurement answers: What happened? It is the data — results, dashboards, scores.
Management answers: What do we do about it? It is the leadership response that turns data into decisions, actions, and accountability.

Most organisations have sophisticated measurement systems and believe they have performance management systems. A measurement system without a management architecture produces reports. A management system converts those reports into movement.

The Correct Sequence

Step 1
Define Success
▼

Before any system is designed, be explicit about what success looks like. What are you trying to achieve? By when? At what level of quality or scale? Without this, measurement has no target and management has no compass.

Step 2
Design the Management System
▼

Design the system for managing toward success. What conversations will you have? How often? Who is accountable? What decisions will be made? The management architecture must exist before measurement is collected — otherwise data accumulates with no system to act on it.

Step 3
Design the Measurement Infrastructure
▼

Design the measurement tools that feed the management system. What data do you need? How frequently? In what format? Measurement should serve the management conversations already defined.

Step 4
Manage Using Data
▼

Execute the management system using measurement data as the evidence base for conversations, interventions, recognition, and course correction. Most organisations start here — collecting data before having a system to use it. This is the most common PM design failure.

1.2 — The FACES Framework
Five functions of PM

A complete performance management system serves five interconnected functions simultaneously.

F
Foster a Results Culture
▼

The PM system must reinforce a culture in which results matter, are visible, and are consistently celebrated or addressed. Culture is shaped by what gets measured, discussed, and rewarded. A PM system that avoids difficult conversations actively undermines results culture — regardless of how sophisticated the dashboard.

A
Align Individual Performance
▼

Every individual must see a clear line of sight between their daily work and the organisation’s strategic outcomes. Without this alignment, effort is misdirected. The PM system must decompose strategic outcomes into team and individual targets that are genuinely connected.

C
Create Measurement Transparency
▼

People perform at a higher level when they can see their own performance data and understand how it is calculated. Transparency ensures the logic of measurement is understood and trusted. Hidden formulas and opaque scoring destroy confidence in the PM system.

E
Embed Shared Accountability
▼

Accountability must be a shared expectation. Shared accountability means co-owning the results — participating in target-setting, understanding the evidence, and being part of the intervention process. Systems that locate accountability solely in the leader create dependency and fragility.

S
Support Continuous Development
▼

The PM system must identify what performance reveals about capability gaps, development needs, and growth trajectories. Performance data is a development signal as well as an accountability score. Leaders who use PM data only for ratings miss its most powerful application: designing development that improves future performance.

1.3 — The Three Sights of Progress
What gets measured

A well-designed PM system tracks performance across three distinct categories. Each sight provides a different angle on whether the organisation is progressing toward its strategic intent.

Sight 1
Strategy Category — OKRs
What it measures
Strategic OKRs directly connected to the organisation’s multi-year strategy. Asks: Are we making progress on what matters most for where we are going?
Typical weighting
30–50% at senior levels. Lower for frontline roles. Reviewed quarterly with executive visibility.
Sight 2
Operational Category — KPIs
What it measures
Departmental KPIs tracking the operational engine — efficiency, output, quality, and service delivery. Asks: Are operations performing at the standard required to sustain strategy?
Typical weighting
30–50% across most role levels. Highest for frontline and specialist roles. Reviewed monthly or fortnightly.
Sight 3
Behavioural & Values Alignment
What it measures
How individuals demonstrate the organisation’s values and leadership behaviours — assessed through 360° feedback and peer observation. How results are achieved matters as much as what results are achieved.
Typical weighting
20–40% across role levels. Higher for leadership and customer-facing roles. Assessed bi-annually via structured 360°.
1.4 — Leader’s Response
Ratings & leadership responses

A rating scale only has value if it is consistently understood and every rating triggers a defined leadership response.

A good performance management system reduces subjectivity in how people’s performance is evaluated. It gives you and your fellow managers a common language for discussing contribution, results, behaviour and development. The number is meant to create consistency. It helps you avoid vague judgements such as “doing well”, “not trying hard enough”, or “needs improvement” without evidence.

The rating scale only works when everyone understands what each number means. A rating of 3 should not mean “average” to one manager, “safe” to another manager, and “not good enough” to another. If the meaning is unclear, the system becomes subjective again, even though it looks objective.

By anchoring performance in clearly defined standards, the system achieves three critical outcomes:

  • Personal bias is significantly reduced: you can challenge and defend a rating against explicit criteria, which take the place of managerial opinion.
  • A shared language of performance is established across teams and functions: a “3” means the same thing in Finance as it does in Operations.
  • Evaluation becomes consistent, comparable, and defensible, enabling fair decisions on rewards, development, and advancement.

A rating should trigger a clear leadership response.

  • If performance is strong, your response may be recognition, stretch opportunities, succession consideration, or increased responsibility.
  • If performance is acceptable but not growing, your response may be coaching, clearer goals, or a development plan.
  • If performance is weak, your response may be closer supervision, targeted support, performance improvement actions, or consequences where needed.

The first question is: What rating did this person receive?
The more important leadership question is: What must we now do because of this rating?

The scale below defines each level and the leadership response it requires.

Rating 1 — Poor
Leadership response: Immediate performance improvement plan within 30 days
▼

Definition: Performance is significantly below the minimum standard. Output is insufficient, quality is unacceptable, or behaviour is actively damaging.

Leadership response: Immediate intervention. A formal performance improvement plan within 30 days. Weekly check-ins. Assess whether the gap is capability, motivation, or role-fit. A Rating 1 sustained beyond 60 days without visible movement is a role-fit decision.

Rating 2 — Needs Improvement
Leadership response: Structured development plan
▼

Definition: Performance is below the expected standard in one or more significant areas. The individual shows some capability but has consistent gaps in output, quality, or behaviour.

Leadership response: Structured development plan with specific targets and milestones. Fortnightly check-ins. Clear articulation of the standard required to achieve a Rating 3 by the next review cycle.

Rating 3 — Meets Expectations  SOLID GOLD
Leadership response: Recognition & retention conversation
▼

Definition: Performance fully meets the standard expected for this role, at this level, in this context. Output is consistent, quality is reliable, behaviour is aligned. This is exactly what the role requires.

The Solid Gold Reframe: Most PM systems implicitly treat a Rating 3 as mediocre — a design flaw that erodes morale and incentivises performative overreach. A Rating 3 is “Solid Gold”: a person who reliably delivers and represents the backbone of execution in any high-performing organisation.

Leadership response: Recognition of consistent contribution. Exploration of growth aspirations — without implying that remaining at a 3 is a failure. Many exceptional contributors are sustainably and appropriately at a 3.

Rating 4 — Exceeds Expectations
Leadership response: Stretch opportunities; retention investment
▼

Definition: Performance consistently goes beyond what the role requires. The individual proactively adds value, solves problems beyond their mandate, and elevates the team. A Rating 4 is a pattern.

Leadership response: Explicit recognition and visibility with senior leadership. Conversation about stretch opportunities, expanded scope, or accelerated development. Failure to invest in a sustained Rating 4 is a retention risk.

Rating 5 — Outstanding
Leadership response: Mandatory structural follow-through
▼

Definition: Performance is transformational within the context of the role. The individual exceeds their own targets and demonstrably elevates others and advances the organisation. A Rating 5 is rare and must be ratified.

Leadership response: A Rating 5 carries a leadership obligation — increased scope, a promotion discussion, a compensation review, or a high-impact project assignment. A Rating 5 acknowledged verbally with no structural follow-through is a trust-destroying event. The individual will note that outstanding performance produced no change — and will adjust their effort accordingly.

1.5 — The Impact of a Calibrated System
Three shifts

When your performance management system is functioning correctly, the performance review becomes a no-surprise event.

Because the criteria are transparent and the weighting is predetermined, your team members understand exactly where they stand at any given moment. They know what is being measured, how much it counts, and what each rating means, including what you will do in response.

This transparency produces three powerful shifts in the organisational dynamic:

Shift 1
Strategic Transparency
▼

Every employee sees exactly how their specific contribution fuels the enterprise’s broader strategic goals. The line of sight between individual effort and organisational outcome becomes visible and motivating.

Shift 2
Shift in Dynamics
▼

The relationship between you and your team evolves from one of top-down oversight to one of shared investment in a common outcome. The scorecard becomes a shared tool and stops being a judgement handed down from above.

Shift 3
Pathways to Excellence
▼

The rating scale provides a clear roadmap for growth, showing your team members exactly what is required to move from one level to the next. Ambition becomes actionable.

By anchoring ratings in clear descriptors and decision triggers, your organisation replaces subjective, instinct-based assessment with a rigorous, high-alignment framework that drives execution.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

PM as an Execution Bridge

Understanding the structural logic of how performance management connects strategy to individual action, and why most PM systems fail to make that connection.

Section learning outcomes
  • Define how performance management connects enterprise outcomes to individual accountability.
  • Evaluate whether current performance arrangements build compliance or commitment.
2.1 — PM as the Execution Bridge
Four bridge functions

Performance management is the intelligence layer that determines whether your organisation’s high-level objectives actually translate into observable progress, or whether they remain aspirational statements in a presentation deck that is reviewed at the beginning of the year and quietly forgotten by the middle of it.

Moving from strategy design to execution requires a bridge. Without this bridge, your strategic goals may be intellectually coherent but operationally invisible. They exist in language that you and your leadership colleagues understand, but that language has not been translated into the daily decisions, priorities, and habits of the people responsible for delivery.

The primary purpose of performance management is to build and sustain that bridge, ensuring that every ounce of individual effort contributes to your organisation’s strategic direction. Without it, organisations generate what might be called random acts of excellence: genuine effort, real capability, strong intentions, none of which reliably move the needle.

Performance management frameworks create a line of sight: a direct, visible connection between enterprise strategy and individual contribution. When this line of sight exists, performance management becomes a motivational system and stops being a compliance exercise. A member of your team can sit down on a Monday morning and understand precisely how their work connects to the organisation’s most critical outcomes.

A performance management system performs four critical bridging functions between strategy and execution. When any one is missing, the bridge develops a structural gap — and strategy leaks through it.

Bridge 1
Decomposing the Big Picture
▼

Strategy lives at enterprise level. PM translates it into progressively smaller, actionable units: enterprise outcomes → business unit contributions → team targets → individual responsibilities. Without decomposition, strategy remains a declaration and never becomes a task.

In practice: Breaking each Key Result into functional measurable delivery signals: the specific, daily contributions required from every team. Strategy descends from the enterprise level to the desk level.

Bridge 2
Defining the Logic of Success
▼

The PM system must make explicit what “success” looks like at each level — as a measurable, observable outcome with defined evidence. When the logic of success is ambiguous, individuals define it for themselves — and those self-definitions are rarely aligned.

In practice: Establishing exactly how performance is measured, where the data comes from, and which levers carry the most weight. This removes ambiguity and ensures that the rules of the game are agreed before the game is played.

Bridge 3
Calibrating the Rhythm
▼

Strategy operates on an annual or multi-year timeline. Individual performance operates daily. The PM system must establish a structured rhythm connecting these timelines. Without rhythm, PM becomes an isolated event.

In practice: Setting incremental targets so that you are never guessing about whether you are on track. The calculation logic is straightforward, and it tells your leadership team exactly what “on track” looks like every single month.

Progress per Period = (End Target − Baseline) ÷ Number of Periods
This establishes the expected incremental progress in each review period — creating a visible trajectory through the year.

Bridge 4
Structuring the Dialogue
▼

Data without conversation is noise. The PM system must structure the dialogue that turns performance data into decisions. Define: who speaks, how frequently, about what, and with what authority to decide. The quality of the PM conversation is the quality of the PM system.

In practice: Establishing a cadence for reviews so that performance updates become active leadership conversations: structured moments where your team asks what this data means and what to do next. A performance update is more than a data entry.

2.2 — Compliance vs Commitment Architecture
The design choice

Every PM system is built on one of two architectural orientations: compliance or commitment.

A PM system fails when it is built solely for compliance, using pressure and monitoring to force results. The Strategy2Results® approach is built on a fundamentally different architecture: shared commitment, where the scorecard is a product of alignment and shared ownership, with no directive issued from above.

Compliance

A compliance-driven PM depicts an environment where:

  • Leader holds accountability
  • Assessment and judgement
  • Consequence and control
Commitment

A commitment-driven PM depicts an environment where:

  • Team co-owns accountability
  • Inquiry and problem-solving
  • Root cause; co-designed recovery

The choice is rarely made explicitly — but it is always made. The six dimensions below reveal which architecture is currently operating in your organisation.

PurposeEnsure minimum standards are met and deviations correctedGenerate shared ownership of results and continuous improvement
OwnershipLeader holds accountability; team is subject to itTeam co-owns accountability; leader facilitates
ToneAssessment and judgement; hierarchicalInquiry and problem-solving; collaborative
UnderperformanceConsequence and controlUnderstanding root cause; co-designing recovery
MotivationAvoiding negative consequenceIntrinsic meaning; shared pride in progress
PM experienceAnxiety-producing; done to the individualEnergy-generating; done with the individual

2.3 — Leader vs Team: A Critical Distinction
Who is measured on what

One of the most frequently misunderstood aspects of performance management is the distinction between how a team is measured and how its leader is measured. Conflating the two creates misaligned incentives and obscures accountability.

The team is measured on the quality and completion of specific activities. The leader is measured on the cumulative outcome that those activities produce.

Dimension The Team Is Measured On The Leader Is Measured On
FocusActivities, outputs, and behaviours within the review periodCumulative outcome trajectory across the full performance cycle
Time horizonCurrent period: what was done, how it was doneEnd-of-cycle: are we on track to reach the target by year-end?
EvidenceDeliverable completion, quality scores, behavioural indicatorsCumulative progress calculation; gap to target; trend direction
Review frequencyMonthly / quarterlyQuarterly / bi-annually

The practical implication: A team can complete every planned activity on time (strong team performance) while the leader’s cumulative outcome trajectory is still off track (weak leadership performance). The leader is accountable for ensuring tasks are producing the outcomes the strategy requires.

Example: The strategic goal is to reduce complaint resolution time from 48 hours to 12 hours. The team is measured on their daily processing speed, quality of resolution, and adherence to handling protocols. The leader is measured on the steady, cumulative reduction in cycle time that demonstrates the strategy is gaining traction. If the team is active but the number is not moving, the leader must intervene.

3.1 — Unity in Diversity
10 executive PM contributions

At the enterprise level, the performance management system sits in the executive team. The C-suite is, collectively, the organisation’s measurement alignment system. When the executive team is not aligned on what they are measuring, why, and what they are doing about it, the enterprise PM system is structurally broken regardless of the tools in place.

Think of your executive team as The Alignment Brigade, more than heads of departments. Every leader carries deep professional instincts about what “progress” looks like. A CFO sees progress in the margins. A CTO sees it in system uptime and deployment velocity. A CHRO sees it in engagement scores and leadership capability. These instincts are valuable, but if they are not synchronised, they create measurement imbalances that fragment your organisation’s understanding of its own performance.

The Alignment Brigade concept recognises that each executive function carries a unique measurement responsibility — a “hot zone” of the enterprise that they are best positioned to track, interpret, and act upon. When these hot zones are integrated through a shared cadence and common language, the executive team becomes a precision measurement system. When they operate independently, they produce competing narratives of organisational performance.

The critical question for your executive team: “When we look at the same performance data, do we interpret it from the same perspective — or from the perspective of our function? And when the data tells us to act, do we act as a system or as a collection of functions?”

3.2 — The Three Execution Gaps
Where PM breaks down

Three structural gaps account for the majority of PM system failures at the enterprise level.

Gap 1
Competing Definitions of Progress
▼

Different functions use different definitions of what constitutes progress — finance says margin, operations says throughput, sales says pipeline. Without a unified definition of progress at the enterprise level, each function optimises its own metric at the expense of collective momentum.

Intervention: Agree on three to five metrics that, if achieved, confirm the strategy is working. Everything else becomes a supporting indicator.

Gap 2
Measurement Blind Spots
▼

The organisation is systematically not measuring something that matters. Common blind spots: cross-functional collaboration quality, leadership pipeline health, customer experience at key touchpoints. What is not measured is not managed — and eventually becomes a crisis.

Intervention: Map your measurement system against the Three Sights. Ask: “What would we need to see to know our strategy is working before the financial results confirm it?”

Gap 3
Incentive Misalignment
▼

The incentive structure rewards behaviour inconsistent with the strategic intent. People follow the incentives. When incentives and strategy diverge, strategy loses. This is a design failure.

Intervention: Audit the current incentive structure against strategic priorities. Redesign to reward the behaviours the strategy actually requires.

3.3 — The Continuous PM Shift
Industry context

41% of organisations have moved to real-time or near-real-time feedback systems, replacing annual review cycles with continuous check-in models. This shift reflects a fundamental change in how high-performing organisations understand the relationship between feedback and performance.

Trend 1
From Annual to Continuous
▼

The annual performance review is being supplemented or replaced by structured quarterly conversations, monthly check-ins, and continuous pulse feedback. Annual feedback is too infrequent to change behaviour and too delayed to be developmental.

Trend 2
From Rating-Centric to Development-Centric
▼

Leading organisations are shifting from the rating (a backward-looking judgement) to development planning (a forward-looking investment). The rating becomes an input to development. Associated with higher engagement, stronger retention, and more rapid capability improvement.

Trend 3
From Individual to Team Performance
▼

Individual performance ratings are being complemented by team performance assessments. Most outcomes require coordinated contribution. Organisations that exclusively measure individual performance create hidden disincentives for collaboration.

4.1 — The EXECUTION Framework
Nine design principles

The EXECUTION framework synthesises the nine design principles of a high-quality enterprise PM system. Each letter represents a principle that, when absent, creates a specific and predictable PM failure mode.

FACES and EXECUTION

FACES · the function test

FACES is the function test. It asks whether the PM system is serving its five essential purposes: building a results culture, aligning individual performance, making measurement transparent, embedding shared accountability, and supporting development. In simple terms, FACES answers: “What must this PM system achieve for people and performance?”

EXECUTION · the design quality test

EXECUTION is the design quality test. It goes deeper into the architecture of the system. It asks whether the PM system has the right design principles: strategy anchoring, line of sight, evidence, collective ownership, outcomes, transparent logic, incentive alignment, review rhythm and collective insight. In simple terms, EXECUTION answers: “How must this PM system be designed so that it actually produces those functions?”

FACES tells us what a PM system must deliver.
EXECUTION tells us what must be designed into the system for it to deliver.

The Nine Design Principles

E
ENTERPRISE — Strategy First
▼
Principle
The PM system must be anchored to enterprise strategy. Every metric should be traceable to a strategic outcome. The enterprise intent is the filter through which all PM design decisions are made.
Self-check: Can every metric in your PM system be traced back to a strategic priority?
X
eXECUTION — Line-of-Sight
▼
Principle
Every individual must see a clear line of sight from their daily work to enterprise strategy. Line-of-sight is the translation mechanism that makes strategy personal. Without it, individuals execute their role description and lose sight of the strategy.
Self-check: Can every person on your team draw a line between their daily work and the strategy?
E
EVIDENCE — Evidence-Based Measurement
▼
Principle
Performance assessments must be grounded in defined, agreed evidence. Evidence-based measurement protects the integrity of the PM system and builds trust. Without it, ratings are subjective and disputes are inevitable.
Self-check: Does your team know exactly what evidence is required to achieve each rating?
C
COLLECTIVE — Collective Ownership
▼
Principle
Accountability for results must be shared across the team and organisation. Collective ownership means everyone with a contribution to an outcome has a stake in its measurement and a responsibility for its achievement.
Self-check: When a result is missed, does accountability sit with one person or is it genuinely shared?
U
UNDERSTAND — Outcomes First
▼
Principle
The PM system must measure outcomes — the actual change produced. A team can be 100% active while producing 0% of the required outcome. Leaders must measure impact.
Self-check: If your team completed all planned activities this period, would outcomes automatically follow?
T
TRANSPARENT — Measurement Logic
▼
Principle
The logic by which performance is calculated, rated, and rewarded must be transparent to everyone subject to it. Hidden formulas and opaque scoring destroy confidence in the system and generate cynicism that undermines the entire PM architecture.
Self-check: Does your team fully understand how their performance score is calculated?
I
INCENTIVE — Incentive Alignment
▼
Principle
The incentive structure must reward the behaviours and outcomes the strategy requires. Misaligned incentives are one of the most reliable predictors of strategy failure. When what gets rewarded diverges from what the strategy needs, people follow the incentive — this is a design failure.
Self-check: What behaviours does your incentive system actually reward? Do they match your strategy?
O
ONGOING — Review Rhythm
▼
Principle
PM must be an ongoing rhythm. An annual review cycle produces information too infrequently to inform in-year course correction. The review rhythm must be consistent and embedded in the operational calendar so that PM is the way work is managed. Monthly reviews monitor operational signals. Quarterly reviews evaluate strategic progress. Annual reviews assess long-term performance and development.
Self-check: Is PM embedded in how your team works week to week, or does it only surface at review time?
N
NAVIGATE — Collective Insight
▼
Principle
The ultimate function of a PM system is navigation: using performance data, collectively interpreted, to make better decisions about where to go and how to get there. A PM system that collects data but does not generate collective insight is a reporting system dressed as management.
Self-check: In your last performance review, did you make a decision together that you would not have made individually?

  1. Read the guide in each card. It is the lesson from part 1.2.

F · Foster a Results Culture

From part 1.2The PM system must reinforce a culture in which results matter, are visible, and are consistently celebrated or addressed. Culture is shaped by what gets measured, discussed, and rewarded. A PM system that avoids difficult conversations actively undermines results culture — regardless of how sophisticated the dashboard.

A · Align Individual Performance

From part 1.2Every individual must see a clear line of sight between their daily work and the organisation’s strategic outcomes. Without this alignment, effort is misdirected. The PM system must decompose strategic outcomes into team and individual targets that are genuinely connected.

C · Create Measurement Transparency

From part 1.2People perform at a higher level when they can see their own performance data and understand how it is calculated. Transparency ensures the logic of measurement is understood and trusted. Hidden formulas and opaque scoring destroy confidence in the PM system.

E · Embed Shared Accountability

From part 1.2Accountability must be a shared expectation. Shared accountability means co-owning the results — participating in target-setting, understanding the evidence, and being part of the intervention process. Systems that locate accountability solely in the leader create dependency and fragility.

S · Support Continuous Development

From part 1.2The PM system must identify what performance reveals about capability gaps, development needs, and growth trajectories. Performance data is a development signal as well as an accountability score. Leaders who use PM data only for ratings miss its most powerful application: designing development that improves future performance.

E · ENTERPRISE — Strategy First“Can every metric in your PM system be traced back to a strategic priority?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleThe PM system must be anchored to enterprise strategy. Every metric should be traceable to a strategic outcome. The enterprise intent is the filter through which all PM design decisions are made.

X · eXECUTION — Line-of-Sight“Can every person on your team draw a line between their daily work and the strategy?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleEvery individual must see a clear line of sight from their daily work to enterprise strategy. Line-of-sight is the translation mechanism that makes strategy personal. Without it, individuals execute their role description and lose sight of the strategy.

E · EVIDENCE — Evidence-Based Measurement“Does your team know exactly what evidence is required to achieve each rating?”

From part 4.1 · PrinciplePerformance assessments must be grounded in defined, agreed evidence. Evidence-based measurement protects the integrity of the PM system and builds trust. Without it, ratings are subjective and disputes are inevitable.

C · COLLECTIVE — Collective Ownership“When a result is missed, does accountability sit with one person or is it genuinely shared?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleAccountability for results must be shared across the team and organisation. Collective ownership means everyone with a contribution to an outcome has a stake in its measurement and a responsibility for its achievement.

U · UNDERSTAND — Outcomes First“If your team completed all planned activities this period, would outcomes automatically follow?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleThe PM system must measure outcomes — the actual change produced. A team can be 100% active while producing 0% of the required outcome. Leaders must measure impact.

T · TRANSPARENT — Measurement Logic“Does your team fully understand how their performance score is calculated?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleThe logic by which performance is calculated, rated, and rewarded must be transparent to everyone subject to it. Hidden formulas and opaque scoring destroy confidence in the system and generate cynicism that undermines the entire PM architecture.

I · INCENTIVE — Incentive Alignment“What behaviours does your incentive system actually reward? Do they match your strategy?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleThe incentive structure must reward the behaviours and outcomes the strategy requires. Misaligned incentives are one of the most reliable predictors of strategy failure. When what gets rewarded diverges from what the strategy needs, people follow the incentive — this is a design failure.

The review rhythm must be consistent and embedded in the operational calendar so that PM is the way work is managed. Monthly reviews monitor operational signals. Quarterly reviews evaluate strategic progress. Annual reviews assess long-term performance and development.

N · NAVIGATE — Collective Insight“In your last performance review, did you make a decision together that you would not have made individually?”

From part 4.1 · PrincipleThe ultimate function of a PM system is navigation: using performance data, collectively interpreted, to make better decisions about where to go and how to get there. A PM system that collects data but does not generate collective insight is a reporting system dressed as management.
