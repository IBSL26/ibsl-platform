Unit 3 — Session Overview

Use this tab to prepare before each section. It contains the unit intent, key facilitation questions, and tone and watch points for the full unit.

◆ Unit Intent

Unit 3 converts Success in Practice into a measurable execution architecture. The KISS reflection examines current practice honestly before any target is set; OKRs then express the enterprise priorities that reflection reveals.

The five-step path is the core of the unit. It moves executives from functional objectives to enterprise priorities judged on strategic merit.

How the unit runs: Teach the whole unit from the deck first, including one learning round of the Strategy Airport game played with the room. Participants then go to the portal, in the session or after it. The matching exercise in Section 3 and the game in Section 5 are portfolio work: each participant completes them alone, and they become part of the participant's Learning Portfolio. Section 4 is group work for the Capstone.

◆ Key Facilitation Questions
  • “Which current practice in your organisation most advances the SiP, and which most constrains it?”
  • “Look at your draft Key Result. Does it measure a result or an activity?”
  • “Which OKR hot zone describes how you instinctively set objectives? What would an enterprise-level Key Result look like instead?”
  • “If the functional labels were removed from the proposed OKRs, which would the team still choose?”
◆ Tone & Watch Points

Watch for: a rush to OKRs before the KISS reflection is complete. The reflection is the bridge; hold the sequence.

Watch for: Key Results written as activities such as “launch” or “implement”. Apply the formula: verb of change + metric + from X to Y + by deadline.

Watch for: advocacy for functional OKRs during synthesis. Use the four-question alignment test so each proposal is judged on its merit.

Section 1 · Awareness — What

What is KISS Framing & OKR Definition?

Interrogative Anchor · Before defining Objectives and Key Results, the leadership team must examine current operating reality in relation to its Success in Practice.

Section learning outcomes
  • Distinguish the current practices that reinforce strategic progress from those that constrain it.
  • Translate the intent to measurable results.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1 · Awareness

Section intent: This section establishes the full conceptual architecture of Unit 3 — the KISS framework, the OKR anatomy, and the five-step path from KISS output to enterprise OKRs. Participants need to understand all three before they can do the work. Do not rush this section — the quality of the Integration and Application sections depends entirely on how well these foundations are laid.

Transition from Unit 2: Open by connecting to what was built in Unit 2. Ask — "In Unit 2, we built our Strategy Intent Statement and our Success in Practice. Today we answer the harder question: what must actually change in this organisation to make that future real?" This framing positions KISS as a practical act of honesty.

Facilitator Opening

Frame this unit as the bridge between the SiP the team has built and the execution reality they must now create. The KISS reflection is a direct examination of the organisation against the specific future they have described.

1.1 — Translating Success in Practice into Action
Foundation
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.1

Directing participants: Read the central quote aloud — "If the Success in Practice we described is the future organisation, what must change in the organisation today to make that future possible?" Ask participants to sit with it for a moment. Then ask: "What is the one thing that most needs to change in your area to close that gap?" Take 3–4 responses. Do not evaluate — just note them. They will resurface when the groups build their KISS map in Section 4.

Framing the purpose: Emphasise that KISS is a targeted filter applied against the specific future the team has described. Generality is the enemy of this exercise — push for the operating reality.

The Strategy Intent Statement and the four Success in Practice (SiP) statements provide a vivid and shared description of the future organisation: how customers experience the enterprise, how operations function, how people collaborate, and what value the organisation consistently creates.

The question now becomes: If the Success in Practice we described is the future organisation, what must change in the organisation today to make that future possible?

The next step is to move from this future description to a practical execution agenda. However, before defining Objectives and Key Results, the organisation must first examine its current operating reality in relation to the SiP.

1.2 — KISS: Keep · Improve · Start · Stop
4 Reflective Filters
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.2

Teaching the four filters: Take KEEP, IMPROVE, START, and STOP one at a time. For each, ask participants to give a live example from their own function — anchored to the SiP. This prevents the exercise from becoming abstract.

The specificity standard: Use the Facilitator Move below as the standard: "improve cross-functional handover time from 72 to 24 hours" is specific enough to act on. Reinforce this standard before the group work in Section 4.

STOP — the hardest filter: Give this extra time. Ask — "What is one thing your function is currently doing that actively contradicts the future we described? What would it take to stop it?" Silence after this question is productive. Let it run.

The Strategy2Results® process introduces a reflective step designed to answer this question. The SiP statements are passed through a simple but powerful filter called KISS — Keep, Improve, Start, Stop.

Each element asks leaders to examine the organisation in relation to the future state described in the SiP:

◆ KEEP — Protect what is already working▾

Identifies the practices, capabilities, and behaviours that are already moving the organisation toward the SiP and should therefore be protected and reinforced. These are strategic assets that must not be disrupted in the pursuit of change.

◆ IMPROVE — Strengthen what is partially working▾

Highlights areas where the organisation is already moving in the right direction but where performance, coordination, or capability must strengthen to support the SiP more fully. These are often the highest-leverage interventions.

◆ START — Introduce what does not yet exist▾

Identifies new practices, capabilities, or initiatives that do not yet exist but will be necessary for the organisation to operate in the way described in the SiP. These represent genuine strategic gaps.

◆ STOP — Eliminate what contradicts the SiP▾

Surfaces activities, routines, or commitments that consume energy and resources but do not contribute to the future organisation described in the SiP. Stopping is often harder than starting — and more strategically valuable.

Critical principle: Every KISS reflection must be anchored to the Success in Practice statements. Leaders evaluate the organisation against the future organisation they have collectively described.

Facilitator Move

When leaders generate KISS items, insist on specificity. "Improve cross-functional handover time from 72 hours to 24 hours" is a KISS reflection. Push for the operating reality.

1.3 — The KISS Reflection Table: Four SiP Domains
4 Domains · Click to expand each
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.3

Teaching the domains: Walk through each domain (Customer Experience & Value, Operational Capability & Execution Rhythm, People & Culture Dynamics, Enterprise Value Creation) and read the SiP anchor question aloud. For each, ask: "What does your organisation currently look like in this domain — and how far is that from the SiP you described?"

Purpose of this section: This is the preparation for the KISS map the groups build in Section 4. Participants need to understand all four domains and their anchor questions before they begin writing. Rushing here creates superficial responses in the group work.

Key facilitation point: Point out that KISS items must be domain-specific. A STOP item in the Customer Experience domain looks different from a STOP item in People & Culture. Keeping them separate prevents the vague cross-functional generalisations that make KISS useless.

The KISS reflection is structured across the four SiP domains. Each domain has an SiP anchor question to ground the reflection.

1.4 — OKR Anatomy: Objectives & Key Results
Structure & Formula
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.4

Teaching the OKR anatomy: Present Objectives and Key Results side by side. Ask participants: "Think of a goal your team set in the last 12 months. Was it an Objective, a Key Result, or a task disguised as one of these?"

The Key Result Construction Guide: Walk through all four elements with participants. Then give a live example — ask a participant to state a goal they are working toward, and workshop it through the formula in real time. One concrete example does more than ten theoretical ones.

The critical distinction to land: Land the Critical Distinction: outcomes versus activities. Ask: "Can you check it off a task list? If yes — it's a task." This single test is the most useful tool in the room. Reinforce it every time a task-disguised-as-KR appears in the session.

Objective Key Results (OKRs) translate the identified changes into measurable progress.

Objectives

Define strategic ambition. Must be: Qualitative · Ambitious · Action-Oriented · Inspirational · Memorable. If you cannot wear it on a T-shirt, it is not worth pursuing.

Key Results

Define measurable progress. Formula: Verb of Change + Metric + From X to Y + By Deadline. Example: Increase digital transaction completion rate from 40% to 75% of total transactions by Q4 2026.

Key Result Construction Guide — 4 Elements▾
Verb of Change

Indicates the direction of improvement. E.g.: Increase, Reduce, Achieve, Improve, Maintain.

Metric

A clear and measurable indicator. E.g.: Customer satisfaction score, cycle time, adoption rate.

From X to Y

Defines the baseline and target. E.g.: From 40% to 75%. Always establish a starting point.

Deadline

Specifies when the result must be achieved. E.g.: By Q4 2026, By end of FY.

Critical Distinction: OKRs define outcomes. If a statement can be checked off a task list — "launch new platform" or "run customer survey" — it is a task. Those are activities that support the Key Result. Key Results describe the impact those activities must produce.

1.5 — Five Steps: From KISS Output to Enterprise OKRs
5 Steps · Click each to explore
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 1.5

Teaching the five steps: Take the steps in order. Each step builds on the one before it and prepares the next. For each step, read the guiding question aloud and ask participants to apply it mentally to their own area.

Step 1 — Find Themes: Ask — "When you review the KISS reflections across all four domains, what is the pattern? What themes keep appearing?" Themes are the connective tissue between scattered KISS observations and purposeful OKRs.

Step 2 — Inspiring Objectives: Reference the "T-shirt test" — if the Objective cannot survive a hallway conversation, it is too complex. Ask participants to try stating an Objective in one sentence before writing it.

Step 3 — Define Key Results: Apply the four-part formula rigorously. The example in the content (shift response time from 12 to 4 hours by Q3) is a useful benchmark. Hold each KR to the same standard.

Step 4 — Alignment Test: Read the four questions aloud. They show whether an OKR is enterprise-level or functionally disguised.

Step 5 — Priority Matrix: Walk through the matrix structure and its nine positions. The debate about what constitutes high impact versus high effort is often more valuable than the final placements.

Closing Awareness: Before moving to Intelligence, summarise — "We now have the full toolkit: KISS as the honest diagnostic, OKR anatomy as the building structure, and the five-step path to connect them. The next two sections explain why this sequence matters and where it typically fails."

Guiding Question: What insights emerged from the SiP and KISS reflections?

Each leader reviews their KISS reflection. What are the 2–3 big shifts that will contribute to moving the whole organisation forward? The real question is: "If the organisation is going to reach the future it has described, what is the one thing my area can change that would make the biggest difference for everyone?"

Review the patterns that surfaced across the four SiP domains. Identify the strategic themes that repeatedly appeared in the KEEP, IMPROVE, START, and STOP reflections. These themes represent the most important shifts required to move the organisation toward the SiP.

Output produced: A small set of strategic themes that reflect the organisation's priority execution shifts.

Guiding Question: If we get this theme right, what will we be known for?

Each leader gives their themes a voice. Translate each theme into a bold, qualitative statement that expresses strategic ambition. Start with a verb and describe the desired transformation. An Objective should look like a transformation. The real question: "If the organisation actually pulls this off, what will customers and competitors say it is known for?"

  • Make it ambitious — stretch the team.
  • Keep it qualitative — describe the destination.
  • Keep it memorable — if you cannot say it in a hallway conversation, it is too complex.

Output produced: A clear Objective that expresses the strategic ambition.

Facilitator Move

After each Objective is proposed, ask the room: "Is this statement something we could announce publicly with pride?" If the answer is no — it lacks ambition or clarity. Objectives that describe activities ("Implement new CRM") are tasks disguised as goals.

Guiding Question: What measurable outcomes will prove the Objective is being achieved?

For every Objective, identify 2–3 measurable results that demonstrate progress toward it. Each Key Result must describe a meaningful shift in performance and must follow the Key Result formula: Verb + Metric + From X to Y + Deadline.

Example: Shift response time from 12 hours (frustrating) to 4 hours (exceptional) by Q3.

Quick check: If the team can simply "check it off" a list, it is a task. Key Results describe outcomes — the actual change in the world that proves the Objective is being achieved.

Output produced: Measurable Key Results that define progress toward the Objective.

Before sharing, every leader tests their OKR against four questions:

  • Does this truly get the organisation closer to the shared SiP vision?
  • Would fellow leaders see this as a win for the whole organisation — or just for one function?
  • Does it visibly improve culture, operations, or value creation?
  • Is there clear accountability for delivering this outcome?

The Prioritisation Matrix evaluates each initiative against two dimensions: Impact — the degree of strategic value delivered if achieved — and Effort — the time, resources, coordination, and organisational change required. This structured approach helps distinguish initiatives that accelerate strategic movement from those that consume resources without meaningful progress.

The leadership team must pay particular attention to initiatives in Strategic Catalysts, Accelerated Enablers, and Core Strategic Drivers — these represent the highest leverage execution signals.

Section 2 · Intelligence — Why

Why the KISS-to-OKR Sequence Matters

Ignition Point · Most OKR failures are reflection failures. The KISS-OKR transition provides the missing bridge between strategic intent and measurable execution.

Section learning outcomes
  • Recognise the benefits of mapping the terrain before setting goals.
  • Practise the translation of SiP into OKRs.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2 · Intelligence

Section intent: This section answers the question participants are quietly asking: why can't we just write the OKRs directly? The answer — because OKRs without KISS become disconnected targets, fragmented efforts, and activity traps — must land personally. This section should prompt candid recognition.

Framing before entering content: Ask — "Has your organisation ever set goals that felt disconnected from the reality of what was actually happening on the ground? Where Key Results became a to-do list that nobody believed in?" Use the responses to anchor why the sequence matters before content begins.

Facilitator Frame

Open this section by asking the leadership team: "Has your organisation ever set goals that felt disconnected from reality — or where Key Results became to-do lists?" Use their answers to anchor why this sequence matters before moving into content.

2.1 — The Danger of Moving Too Fast
3 Human Problems
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2.1

Directing participants: After presenting the three problems (Disconnected Targets, Fragmented Efforts, The Activity Trap), ask: "Which of these three describes your organisation's most recent goal-setting cycle most accurately?" Take a show of hands or quick responses. The near-universal recognition of at least one pattern is itself the lesson.

The closing line: Read it aloud — "The organisation starts measuring the finish line before it has mapped the terrain." Ask: "What does 'mapping the terrain' mean practically — and what does KISS give us that we don't currently have?"

The usual cause of OKR frustration: organisations try to measure progress before they have had an honest conversation about where they are actually standing.

When leadership moves straight from a big vision to hard targets, they unintentionally skip the reality check. This creates predictable human problems:

Disconnected Targets

People are asked to hit numbers without anyone acknowledging the hurdles that make those numbers nearly impossible.

Fragmented Efforts

Different functions row in different directions because they have not agreed on what success looks like in the trenches.

The Activity Trap

People default to measuring busyness because they are not sure how to prove they are achieving real outcomes.

Essentially, the organisation starts measuring the finish line before it has mapped the terrain.

2.2 — Turning Intent into Action: The Strategy2Results® Sequence
Worked Example
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 2.2

Teaching the three-stage sequence: Present Success in Practice, KISS and OKRs one at a time. For each, ask participants to briefly state what stage they are at in their current strategic cycle. This creates a live diagnostic of where the group's execution journey currently sits.

The worked example: Take the example in order: the SiP statement, the KISS table, then the five steps. Each step uses what the step before it produced. At Step 2 say — "Notice how the KISS item maps directly to the Objective. There is no gap. The Objective is the direct consequence of what the KISS reflection surfaced." After Step 5 ask — "At this level of specificity, could you trace every OKR back to a specific KISS observation? That is the standard we are working toward."

Steps 4 and 5 in the worked example: All four OKRs earn four of four on the alignment test and go forward to the Prioritisation Matrix. Ask — "Why is execution speed placed at low effort, while collaboration culture is placed at high effort?"

The Facilitator Insight: Use the question in the Facilitator Insight below — "At which point in this sequence does our organisation typically enter?" — as a group discussion prompt. The honest answer often unlocks the most candid conversation of the unit.

Closing Intelligence: Transition to Extrapolating by saying — "Now we know why the sequence matters. The next section maps where it most commonly breaks down — and which leadership role is most responsible for each break point."

Once these patterns are visible, strategy stops being a deck of slides and starts being a shared journey. The Strategy2Results® sequence makes the path forward clear and respectful of the people walking it:

Success in Practice

The dream of where the organisation wants to be. The vivid future state across the four SiP domains.

KISS

The honest look at the changes required to get there. The bridge between the future and the present reality.

OKRs

The manageable, measurable steps taken to prove the organisation is moving. The execution compass.

When this sequence is respected, OKRs become a way for the organisation to learn, breathe, and align. Strategy transforms from a statement of intent into a living system that actually helps people succeed.

Worked Example: One SiP Statement Through the Sequence

The example follows one simple SiP statement through the whole sequence: first through the KISS filters, then through the five steps of 1.5. Each step takes its input from the step before it. Participants see the same example in 2.2 of their own file, to refer to when they do the group work in Section 4.

Facilitator Insight

Ask the leadership team to pause and reflect: "At which point in this sequence does our organisation typically enter? Do we skip the KISS reality check and jump straight to targets?" The honest answer often unlocks the conversation about why previous goal-setting cycles felt frustrating.

Section 3 · Extrapolating — Where

Where do OKR Hot Zones Appear?

The Alignment Brigade · Where do OKR hot zones appear across leadership functions? Where does functional bias create enterprise fragility?

Section learning outcomes
  • Recognise how each leadership function’s emphasis shapes the priorities it proposes.
  • Distinguish functional contributions from the enterprise priorities they serve.
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 3 · Extrapolating

Section intent: This section does for OKR definition what Unit 2's Extrapolating section did for the SiP — it maps where the process predictably breaks down, and who is responsible for each break. The Hot Zone cards are the diagnostic instrument. The goal is to make the systemic pattern visible before it plays out in the room.

Framing before entering content: Ask — "Before we start writing OKRs together, it helps to know the natural biases each of us brings to the table. This section maps those patterns so the team can compensate for them collectively."

Facilitator Frame

This section surfaces the natural biases each leader brings to OKR definition. Use the hot zone cards to name these tendencies explicitly and reveal the systemic pattern. Ask: "Which of these descriptions resonates most with how you instinctively write Key Results?"

3.1 — Natural OKR Emphasis Across Leadership Functions
10 Roles · Portfolio Work
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 3.1

Teaching the natural emphasis: Read the four functional tendencies aloud (commercial, operational, people, financial). Ask each participant to privately identify which category their natural OKR emphasis falls into. Then ask 3–4 to share.

Key point to reinforce: Each perspective is valid — the problem arises when these perspectives remain unexamined and unchallenged. The purpose of this part is to make the invisible visible before OKR writing begins, so that the group can consciously compensate.

The four execution risks: Read each risk aloud and ask for a show of hands — "Have you personally experienced this in a previous goal-setting cycle?" The near-universal recognition creates the psychological readiness to work differently in the Integration section.

The hot zone cards: Teach the pattern with two or three roles: the dominant future realities, the natural OKR emphasis, the typical hot zone and the alignment question. Leave the other roles for the matching exercise, which participants complete in their own file after the teaching.

Group debrief: Ask — "If all ten of these hot zones operated simultaneously in our OKR work — what would our final OKR landscape look like? Which domains would be over-represented, and which would be left out?"

The alignment question: Tell participants — "Hold your alignment question in your mind throughout the group work in Section 4. It is your personal quality check on every OKR you propose."

The shift to name explicitly: Ask — "What is the difference between a functional interpretation of success and a collective definition of success? What does that difference look like in a room full of leaders with different mandates?" This is the conversation the Integration section is designed to produce.

Closing Extrapolating: Before moving to Integration, tell participants — "We now know what biases we each bring into the room. The Integration section is where we use that awareness to build something none of us could build alone."

Every leadership role tends to emphasise some of the four SiP domains more strongly than others — because each role carries responsibility for specific organisational outcomes, leaders instinctively prioritise the realities that sit closest to their domain.

This creates a natural imbalance in how OKRs are proposed and evaluated:

  • Commercial leaders emphasise Customer Experience & Value and Enterprise Value Creation
  • Operational leaders emphasise Operational Capability & Execution Rhythm and Enterprise Value Creation
  • People leaders emphasise People & Culture Dynamics and Operational Capability & Execution Rhythm
  • Financial leaders emphasise Enterprise Value Creation and Operational Capability & Execution Rhythm

Each perspective reflects an important dimension of strategy execution. When these perspectives remain unexamined, OKRs gradually drift into functional scorecards and the organisation accumulates functional OKRs where enterprise OKRs are needed. This produces four execution risks:

  • Competing definitions of success across functions
  • Fragmented measurement systems that cannot be integrated
  • Functional optimisation that stalls enterprise progress
  • Slow decision-making due to misaligned incentives

Each leadership role therefore has a typical hot zone: the point where its natural emphasis distorts the OKRs it proposes. Each role also has an alignment question that challenges it toward enterprise thinking.

When the leadership team names its natural biases explicitly, the conversation shifts from functional interpretation of success to collective definition of success.

Recognising these patterns allows the leadership team to integrate perspectives into a balanced execution system that reflects the full enterprise.

4.1 — Translating SiP to KISS
4 Domains · Group Work
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 4.1

Before the groups begin: Remind participants — "Every item you enter must be anchored to the SiP. For example: 'we should improve cross-functional handover time from 72 to 24 hours because the SiP describes seamless execution rhythm.'"

Individual before collective: Each member writes their own inputs for a domain first, then the group shares and agrees its entries. This prevents the first speaker from dominating the KISS landscape.

Selection: Aim for 3–4 items per KISS element per domain. More than 5 items per element means the group has not prioritised — push for selection.

After all four domains are captured: Ask the group — "Look across all four domains. What are the 2–3 patterns that keep appearing? What does the organisation most consistently need to START, STOP, or IMPROVE relative to the SiP?" These patterns become the strategic themes of 4.2.

4.2 — Translating KISS to OKRs: The Five Steps
5 Steps · Group Work
◆ FACILITATOR GUIDANCE — Section 4.2

Step 1 — Find Themes: Each leader identifies their 2–3 themes in writing before sharing. Written submissions prevent the anchoring bias where the first speaker dominates the room. Collect all themes before any discussion begins.

Step 2 — Inspiring Objectives: After themes are collected, ask participants to draft their Objective privately. The "T-shirt test" applies — if they cannot say it in a hallway conversation, send them back to simplify.

Step 3 — Define Key Results: Apply the four-part formula rigorously. The example in 1.5 (shift response time from 12 to 4 hours by Q3) is a useful benchmark. Hold each KR to the same standard.

Step 4 — Alignment Test: Run the four-question test publicly. Ask each leader to read their OKR and answer the four questions aloud. The room will quickly surface whether the OKR is enterprise-level or functionally disguised.

Step 5 — Priority Matrix: Walk through the matrix structure before the group places any items. Establish shared definitions first — "What does 'high impact' mean for this organisation specifically? What does 'high effort' mean in our context — time, money, change management, or all three?" The debate about what constitutes high impact versus high effort is often more valuable than the final placements.

📋 PARTICIPANT ACTIVITY

Instructions for 4.2. In 4.2 of the participant file the group works through the five steps in order. Each step shows what the steps before it produced, so the group always sees what it is working on:

  1. Step 1 — Find Themes: the group reads its confirmed KISS map and records up to four themes.
  2. Step 2 — Inspiring Objectives: each theme from Step 1 is shown. The group writes one Objective under each theme.
  3. Step 3 — Define Key Results: each theme is shown with its Objective. The group writes up to two Key Results under each Objective and names the contributing roles for each Key Result.
  4. Step 4 — Alignment Test: each OKR is shown in full: the theme, the Objective and its Key Results. The group ticks each of the four questions it answers with a clear yes. An OKR with all four ticks is aligned and goes forward.
  5. Step 5 — Priority Matrix: each aligned OKR is shown in full. The group sets its Impact and its Effort, and the priority takes its position on the matrix.

The five-step record: above the steps the group's record builds from Step 1, theme by theme: the theme, its Objective, its Key Results with the contributing roles, the alignment test and the matrix position. It is saved on the page. When the five steps are complete the group reads the record, confirms its Enterprise Priorities and its Enterprise OKRs, and prints the record.

The group agrees each entry and one member acts as scribe; every member types the agreed entries into their own page. Both confirmed outputs feed the team's Capstone Blueprint.

With the KISS map confirmed, each group converts it into enterprise OKRs through the five steps of 1.5:

  1. Find Themes: up to four themes drawn from the confirmed KISS map.
  2. Inspiring Objectives: one Objective for each theme.
  3. Define Key Results: up to two Key Results for each Objective, each in the form Verb + Metric + From X to Y + Deadline, with the contributing roles.
  4. Alignment Test: the four questions, answered yes for each OKR.
  5. Priority Matrix: each aligned priority placed by Impact and Effort in one of the nine positions.
Facilitator Move

As the team debates placements, listen for disagreements about what constitutes "high impact" versus "high effort." These disagreements are where collective intelligence is built. Do not rush to consensus — the quality of the debate is as valuable as the final placement.

How the game is used: In the lesson you play one learning round with the room, from this page. After the teaching, each participant plays the same game alone in 5.1 of their own file and submits it with the unit.

Gate 1 · Baggage Check: Read the SiP statement aloud. Take KEEP, IMPROVE, START and STOP in turn. Let participants choose an option first, then select it: the answer is revealed only after selection. Closing the unit: Read each of the five summary blocks aloud, or ask participants to read them in turn. The summary is intentionally concise — it crystallises the arc of the unit without adding new content.

Return to the opening question: Go back to the question you asked at the start of Awareness — "If the Success in Practice we described is the future organisation, what must change in the organisation today to make that future possible?" Ask 2–3 participants: "Looking at the OKRs we have just built — have we answered that question? Where are the remaining gaps?"

Tangible outputs check: Confirm with the group that the following have been produced or are in progress:

  • The matching exercise from each participant, as portfolio work (3.1)
  • A confirmed KISS map from each group (4.1)
  • Confirmed Enterprise Priorities and Enterprise OKRs from each group — maximum 4 Objectives, maximum 2 Key Results each (4.2)
  • A completed Strategy Airport learning round from each participant (5.1)

Closing commitment: Ask each participant to complete this sentence privately — "The one thing I am committing to before our next session to make these OKRs real is…" Invite 2–3 to share. These are the accountability anchors between now and Unit 4.

Transition to Unit 4: Close with — "We have built our Strategy Intent Statement, described our future state through SiP, and now defined how we will measure our progress through OKRs.
